The sound of the Tibetan

Before it was studied, it was chanted

Gyalse Tokme Zangpo wrote the 37 Practices in the classic shape of Tibetan teaching verse: four lines to a stanza, nine syllables to a line. It was made to be memorised and sounded, and it still is: monasteries chant it, Garchen Rinpoche’s students recite it, and sanghas in the West have recorded it in English. The Tibetan word for poetry, nyen-ngak, means “beautiful sound”.1

Say a line of it aloud and you feel how it moves: in pairs of syllables, like footsteps, with a group of three to finish. Nine syllables make about four steps. Then comes the thing everyone who has chanted the text remembers. Every one of the thirty-seven verses ends with the same five syllables.

རྒྱལ་སྲས་ལག་ལེན་ཡིན། gyal sé · lak len · yin
gyal séthe Victor’s children lak lenpractice · “hand” + “take” yinis

“…is what the heirs of the buddhas practise.”

Gyal sé, the Victor’s children, is the Tibetan’s name for bodhisattvas here: the heirs of the buddhas. Lak len, practice, is built from the words for “hand” and “take”: practice as something you take in hand. Thirty-seven times the verse ends on these words, like a bell struck at the end of each round. By the tenth verse you hear it coming. By the thirtieth you are saying it with the chanter.

The fourth line of every verse is built the same way: four syllables that name the practice, then the five of the refrain. The images and the reasons come first. The practice itself lands last, one breath before the bell.

The refrain is the whole text in small. The Tibetan title is Gyalse Laklen Sodünma, “the thirty-seven practices of the Victor’s children”, and Tokme Zangpo’s own title, Gyalse, is the refrain’s first word. The opening promise ends by announcing it (“I will explain the practice of the Victor’s children”), and the closing verses sound it again after the thirty-seventh. It is struck before the first verse and it rings on after the last.

Three good translations, three different sounds

What English loses

Here is how the three translations on this site end verse 1. Each is careful and each is right for study. But listen to where the refrain falls:

Garchen Institute

…listen, reflect, and meditate day and night without distraction. This is the way of a bodhisattva.

Adam Pearcey

The practice of all the bodhisattvas is to study, reflect and meditate, / Tirelessly, both day and night…

Ken McLeod

…Day and night, fully alert and present, / Study, reflect, and meditate — this is the practice of a bodhisattva.

Pearcey moves the refrain to the front, which reads naturally in English, but it means the bell rings before the verse begins. McLeod keeps it where the Tibetan has it, at the end of line four. The Garchen booklet, which Garchen Rinpoche arranged for daily recitation, gives it a sentence of its own, This is the way of a bodhisattva, and uses exactly those words in all thirty-seven verses. Our own lineage’s text already keeps the bell.

Other things fall away in any translation made for study:

  • The length of the words. Gyal sé is two syllables; bodhisattva is four. English, full of long words, cannot fit the nine-syllable line the way Tibetan translators once fitted Sanskrit into it.2
  • The order. Tibetan puts the verb last, so a verse unfolds as picture, reason, then practice. English wants the instruction first.
  • The pictures inside the words. The Victor’s children, the hand that takes: these live in the Tibetan and vanish into “bodhisattva” and “practice”.
  • The sound itself. The footsteps of the line, the bell of the refrain, the shared breath of a room chanting together.

None of this is a fault in the translations. A translation for study has to choose clarity, and the commentaries depend on it. Carrying the sound is a second job, and it needs its own layer.

We are not the first

How others have carried a song across

Every tradition whose scripture is poetry has faced this, and the solutions are strikingly consistent. The Hebrew Psalms have the longest history of it in English, so that is where we start.

From the Psalms
Psalm 136 · King James Bible, 1611 · Robert Alter, 2007

One refrain, twenty-six times, answered by everyone

Every verse of Psalm 136 ends with the same Hebrew refrain, ki le’olam ḥasdo. The King James Bible renders it identically every time: “for his mercy endureth for ever.” Alter believes it was sung back by the whole congregation:

“This repeated refrain clearly must have been an antiphonal response in the liturgy, perhaps by the whole community of worshippers.”Robert Alter, The Book of Psalms

For usChoose one refrain and never vary it. Set it apart on the page. Let a leader read the verse and everyone answer with the refrain.

Robert Lowth, 1753 · C. S. Lewis, 1958

Rhyming thoughts, not sounds

Lowth discovered that Hebrew poetry is built on parallelism: the second line answers the first, echoing it, opposing it or carrying it further. Parallelism is the part of a poem that survives translation, because it lives in the meaning, not in the sound.

“…one that does not disappear (as mere metre does) in translation.”C. S. Lewis on parallelism, Reflections on the Psalms

For usTokme Zangpo pairs things constantly: water and fire, self and others, day and night. Keep every pair inside its line.

Joseph Gelineau & the Grail Psalms, 1963

Count the beats, not the syllables

Gelineau wanted congregations to sing the psalms in English with the pulse of the Hebrew. His translators gave each line a fixed number of stressed syllables and let the unstressed ones vary. The stresses were printed with accents so anyone could find the beat, and each psalm was framed by a short sung refrain.

“The words must never be allowed to fall into set musical rhythmical patterns which are opposed to the natural relative lengths of their syllables.”Joseph Gelineau

For usThis is the closest model we have. Give each line about four beats, mark them, and let the English syllables fall where English wants them.

Buber & Rosenzweig, 1925 · Everett Fox, 1995

A line is a breath; a word is a melody

Fox prints the Bible in lines based on spoken phrasing, each one, as Buber put it, “simultaneously a unit of breathing and of meaning.” He also keeps a key word the same each time it recurs, so the echo can be heard, the way a theme returns in music.

“…cola divisions do facilitate reading aloud and make it possible for the listener to sense the text’s inner rhythm.”Everett Fox, The Five Books of Moses, preface

For usOne breath per line. The same English for the same Tibetan key word across all thirty-seven verses, loosened where it strains, as Fox himself did.

Robert Alter, 2004

Don’t explain the poem inside the poem

Alter’s complaint about modern Bibles is that they smooth, clarify and lengthen until the compact, strange original is gone. He calls this “the heresy of explanation”:

“…the use of translation as a vehicle for explaining the Bible instead of representing it in another language.”Robert Alter, The Five Books of Moses, introduction

For usThe commentaries do the explaining. The sounded version leaves the dewdrop a dewdrop.

Thomas Cranmer, 1544 · the Coverdale Psalter

For every syllable a note

When Cranmer made the first English litany, he asked for music “not full of notes, but, as near as may be, for every syllable a note.” Coverdale’s prose Psalms have been sung for nearly five centuries on simple chant tones, each verse marked at its midpoint so the voice knows where to turn.

For usEnglish needs no strict metre to be chanted. Plain lines on one note, with a marked turn, carry a room of voices.

From other songs
Arthur Waley, 1918

One stress for every syllable of the original

Translating Chinese poems whose lines are counted in syllables, Waley found an English rhythm by counting beats instead:

“Each character in the Chinese is represented by a stress in the English; but between the stresses unstressed syllables are of course interposed.”Arthur Waley, A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems

He also refused to add or remove a single image: “considering imagery to be the soul of poetry.”

For usA beat for each Tibetan pair gives four or five to a line. Every image of the Tibetan stays; none is added.

Jerome Rothenberg, 1970

Total translation: the sound is part of the meaning

Earlier translators of Navajo horse songs kept the words and threw away the sung syllables that carry no dictionary meaning. Rothenberg translated those too, and sang the results, treating the song, not the words, as the thing to be carried.

For usKeep the Tibetan audible. Print it, give its sound, and let groups chant it.

Seamus Heaney, Beowulf, 1999

Find the voice first

Heaney found his Beowulf in the speech of his “big-voiced Scullion” relatives, and rendered the poem’s first word, Hwæt, as a plain “So.”

For usTokme Zangpo was a hermit monk teaching students plainly. Every line is tested aloud in that voice: direct, humble, unornamented.

From Tibet itself
Garchen Institute · our own lineage

Our lineage already sings this text in English

Besides the recitation booklet quoted across this site, the Garchen Institute publishes an English verse translation, What Bodhisattvas Do, in rhymed four-line stanzas made to be sung. Its first three-year retreat group recorded it in an “English musical version” and a “simple melody version”. It too keeps one refrain for every verse: “…is what bodhisattvas do.”

For usThis page does not replace that version. It tries a different road to the same place: unrhymed, closer to the Tibetan’s order and beat, and built to be read alongside the Tibetan.

Tokme Zangpo, 14th century

The text itself keeps a line in the original

The 37 Practices opens with Namo Lokeśvarāya, a Sanskrit homage left untranslated inside the Tibetan. Christian liturgy does the same with the Hebrew Amen and Hallelujah, and the Latin Mass kept the Greek Kyrie eleison. Holding one line in the original language is old and honourable.

For usThe refrain can be sung in Tibetan, gyal sé lak len yin, even when the verse is read in English.

FPMT prayer practice

Tibetan for the melody, English for the meaning

The Foundation for the Preservation of the Mahayana Tradition mixes the two languages on purpose:

“Verses chanted in Tibetan are emphasized for receiving the blessing of the tunes… Verses or sections recited in English are recommended… due to the importance of understanding the meaning.”FPMT, Lama Chöpa Audio Guide

For usA living Tibetan community already uses the hybrid we propose.

Alexander Berzin, 2006

The Tibetans changed the metre too

When Tibetans translated Indian verse they recast it into their own seven- and nine-syllable lines, making no attempt to keep the Sanskrit metre. Berzin draws the conclusion:

“Tibetans changed the meter, and the Chinese changed the meter, so we can change the meter as well. The point is to make it beautiful and harmonious.”Alexander Berzin, Study Buddhism

He also warns against the opposite failure, ritual texts set so they sound “like some hippie singing a folk song.”

For usEnglish should not imitate nine syllables. It needs its own rhythm, and dignity rather than jingle.

Chögyam Trungpa & the Nālandā Translation Committee, 1970s

Translate literally, then chant it together until it works

Trungpa’s translators decided early that Western students would chant in English. They aimed to be “as literal and accurate as possible,” then spent hours at their seminaries “experimenting with different styles of chanting, drum patterns, gong ringing” until the English could be sung.

For usNothing here is finished until a group has chanted it.

Khenpo Tsültrim Gyamtso Rinpoche, 2007

Singing the instructions is the tradition

One of the seven teachers on this site had his translators set the songs of Milarepa and his own songs in plain English so they could be sung. One of his shortest songs, in Ari Goldfield’s translation, is the whole argument of this page:

“Singing the key instructions / Isn’t meaningless, / It’s the lineage tradition.”Khenpo Tsültrim Gyamtso, “Why We Sing”
Two warnings
The Bay Psalm Book, 1640

Forcing the song into a familiar tune

The first book printed in British North America squeezed the psalms into English hymn metres so they could be sung to tunes people already knew. Its editors admitted they “attended conscience rather than elegance, fidelity rather than poetry.” None of its versions are still in use.

For usRhyme and a familiar metre make a verse easy to sing, and they cost something in exchange. The version on this page makes the other trade: no rhyme scheme, and a rhythm that comes from the Tibetan’s own beat.

Coleman Barks’s Rumi, 1990s to now

Wonder cut loose from its source

Barks made Rumi one of the most widely read poets in America, working without reading Persian: his versions were reworked from earlier English translations. Critics have shown how much of Rumi’s Islam disappeared on the way.3 The result is lovely, and much of it is no longer Rumi.

For usEvery choice must be traceable to Tokme Zangpo’s Tibetan. Refuge, bodhicitta and emptiness stay in; nothing is universalised away.

What we take from all of this

Eight rules for sounding the verses in English

  1. The refrain comes last, in the same words, every time.We use the Garchen booklet’s own refrain, This is the way of a bodhisattva, so it matches what our lineage already recites. Psalm 136 · King James Bible · Garchen Institute
  2. The refrain can also be sung in Tibetan.Groups can answer every verse with gyal sé lak len yin, its original five syllables. Namo Lokeśvarāya · FPMT · Amen, Hallelujah, Kyrie
  3. The practice lands just before the refrain.As in the Tibetan fourth line: pictures and reasons first, then the practice, then the bell. The Tibetan · McLeod
  4. Four lines, one breath each.Never run on as prose. Buber & Rosenzweig · Fox
  5. About four beats a line, marked.Stresses, not syllables, carry the rhythm; the beats are marked so anyone can find them. Waley · Gelineau · Alter · Berzin
  6. Every image whole, none added, none explained.The ship, the ocean, the dewdrop, the rainbow, the dream all stay. Waley · Alter
  7. Pairs stay paired; key words stay the same.What Tokme sets side by side stays side by side, and a key Tibetan word keeps one English word throughout. Lowth · Buber & Rosenzweig · Fox
  8. A plain voice, tested aloud, with every choice shown.Each verse says what we kept and what we let go, so no one mistakes this for the source. Heaney · Nālandā Translation Committee · the warning of Barks

Nine verses from across the text, from the great ship to the dedication. The great ship

Sounded in English
Freedom and fortune, the great ship, hard to find, now found: to free myself and others from samsara’s sea, by day and by night, never idle, listen, reflect, and meditate. This is the way of a bodhisattva.gyal sé lak len yin རྒྱལ་སྲས་ལག་ལེན་ཡིན།
At its heart

A precious human life, with its freedoms and fortunes, is pictured as a great ship on the ocean of samsara: big enough to carry others, not a raft for one. Shantideva uses the same picture. A ship is built for crossing, and the verse lives in the gap between now found and the open sea still ahead.

Kept & let go

Kept: the Tibetan order. The verse opens on the gift (freedom, fortune, the ship) before it names the task. “Hard to find, now found” keeps the Tibetan’s two verbs side by side, nyé ka and tob.

Let go: the count. Tradition numbers the freedoms and fortunes at eight and ten; the chant leaves the counting to the commentaries. And a rhyme: the Tibetan’s first two lines end alike, tob dü dir / dral jé chir. The English does not try.

Water, fire, darkness

Sounded in English
For my kin, desire surges like water; for my foes, hatred blazes like fire; pitch-dark delusion forgets what to take up, what to put down: leave your homeland. This is the way of a bodhisattva.gyal sé lak len yin རྒྱལ་སྲས་ལག་ལེན་ཡིན།
At its heart

Three poisons, three elements. Desire moves like water and will not stay still; hatred blazes like fire; delusion is pitch-dark. The home you know best is where all three are fed every day.

Kept & let go

Kept: the first two lines are built identically in Tibetan, nyen gyi chok la / dra yi chok la, so the English repeats its shape exactly. And “take up”: lang is a form of the same verb as len in the refrain. Delusion forgets what to take up; the refrain names what to take in hand.

Let go: pa yul is literally “father-land”. In English that word carries other history, so we say “homeland”, as all three translations do.

Sounded in English
Friends of many years will part, each alone; the wealth we worked for will be left behind; consciousness, the guest, will leave the body’s guesthouse: let go of this life. This is the way of a bodhisattva.gyal sé lak len yin རྒྱལ་སྲས་ལག་ལེན་ཡིན།
At its heart

The body is a guesthouse and consciousness is the guest. You stay a while, and in the morning you go. Nothing in the verse is harsh: friends part, the savings stay behind, the guest leaves, the way it always happens at an inn.

Kept & let go

Kept: the echo of guest and guesthouse, drön khang and drön po, which Tokme sets in one line.

Let go: a pun. Line two ends with ལུས lü, “left behind”; line three begins with the same written word, ལུས lü, “body”. What is left behind, and the body, sound the same. English cannot keep it.

A dewdrop on the tip of a blade of grass holds the whole morning sky, and it is gone as soon as the sun is up. Tokme does not call happiness false. He shows you something beautiful and lets you watch it go.

Kept & let go

Kept: the dewdrop, untouched: no “fleeting pleasures” to explain it. Line two ends on yin, “is”, like the refrain: what the world’s joy is, and what the practice is. The English puts “is” at the heart of both.

Let go: go pang chok, literally “supreme rank”, becomes the plainer “highest state”.

They have loved me since time without beginning: if my mothers suffer, what good is my own joy? So, to free beings without limit, give rise to bodhicitta. This is the way of a bodhisattva.gyal sé lak len yin རྒྱལ་སྲས་ལག་ལེན་ཡིན།
At its heart

Over lives without beginning, every being has been our mother and loved us. The verse runs from time without beginning (tok mé) to beings without limit (ta yé): no first moment, no outer edge. To a Tibetan ear, tok mé, “beginningless”, also sounds exactly like the author’s own name, Tokme, spelled with one letter more.

Kept & let go

Kept: the delay. The Tibetan speaks of those who have loved us before it names them; “mothers” arrives only in line two. And both withouts, so the echo can be heard. “To free” is the same phrase as in verse 1, dral jé chir.

Let go: chi zhik ja, “what is to be done with it?”, becomes “what good is it?”

Sounded in English
One I cherished and raised like my own child may look at me like an enemy: like a mother with her child struck by illness, love them all the more. This is the way of a bodhisattva.gyal sé lak len yin རྒྱལ་སྲས་ལག་ལེན་ཡིན།
At its heart

A mother whose child is ill does not love less when the child is difficult. She loves more, because the child is ill. The verse turns on three likenesses, each built on one small word, zhin, “like”: like a child, like an enemy, like a mother.

Kept & let go

Kept: all three “likes”, in order. The love asked for here, tsé wa, is the same word verse 10 uses for our mothers’ love for us. The love we were given is the love we are asked to give.

Let go: the Tibetan’s “even if” becomes a colon. English parallel lines can carry the turn without it.

Verse 23 · Ultimate bodhicitta

The summer rainbow

Hear it · word by word

Sounded in English
When you meet with what pleases the mind, like a rainbow in the summertime, beautiful as it appears, do not see it as real: give up clinging. This is the way of a bodhisattva.gyal sé lak len yin རྒྱལ་སྲས་ལག་ལེན་ཡིན།
At its heart

A summer rainbow is the most vivid thing in the sky, and there is nothing there to hold. The verse does not say the rainbow is ugly or unreal. It says: it appears, beautifully. Enjoy the appearing, and do not reach for it.

Kept & let go

Kept: the verbs. Verses 23 and 24 are a pair, built from the same words. In both you meet something (tré): here the pleasant, there the painful. In both you see (ta): here not as real, there as delusion. The English uses “meet” and “see” in both, so the pair can be heard.

Let go: yi du ong, literally “coming to the mind”, becomes “pleases the mind”.

Verse 24 · Ultimate bodhicitta

A dream of a child’s death

Hear it · word by word

Sounded in English
All suffering is like a child’s death in a dream: grasping what delusion shows as real wears us out. So, when you meet with what goes against you, see it as delusion. This is the way of a bodhisattva.gyal sé lak len yin རྒྱལ་སྲས་ལག་ལེན་ཡིན།
At its heart

In the dream the grief is complete. You wake, and there was never a child. The verse does not tell you the pain is nothing. It says that holding what delusion shows as real wears you out: a tang ché, your strength is cut.

Kept & let go

Kept: the plain “wears us out”. Grief in the Tibetan is not dressed up. “Meet” and “see” match verse 23.

Let go: na tsok, “of every kind”, becomes simply “all”. And trul nang, “deluded appearance”, becomes “what delusion shows”, which is shorter to sing.

Dedication

All the virtue won by striving like this, to clear the suffering of beings without limit, with wisdom pure of the three spheres, dedicate to awakening. This is the way of a bodhisattva.gyal sé lak len yin རྒྱལ་སྲས་ལག་ལེན་ཡིན།
At its heart

The last verse gives everything away. Whatever good the thirty-six practices have made is turned toward awakening, for beings without limit, the phrase verse 10 used for our mothers. The wisdom that does it is pure of the three spheres: no one giving, nothing given, no one receiving held as real.

Kept & let go

Kept: ta yé, “without limit”, the same in verse 10 and here, so the end answers the middle. In Tibetan the end also answers the beginning: line two closes sal jé chir, the cadence of verse 1’s dral jé chir, so the first and last verses turn on the same sound.

Let go: the three spheres go unexplained in the chant. That is the commentaries’ work.

Words that stay the same

When a Tibetan word comes back, its English word comes back with it, so the echo can be heard from verse to verse. This is the leading-word principle of Buber, Rosenzweig and Fox.

Using it together

Chanting in a study group

  1. Hear the Tibetan first. Play a recording, or have someone read one verse slowly from the sounds above. Listen for the footsteps and the bell, not for the meaning.
  2. Leader and group. The leader reads the four English lines. Everyone answers with the refrain, in English or, once the group knows it, in Tibetan. Gyal sé lak len yin takes about three verses to learn.
  3. One breath a line. Lean on the marked syllables and let the rest fall between them. A steady pulse is enough; no tune is needed.
  4. Leave a breath of silence after each refrain, before the next verse begins.
  5. Chant the session’s verses twice, once before the discussion and once after. Notice whether they sound different.
  6. Say what doesn’t work. These are drafts. A line that trips the tongue in a room full of people is a line to rewrite; that is how the Nālandā translators worked.

Listen first
