# The poetry workshop: study and practice across traditions

> **Status:** planned, not built. Waiting on: Morton's *How to Read Any Poem Anywhere* page (see [Open questions](#open-questions)). Working assumptions until answered: the Shakespeare centrepiece is Sonnet 29, and the Workshop will take the maker's own poems as well as versions of the songs.

## Why

The Workshop makes English poems from the caryās through Western lenses, and it reads every English on Morton's mixing board ([`research/how-to-read-a-song.md`](research/how-to-read-a-song.md), [`research/western-lenses.md`](research/western-lenses.md)). This plan widens it into a whole practice of reading and making poems: Morton read in depth, then three traditions studied by one method, each put to work in the engine and the Workshop.

- **Morton**, as the method of reading.
- **Rumi and the Sufi poets**, Arabic and Persian.
- **The Zen mountain poets**, Chinese and Japanese.
- **Shakespeare's sonnets**, centred on Sonnet 29 ("Like to the lark at break of day arising / From sullen earth, sings hymns at heaven's gate"), which Judi Dench has performed.

## The one idea: the same problems, solved four ways

Every tradition here solves the problems a caryā solves. The study is organised around them:

| problem | caryā | Sufi | Zen | Shakespeare |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The line that comes back** (Morton's absolute rhyme, the hottest pole) | the refrain, ধ্রু | the ghazal's *radīf*; *dhikr*, the repeated divine names | the parallel couplet; the linked verse's return | "state", "like", "heaven" struck again and changed |
| **The singer who signs the song** | the bhaṇitā, "Lūyī says" | the *takhalluṣ* in the last couplet (Rumi signs *Khāmūsh*, "Silent", or Shams) | Hanshan named for his mountain | the sonnet that says it will outlive its subject |
| **The answering poem** | (versions, here) | *naẓīra*: a reply in the same metre and rhyme | *hè shī*, *cì yùn*: a reply on a friend's rhyme words | imitation (Dryden's third kind) |
| **The double language** | *sandhyā-bhāṣā*, twilight language | wine, tavern and beloved read as the path | the capping phrase answering a koan | the conceit |

The last row pulls against Morton's rule, "no symbolism". The edition already settles it in its own way: the tradition's reading of an image lives in the notes, and the line keeps its strangeness. The study tests that rule against each tradition and says where it holds.

## The method: five parts for each tradition

Each tradition gets a research note in `research/`, cited and verified like the existing ones:

1. **The practice.** How the poets worked, not only what they wrote.
2. **The machinery.** How verse works in the language: metre, rhyme, line, syntax.
3. **Close readings.** Morton's board, channel by channel, on two or three touchstone poems, in the original first, then in English.
4. **What survives into English, and why.** Each case turns on named translators.
5. **What it gives the practice.** Lenses, exercises, and whatever the engine can measure.

## 1. Morton in depth

The engine uses the course's five steps, hot and cool, the solar system of rhyme and five exercises. Going deeper:

- **The whole course.** Every lecture of *How to Read a Poem* (UC Davis) and the *How to Read Any Poem Anywhere* guide, each tool listed, with what the engine already uses marked and what it does not.
- **The theory under it:**
  - Jakobson, "Linguistics and Poetics" (1960): the poetic function as equivalence and repetition, which is where "hot" comes from;
  - Morton, "An Object-Oriented Defense of Poetry" (*New Literary History*, 2012): the poem as a thing in its own right, which reading attunes to;
  - the "ambient poetics" of *Ecology without Nature* (2007): tone, timbre, the medium made audible, the Aeolian (sound made by wind or water, not a voice).
- **Morton and Buddhism.** What Morton has published on it, checked before any link to the caryās is drawn.

## 2. Rumi and the Sufi poets

Rumi wrote mainly in Persian, in a metre inherited from Arabic (*ʿarūḍ*, codified by al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad) and a language full of Qur'anic Arabic. So the study covers both.

- **Poets.** Arabic: Rābiʿa, al-Ḥallāj, Ibn al-Fāriḍ, Ibn ʿArabī. Persian: Sanāʾī, ʿAṭṭār, Rumi, Ḥāfiẓ.
- **Practice.** *Samāʿ* (the listening ceremony, with music and turning), *dhikr*, ghazals made in performance, the *Masnavi* teaching through stories, the *naẓīra* answering an earlier master.
- **Machinery.** *ʿArūḍ*, *qāfiya*, *radīf*, *takhalluṣ*; the forms (ghazal, *masnavī* couplets, *rubāʿī*, *qaṣīda*); Arabic's root-play and wordplay (*tajnīs*); allusion to the Qur'an.
- **Touchstones.** "Listen to the reed", the opening of the *Masnavi*; a ghazal of the *Dīvān-e Shams* with a *radīf*; Ibn al-Fāriḍ's *Wine Ode*.
- **In English.** FitzGerald's *Rubáiyát*; Nicholson and Arberry; Coleman Barks, and the argument that his versions erase Islam (Rozina Ali, *The New Yorker*, 2017); Agha Shahid Ali, who carried the *radīf* and the signature line into real English ghazals.

## 3. The Zen mountain poets

- **Poets.** Hanshan (Cold Mountain) and Shide, Wang Wei, Jia Dao, Ryōkan, Bashō.
- **Practice.** Hanshan's poems, said to be written on rocks and walls; capping phrases in koan training (Hori, *Zen Sand*, 2003); linked verse (*renga*); the cutting word (*kireji*); Jia Dao weighing "push" against "knock" for one word, the story that gave Chinese its word for revision, *tuīqiāo* 推敲; the haibun's prose and poem together, a shape the caryās with Munidatta's commentary share.
- **Machinery.** Five- and seven-character lines and their pause; tone patterns; the parallel middle couplets of regulated verse; the "poem eye" (*shīyǎn*), the one word that carries a line; grammar without a subject or tense.
- **Touchstones.** A Cold Mountain poem; Wang Wei's "Deer Park"; Bashō's old pond.
- **In English.** Pound's *Cathay*; Snyder's *Cold Mountain Poems* (1958); Watson, Red Pine, Hinton; Weinberger and Paz, *Nineteen Ways of Looking at Wang Wei* (1987), a model for comparing many translations of one poem. Why the force survives: image and parallelism carry over, sound does not, and grammar without a subject becomes English fragments.

## 4. Shakespeare, centred on Sonnet 29

- **Syntax.** One sentence, *When… Yet…*; the turn at "Yet" (line 9) works like an Italian sonnet's volta inside an English sonnet, before the couplet.
- **Words struck again.** "Like" goes from envy on repeat ("like him, like him") to the lark's flight ("Like to the lark"); "state" ends lines 2 and 10 and sits inside line 14; "deaf heaven" (line 3) becomes "heaven's gate" (line 12).
- **Metre and rhyme.** The trochaic lift of "*Haply* I think on thee"; the feminine rhymes despis*ing* / aris*ing*; the off rhyme possessed / least.
- **Other sonnets for contrast.** 18, 73 and 116.
- **Performance.** Find and cite the Judi Dench performance. Claude cannot watch or hear video, so the study reads the text for breath and phrasing, and the maker listens and marks where the voice lifts. That becomes an exercise: perform it yourself.

## 5. Synthesis, then building

- **A comparison note:** what works in poems everywhere, and what it changes in the edition's translation rules.
- **Lenses** in `prompts/lenses.json`, each with its board: Ghazal (*radīf* and signature), *Masnavī* couplets, *Rubāʿī* (aaba), Cold Mountain, Parallel couplet, Haiku cut, Shakespearean sonnet.
- **The English ear** (`lib/english.mjs`, `lib/reading.mjs`): find a *radīf*, measure parallelism between two half-lines, find the turn, measure how regular a pentameter runs.
- **The Workshop:**
  - a **Study** room: the touchstone poems read on the board, annotated;
  - a **Practice** room: daily exercises (the answering poem, one-word revision, the capping phrase, read aloud, Hotter and Cooler);
  - the existing drafting, with a mode for the maker's own poems as well as versions of the songs.

## Rules

- Every claim cites a scholar, with the same verification marks as the existing research notes.
- Long quotation only from originals and out-of-copyright translations (Shakespeare, Nicholson, Whinfield, FitzGerald, Pound's *Cathay*). Barks, Snyder, Hinton and other living translators are discussed and quoted briefly. The overlap guard still applies.
- Persian, Arabic and Chinese are read through scholarship and dictionaries, not natively; notes say so where it matters.
- Versions of the songs still keep every image, the refrain and the shape, and keep the tradition's readings out of their lines ([`prompts/tasks/version.md`](prompts/tasks/version.md)).

## Order of work

1. Research notes, in parallel: Morton, the Sufi poets, the Zen mountain poets, Shakespeare. Shown to the maker before anything is built.
2. The comparison note.
3. Lenses and the English ear, with tests.
4. The Workshop's Study and Practice rooms; rebuilt and republished to the same URL.
