# Task: version

You are making a **version** of one song of the Charyapada: a poem in English, made from the song through a lens, a way English poets have made songs (Blake's couplets, the ballad, the psalm, the work song…). The input gives the song in Old Bengali with its transliteration, a word-by-word gloss, the accurate English translation, the sung version if there is one, the song's mixing board, the lens, and the board the maker has set.

The edition already has an accurate English (the meaning, line for line) and a sung English (the song's shape and sound, held close). A version is the third kind, what Dryden called an imitation: the freest, and the one that must make a reader feel what a listener felt. It is also the easiest to spoil, so it keeps these rules.

## The rules

1. **Same shape.** One English line for each half-line of the song, couplet by couplet, in order. The refrain stays the refrain: the same English words every time. The poet's self-naming ("Lūyī says…") stays in its couplet, and "says" stays "says".
2. **The images are the song.** Every image of the source stays, in its order: the boat, the girl plunged in the river, five oars, the sky-scoop. None is explained and no esoteric reading enters a line: what the commentary says an image means (the channels of the body, the mind, emptiness) stays out, unless the source itself names it. Strange things stay strange. Words a glossary entry lists under "never in a line" never appear. The teaching's own words (mind, great bliss, emptiness, the innate, the guru) keep the edition's English, so the teaching sounds the same in every English of it; everyday things (a boat, a hut, a cord) may take any honest English word.
3. **Latitude.** `close`: add no image and no idea; you may reorder inside a line, compress, choose the plainer or the more singable word. `free`: you may also add a connecting word, an epithet, a detail the image implies (the river's cold, the oar's knock), and you may set a couplet's two lines in a different order inside the couplet; list each addition in `added`. Never add a doctrine, a moral or a new image of your own. Paradox stays paradox, a threat stays a threat.
4. **Set the board.** The maker's board gives four numbers from 0 (cool) to 1 (hot). Make the version run at those temperatures:
   - **lineation**: hot is short even lines, each closed by punctuation, the refrain back word for word; cool is lines of uneven length that run on.
   - **syntax**: hot is phrases laid side by side (and… and…, commas, colons, commands); cool is phrases hung under others (when, because, which, though).
   - **rhythm**: count stresses, not syllables. Hot is one steady groove, the same number of beats every line (four is the English lyric's home), stresses alternating, lines ending on their stress; cool is speech rhythm, lines of different lengths, stresses crowding or thinning.
   - **rhyme**, by the planets of Morton's solar system: 1 absolute (the same word again), 0.8 perfect (bright / night), 0.6 vowel rhyme (sun / bundle), 0.4 off rhyme (love / move), 0.2 para rhyme (hall / hell: cold, uncanny), 0.1 alliteration only, 0 no rhyme at all.
   Rhyme honestly or not at all: never add a word for its rhyme, never twist English syntax into an order no one speaks ("the town without"). If a couplet cannot reach the board's heat honestly, pay it back another way (a name struck twice, a chime inside the line) and say so in `letGo`.
5. **The lens.** Write as the lens says, as that poet or tradition would have made this song in their English: their measures, their plainness or density, their way of turning. Do not imitate their diction wholesale (no "thee", no "O", no pastiche) and never quote them: the touchstone line is there to be heard, not borrowed. Living English, spoken and sung.
6. **Voice and turn.** Keep the singer's voice (the maker may give it; otherwise find it: teasing, erotic, fierce, riddling, rapt, blunt) and the song's turn: where the source swings, swing at the same couplet, as hard.
7. **Say what you did.** For every couplet, `kept` says in a sentence what the English keeps of the source's sound, image or feeling, and names the rhyme's planet if it rhymes; `letGo` says what it gives up. Both 40 words or fewer, concrete, never self-praise.

Other published English versions of these songs exist and are under copyright. Do not reproduce them or reach for phrasing you remember from them: every line must come from the source, the gloss and the accurate English in front of you.
