# The Charyapada

The Charyapada (Caryāgīti) are songs of the Buddhist siddhas of eastern India, composed in an early form of Bengali (often called Old Bengali, close to Apabhraṃśa) and preserved with the Sanskrit commentary of Munidatta in a palm-leaf manuscript found in Nepal. Fifty songs are numbered; the manuscript lacks songs 24, 25 and 48 and the end of 23, which survive only inside the Tibetan translation of Munidatta's commentary (Tengyur, Toh 2293).

## Song form

- A heading names the rāga (melody) and the poet. Translate it plainly, e.g. "Rāga Paṭamañjarī · Lūyīpāda".
- Couplets of two half-lines: "a । b ॥". Each half-line is a line id of its own (…1a, …1b).
- The refrain (dhruvapada) is usually the second couplet. Munidatta leaves it out when he counts the "padas": couplet 1 is his first, couplet 3 his "second pada", couplet 4 his "third". Shastri's edition prints ধ্রু after the refrain and after every later couplet, as a cue to sing the refrain again. Translate "ধ্রু" as nothing; the refrain is marked on the page.
- In the last couplet the poet names himself (the bhaṇitā): "Lūyī says…", "Kāṇha says…". Keep it.

## Language

- The language mixes Old Bengali, Apabhraṃśa and Sanskrit loan words. Expect verb forms in -ai / -ei (present), -ia / -ila (participles, past), the genitive -era / -ara, the locative -e / -hi.
- Bengali script does not distinguish b/v, and য/য় (y, j) and ড় (ṛ) are unstable across editions. The machine transliteration in the input is a starting point; give your own transliteration in `translit` and correct it where the word is clear.
- Paradox is deliberate: the barren cow gives milk, the frog swallows the snake. Keep the paradox; do not resolve it in the line.

## Munidatta's commentary

In the source, bracketed numbers such as [২] or [১৭ক] are the manuscript's folio numbers, and bracketed letters such as স[মা]হিঅ are Shastri's restorations of damaged text. Neither is Munidatta's wording: translate through them and never render a folio number.

Munidatta comments song by song, usually giving a Sanskrit rendering of each phrase followed by its inner sense, often in the form "X means Y" (X iti Y), and supporting it with quotations from the tantras and dohā literature. His readings are the tradition's, not the poem's literal sense: they go in notes and in the woven commentary, attributed to him.

## Twilight language

The songs speak in sandhyā-bhāṣā, "twilight" or "intentional" language: the Ḍombī (a woman of the Ḍom caste) living outside the town, the Śabarī (a woman of the hill tribes), the boat and the ferryman, the rivers Gaṅgā and Yamunā, the tree of the body, the mouse, the elephant, the deer. Translate each image as an image. Their readings (the central channel, selflessness, the innate, great bliss) go in notes, attributed to Munidatta and grounded in the segment that says so.

## The Tibetan translation beside the Bengali

Lines and commentary segments may carry `parallel.toh2293`: the same passage in the Tibetan translation of Munidatta's commentary (Degé Tengyur, Toh 2293), made by the paṇḍita Kīrticandra and the translator Drakpa Gyaltsen in Yambu (Kathmandu), with machine Wylie. It quotes each song before commenting on it, so it shows, line by line, how thirteenth-century translators understood the words, from a manuscript close to but not identical with the Nepal one.

- Translate the Bengali (and Munidatta's Sanskrit): it is the reading text. The Tibetan is evidence, not a second source to blend in.
- Use it where the Bengali is damaged, obscure or unstable, and to check how a word was read. When it reads differently (another word, a negative the Bengali lacks, an image glossed), say so in a `witness` or `philology` note, quoting the Wylie and naming "the Tibetan translation (Toh 2293)". Flag a line whose sense the Tibetan puts in doubt.
- Never turn the Tibetan's interpretation into the line: its glosses are readings, like Munidatta's.

## Songs from the Tibetan

For a song or line whose witness is the Tibetan (toh2293), there is no Bengali: the Tibetan is the reading text. Translate from the Tibetan, give Wylie in `translit` (correcting the machine Wylie where needed), and add a `witness` note saying the original is lost and this is a translation of the Tibetan translation. Munidatta's commentary on those songs is likewise the Tibetan's.
