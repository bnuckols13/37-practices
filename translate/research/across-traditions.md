# Across traditions: what poems do everywhere, and what it changes in the practice

*Research note for [`POETRY-PLAN.md`](../POETRY-PLAN.md), §7 ("A comparison note"). Compiled 27 Sept 2026. It sets the five studies side by side: [`morton-in-depth.md`](morton-in-depth.md), [`sufi-poets.md`](sufi-poets.md), [`zen-mountain-poets.md`](zen-mountain-poets.md), [`shakespeare-sonnets.md`](shakespeare-sonnets.md) and [`bardic-tradition.md`](bardic-tradition.md). It reads them against the caryā notes ([`charyapada-sound.md`](charyapada-sound.md), [`how-to-read-a-song.md`](how-to-read-a-song.md)) and the edition's rules ([`../prompts/tasks/version.md`](../prompts/tasks/version.md), [`western-lenses.md`](western-lenses.md), [`precedents-song-translation.md`](precedents-song-translation.md)).*

**What this note is.** It adds no new research. Every claim cites the note and section that holds its evidence, and takes that note's mark with it. Where a note marks a claim ◐ or UNVERIFIED, or as its author's own reading, this note says so. Nothing here was checked again at a primary source. The paragraphs headed "What the row shows" are this note's own conclusions, drawn only from the cited evidence.

**Devices, not experiences.** Morton warns of "the perilous edge of New Age syncretism", the idea that "Sufi and Tibetan yogi speak alike" (morton-in-depth.md §4a). So this note compares devices: how a line comes back, where a name is signed, how a reading is kept out of a line. Where two traditions look alike, the notes claim a resemblance, never a contact or a shared experience (bardic-tradition.md §7.4; zen-mountain-poets.md §5.4).

**Citations.** "sufi-poets.md §3.2" is Part 3, section 2 of that note. "morton-in-depth.md §2, Rhyme" is the Rhyme list inside its §2. "shakespeare-sonnets.md §2d" is §2, part (d). "charyapada-sound.md A3" is that note's section A3.

### 1.1 The line that comes back

- **Caryā.** The manuscript writes ধ্রু after the couplets, and the commentary shows that the second couplet was the one sung as the refrain. Ray's account is that it was sung again after each unit "to hold the listener"; the performance is inferred from the label and the commentary (charyapada-sound.md A3). Song 10 also strikes Ḍombī nine times (how-to-read-a-song.md §3).
- **Sufi.** In every couplet the *radīf*, a repeated word or phrase, follows a changing rhyme word. That is Morton's absolute rhyme laid over perfect rhyme (sufi-poets.md §2.3; morton-in-depth.md §4a). Schimmel ties it to *dhikr*: the poet "can go on reiterating one of the divine names in the radif" (sufi-poets.md §1.2). In ghazal 2219 the radīf *hīch magū*, "say nothing", closes twelve hemistichs. The reed prologue strikes *nīst*, "is not", ten times in thirty-six hemistichs (sufi-poets.md §3.1–3.2). The *Burda* is sung with a refrain couplet between its verses, as a caryā is (sufi-poets.md §1.5).
- **Zen.** Here the return is grammatical. Kao and Mei bring Jakobson's equivalence to Tang verse: "parallelism and antithesis are in part grammatical equivalences". So a parallel couplet is a rhyme of grammar (zen-mountain-poets.md §2.1). Words also come back chained across a line break (寒山道，寒山路; 此語，此語) (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.1, §3.1). And a closing *kana* or *keri* sends the reader back to the start (zen-mountain-poets.md §2.2).
- **A correction to the plan.** The plan's "the linked verse's return" is not what the Zen note found. Renga stanzas link only to their neighbours, and the first must stand alone (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.5). Linked verse moves forward. The Zen return is the parallel couplet, the chained word and the closing cut.
- **Shakespeare.** Sonnet 29 strikes *state* three times: at line 2 a condition rhymed with *fate*, at line 10 the subject of *sings* rhymed with *gate*, at line 14 a rank, off the line end. *Like* moves from envy ("like him, like him") to the lark (shakespeare-sonnets.md §2d; morton-in-depth.md §4c). Lettered against every earlier end, the scheme is ABAB CDCD EBEB FF (morton-in-depth.md §4c; shakespeare-sonnets.md §2c). Vendler's hidden *-sing* chime runs through lines 9–14 ◐ (shakespeare-sonnets.md §2c).
- **Bardic.** The *dúnadh* closes the poem on its first word, in three grades (*saigid*, *ascnam*, *com-indsma*): "Pangur Bán" opens and ends on *Messe* (bardic-tradition.md §3.5). The keen's address returns at the head of each run, "changed by a word or two" (bardic-tradition.md §5.3). Croker's keener came back "verse after verse" to "Cold and silent is thy bed" (bardic-tradition.md §4.4). Linking stanza to stanza by a chime is "an aid to memory" (bardic-tradition.md §3.4).
- **What the row shows: one sun, placed four ways.** The return comes after every couplet (ধ্রু), at every couplet's end (radīf), once at the very end (*dúnadh*), or at the opening of every run (the keen) (bardic-tradition.md §7.4). In Shakespeare it is struck where the argument needs it. A version keeps the source's placing, not only the fact of a return.
- **Two kinds of return.** The radīf and the ধ্রু come back the same. The keen's address and Shakespeare's *state* come back changed. Morton's "to happen, it has to happen twice" covers both (morton-in-depth.md §2, Narrative). The difference matters for the refrain rule (§3.4 below).

### 1.2 The singer who signs

- **Caryā.** The bhaṇitā, "X says", names the poet in the third person, generally in the last couplet, and from Lūyī on often in the refrain couplet too (charyapada-sound.md A3). In Morton's terms it is a re-mark that signs the song as made, against Aeolian sound with no source (morton-in-depth.md §3, ambient poetics).
- **Sufi.** The *takhalluṣ* comes "usually toward the end", "usually as an apostrophe to his persona" (Lewis, in sufi-poets.md §1.6). The Persian poet speaks *to* himself; the caryā poet speaks *of* himself. By Saʿdī's time the pen name was "a mandatory structural element" ◐ (sufi-poets.md §1.6). Rumi signs *Khāmūsh*, "Silent!", or with his teacher's name, Shams, "a kind of imagined ghost writer" (sufi-poets.md §1.6). Ḥāfiẓ's pen name means one who has memorised the Qur'an (sufi-poets.md §1.4). Agha Shahid Ali carried the vocative signature into English: "And I, Shahid, only am escaped to tell thee" (sufi-poets.md §4).
- **Zen.** Hanshan names himself in the third person, "寒山出此語" (Hanshan puts out these words), as a bhaṇitā does. In "人問寒山道" the signature is inside the first line: the name is the place (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.1, §3.1). Wang Wei's "Deer Park" has no speaker at all (zen-mountain-poets.md §3.2).
- **Shakespeare.** Sonnet 29 does not sign. The nearest bhaṇitā is 136's "my name is Will"; next is 18's "So long lives this", the poem naming itself as what lasts (shakespeare-sonnets.md §3, §6e).
- **Bardic.** Moran's call, "Gather round poor Zozimus, yer friend", is the gleeman's bhaṇitā: the singer names himself inside the rhyme, in the third person (bardic-tradition.md §2.3). The crowd gave him the name from Bishop Coyle's poem, which he recited, and he wore it. The plan's "took" should read "was given, and wore" (bardic-tradition.md §2.4).
- **What the row shows: the grammatical person is the device.** The signature can be third person (Lūyī, Hanshan, Zozimus), an apostrophe (Ḥāfiẓ, Rumi, Ali), first person (Will), another's name (Shams) or absent (29, "Deer Park"). Each sets a different distance between singer and song (sufi-poets.md §5.3, exercise 3). FitzGerald shows the risk from the other side: readers took the translator for the poet. Identity does not carry across by itself (sufi-poets.md §4; western-lenses.md).

### 1.3 The answering poem

- **Sufi.** Losensky's terms ◐: *tatabbuʿ*, imitating another poem's rhyme and metre; *naẓīra*, "speaking the parallel"; *javāb*, answering the problem another poem set; *istiqbāl*, "welcoming" an old poet (sufi-poets.md §1.5). The Arabic *muʿāraḍa* chain runs from al-Būṣīrī's *Burda* to Shawqī (1910) and al-Barghūthī (2010), all rhyming on *mīm*. A documented *javāb* by Rumi to Sanāʾī is UNVERIFIED; he quotes and comments instead (sufi-poets.md §1.5).
- **Zen.** Wu Qiao grades the reply poem by how much of the rhyme it keeps, from *hè shī* (no shared rhyme) to *cì yùn* (the same rhyme words in the same order). The strictest is "like fighting with your own hands and feet tied" (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.6). Pei Di's answer to "Deer Park" picks up the host's own words (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.2). Chushi Fanqi answered the whole Hanshan corpus ◐, and Ryōkan's "a good hand" echoes Hanshan's (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.4, §1.6).
- **Shakespeare.** The English sonnet was born as translation. Wyatt kept Petrarch's octave and images and changed the ending: "free" became "Caesar's I am" (Wyatt's text ◐) (shakespeare-sonnets.md §4c). The grammar school made answering a drill, with Erasmus's copia and Ascham's double translation (shakespeare-sonnets.md §4a).
- **Bardic.** The Contention of the Bards (c. 1616–24) answers in the same form. It names the poet answered and disputes him point by point, even a dead one, Torna (bardic-tradition.md §4.3).
- **Morton.** A translation is one object's reading of another: a new thing with its own causal force. No version exhausts the song. That is the ground for printing several Englishes side by side (morton-in-depth.md §3).
- **What the row shows.** An answering poem names what it answers, keeps some part of it you can check (the metre and rhyme, the rhyme words, the images), and says something new (sufi-poets.md §1.5). The Workshop's version is this form. Wyatt is its model: held to the images, "with the change owned" (shakespeare-sonnets.md §6e).

### 1.4 The double language

- **Caryā.** Twilight language. Read literally, its images "yield the most absurd meaning" (Dasgupta). One image carries several readings (Kværne): a "charged pointer hub" (Hess) (charyapada-sound.md B8).
- **Sufi.** Wine, tavern, cupbearer, curl and mole, with a received key (Bell) (sufi-poets.md §2.6). The scholars differ on Rumi and agree on Ḥāfiẓ. Schimmel: in Ḥāfiẓ "the oscillation between the two levels of being is consciously maintained". Nicholson: Rumi "is no juggler with mysteries" (sufi-poets.md §2.6). Davis: the wine's charge came from its being forbidden, and "to explain a resonance is like explaining a joke" (sufi-poets.md §2.6).
- **Zen.** 道 is both road and Way; 寒山 is the place, the poet and the state (zen-mountain-poets.md §3.1). The capping phrase answers a koan with an image or plain speech, because technical terms "stink of Zen" (Hori, in zen-mountain-poets.md §1.6). Once Hori saw the point, "every verse expressed 'Mu'" (zen-mountain-poets.md §5.4).
- **Shakespeare.** Besides the conceit, a syntax that points two ways. The Quarto's bracket gives "From sullen earth" to the singing state; the line's position gives it to the lark (shakespeare-sonnets.md §1c).
- **Bardic.** The *aisling*'s radiant woman is Ireland. Its root is the court poem's sovereignty bride, the land seeking its king (bardic-tradition.md §1.3, §1.5). The street had a double language of its own: "I will sell my straw, and make a present of my book" (bardic-tradition.md §2.3).
- **What the row shows: the surfaces differ.** The Persian surface is a coherent love or wine poem; the caryā's is often absurd if read straight. So a Persian-style English may be fluent, and a caryā English must not make the strange fluent (sufi-poets.md §5.4).

### 1.5 The ending in silence or negation

- **Sufi.** The reed prologue ends "so speech must be short", closing in Arabic. The radīf of 2219 is "say nothing", and its last couplet confirms that the moon is "the describing of God" and silences it in one breath. The Wine Ode defines by unsaying: "spirit, but not (joined to) body" (sufi-poets.md §3.1–3.3). Schimmel: "the last mystery of recollection is complete silence" (sufi-poets.md §1.2). The note's summary: "Sufi poems end in silence" (sufi-poets.md §3.4).
- **Zen.** All four touchstones turn "on a sound in a stillness or on a 'not'": Hanshan's 不通, 未釋 and 不同; Wang Wei's 不見; a knock among roosting birds; a splash (zen-mountain-poets.md §3.4). Hanshan: "the song ends, and it is not Chan". Ryōkan: "My poems are not poems" (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.1, §1.4).
- **Shakespeare.** The two sonnets Morton pairs in class 7 are built on negation. Sonnet 116 has nine negators and a privative; 130 switches every blazon image on in order to switch it off (morton-in-depth.md §4c). With class 8's "negation, the minimal unit of thinking" (IDX), the sonnet's turn is itself a negation (morton-in-depth.md §2, Narrative; §4c).
- **Bardic.** The early lyric prizes "the half-said thing" (Meyer), and "Int én bec" has no "I" (bardic-tradition.md §1.2, §5.1). The keen returns to "Cold and silent is thy bed" (bardic-tradition.md §4.4).
- **Morton.** Negative imagery (the image you are told not to picture is pictured anyway), apophasis, occupatio and the privative prefix are the tools of classes 1 and 10 (morton-in-depth.md §2, Perception). Sells's "speaking away" names the Sufi case (morton-in-depth.md §4a; sufi-poets.md §4). Morton's emptiness is "the nonconceptuality of reality", not absence, so Wang Wei's empty mountain is both a negative image and an empty one (morton-in-depth.md §2, Perception; §4b).
- **The caryā.** The notes record riddle and paradox rather than a close in silence, and song 10 ends on a threat (charyapada-sound.md B8; shakespeare-sonnets.md §6e). This row gives a rule about keeping what a source does. It is not a claim that every source does it.

### 1.6 The conditions of composing

- **Sufi.** "While he turned, he composed" is the hagiographers' story, and Aflākī's occasions are "perhaps more often than not purely imaginary". Lewis's own judgement is that the turning "no doubt induced many an impromptu poem" (sufi-poets.md §1.1). Rumi dictated the *Masnavī* to Ḥusām al-Dīn, heard it read back, and corrected it "as any craftsman would" (sufi-poets.md §1.1). The Mevlevi rite as now performed took its shape later (sufi-poets.md §1.2).
- **Zen.** Hanshan wrote on rocks, by his own poem and by the legends; Watson judged the legend may be "literary fiction" (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.1). Stonehouse, in his hut: "Only if you sit on them will they do you any good" ◐ (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.4). Jia Dao laboured over one word on his donkey. Bashō's renga were made by a group, with a scribe and a master (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.3, §1.5).
- **Shakespeare.** The grammar school taught copia, double translation and the commonplace book, though Shakespeare's own schooling "is presumed, not recorded". The sonnets passed "among his private friends" before print (shakespeare-sonnets.md §4a–b).
- **Bardic.** The seer lies down with his palms on his cheeks (*imbas forosnai*) (bardic-tradition.md §1.1). The bardic school set a subject and a form, and the students "work'd it apart each by himself upon his own Bed, the whole next Day in the Dark", writing only when lights came at night. In the Hebrides they lay with a stone on the belly and plaids over the eyes (bardic-tradition.md §4.1–4.2). The keener recited with her eyes shut; the blind gleeman worked a Dublin bridge at dusk (bardic-tradition.md §2.3, §4.4).
- **Caryā.** The songs are headed by rāgas, and in Shaw's account they were improvised at the tantric feast (charyapada-sound.md A5).
- **What the row shows.** Two conditions recur. One is withdrawing sight so the ear can build: the dark hut, the plaid, the shut eyes, the blind reciter (bardic-tradition.md §7.4), and the sitting monk. The other is making inside a practice with others present: *samāʿ*, the renga session, the feast, the school's set subject. A practice should keep both, solitude in the dark and making before listeners.

### 1.7 Revision as a practice

- **Zen.** *Tuīqiāo*: Jia Dao weighed "push" against "knock", Han Yu chose knock, and the pair became the Chinese word for revising (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.3). Wang Anshi's draft circles "reaches", "passes", "enters", "fills", about ten words in all, each marked "no good", before "greens" (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.3). Bashō chose *furuike ya* over Kikaku's *yamabuki ya*, a one-phrase revision that changed what the cut sets side by side (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.5, §3.4).
- **The dissent, and its resolution.** Wang Fuzhi: had the scene met the mind, it would have been push or knock, so "why labour over it?" (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.3). The note resolves it for a translator: decide from the scene, not by weighing words, and the scene is the source. Go back to the gloss, not to the thesaurus (zen-mountain-poets.md §3.3).
- **Sufi.** The *Masnavī* was revised as it was read back (sufi-poets.md §1.1).
- **Shakespeare.** Wyatt changed Petrarch's collar. Erasmus varied one sentence 150 or 195 times, by edition; the count is unresolved (shakespeare-sonnets.md §4a, §4c).
- **Bardic.** Oral transmission revises without meaning to. Moran's text turns Coyle's "'Twas not the Cross" into "'Twas on the cross", drops a rhyme line, and eases a line to say (bardic-tradition.md §2.4).
- **Morton, and one channel per draft.** Morton's homework was to write a hot version and a cool version, or to rearrange a few words (morton-in-depth.md §1). The maker's own revision method takes one channel per draft (morton-in-depth.md §5 (ii), exercise 16; POETRY-PLAN.md §6).
- **What the row shows.** Revision is kept as a record (Wang Anshi's circles), judged by a scene (Wang Fuzhi), and often made at one hinge, one word or one phrase (knock; *furuike*).

### 1.8 Memory and performance

- **Bardic.** Coyle's "St Mary of Egypt" runs about 209 lines, and the *Memoir*'s text of Moran's recitation about 204 (OCR counts). The *Memoir*'s claim that it was condensed from 250 pages is wrong (bardic-tradition.md §2.4). Moran learned by ear what was read to him and could repeat a new pamphlet "line for line, and letter for letter". He improvised a couplet on a sixpenny bet (bardic-tradition.md §2.3). Mrs Harrington, who could not read, carried "a vast store of Irish verses" (bardic-tradition.md §4.4). *Sean-nós* is unaccompanied, ornamented solo song ◐ (bardic-tradition.md §1.5).
- **Sufi.** The early *Dīvān* manuscripts are filed by metre, "a kind of hymn book, for ease of locating poems to perform to sama'". The rite as now performed begins, after a hymn for the Prophet, with an improvisation on the *nay*, the reed of the *Masnavī*'s first line (sufi-poets.md §1.1–1.2).
- **Zen.** Hanshan "sang and laughed with the village cowherds" (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.1).
- **Shakespeare.** Judi Dench has performed Sonnet 29 at least three times on record, once from memory on television, in about 62 seconds by the machine captions ◐. It is her favourite because it is "the one I've learnt most recently" (shakespeare-sonnets.md §5a–b).
- **Caryā.** Newar singers still sing songs attributed to the siddhas. They are "ignorant of the meaning" and "have changed the pronunciation" (charyapada-sound.md A5; bardic-tradition.md §7.4).
- **What the row shows.** Oral transmission keeps the shape and drifts the words (bardic-tradition.md §7.4). The shape is the part made to be remembered: the linking chime, the refrain, the couplet (bardic-tradition.md §3.4). So memory is a test of an English, as the precedents note says of the 37 Practices: judge it by whether it can be memorised and chanted, not only read (precedents-song-translation.md D1).

### 1.9 The couplet as the unit of thought

- **Caryā.** "All these songs are in rhymed couplets", and "usually each couplet constitutes a complete sentence" (N. Sen, in charyapada-sound.md A2).
- **Sufi: the couplet (*bayt*; the note says "couplet").** A Persian hemistich is about as long as an English line, so one English line per half-line is the right unit for Persian too (sufi-poets.md §2.1). Nicholson found the ghazal's couplets "complete in themselves"; Lewis calls that view "reductive" (sufi-poets.md §2.3). The caryā "rhymes like a masnavī and signs like a ghazal" (sufi-poets.md §5.1).
- **Zen.** The middle couplets of regulated verse must be parallel, and the two lines of a couplet are opposed in tone (zen-mountain-poets.md §2.1). Waley: "in Chinese the stop always comes at the end of the couplet" (zen-mountain-poets.md §4).
- **Shakespeare.** The closing couplet. In 130 it trips ("thinking, thinking, thinking, oh, whoops!"); in 29 it opens on "For", explains and lands (shakespeare-sonnets.md §2h). The trip is "where an Italian shape was bent to fit English" (shakespeare-sonnets.md §4c).
- **Bardic.** *Deibhidhe* rhymes each couplet stressed against unstressed, and every quatrain "must make independent sense" (bardic-tradition.md §3.1–3.2). The gleeman's unit is the heroic couplet (bardic-tradition.md §5.4).
- **What the row shows.** In all five traditions the smallest whole thought is two lines. They differ on whether couplets stand alone (the ghazal, the bardic quatrain) or run on (Sonnet 29; the reed prologue's first couplet). The engine can measure that as couplet autonomy (sufi-poets.md §5.2).

### 1.10 What survives translation, and what does not

- **Structure and repetition survive.** Lowth's lesson holds in every note: "repeated structure crosses, repeated sound does not" (sufi-poets.md §3.3; precedents-song-translation.md A1). From the Persian: the radīf, the address, anaphora, the images in order (sufi-poets.md §4, table). From the Chinese: the image, parallelism, the plain statement, the absent "I" as a fragment, a word struck again (zen-mountain-poets.md §4, table). From Sonnet 29: the argument's shape, the turn word, the held-back addressee, one late image (shakespeare-sonnets.md §6a). From the Irish: the syntax, assonance, the clear image, the keen's returning address and pronoun rhyme, the couplet (bardic-tradition.md §6).
- **Quantity, tone, count and consonant class do not.** English has stress, not length (sufi-poets.md §4, table), and no pitch-contrast metre to carry tone (zen-mountain-poets.md §4, table). Lost from the Irish: syllabic strictness, rhyme by consonant class (*comhardadh*), *deibhidhe*'s stressed-against-unstressed rhyme, alliteration in every line (bardic-tradition.md §6). *Tajnīs*, puns and "Haply" are lost too (sufi-poets.md §4; zen-mountain-poets.md §4; shakespeare-sonnets.md §6a). The cut is lost when English reaches for "like" (zen-mountain-poets.md §4).
- **The *dúnadh* survives only if kept on purpose.** None of the versions the bardic note checked keeps it (bardic-tradition.md §6). The radīf, by contrast, is "the most portable heat" (sufi-poets.md §3.4). Davis finds it "often transferable" (sufi-poets.md §3.2).
- **Every tradition splits its English into a prose pole and a rhymed pole.** Nicholson's prose "on principle" stands against FitzGerald (sufi-poets.md §4); Waley's unrhymed stresses against Giles and Fletcher (zen-mountain-poets.md §4); Meyer's "no attempt at either rhythm or rhyme" against Hyde and Mangan (bardic-tradition.md §6). Each pole keeps something the other loses. The edition already keeps them apart: a gloss and an accurate English for the sense, a sung English for the shape and sound, and the version as Dryden's third kind (western-lenses.md).

## 2. Heat across traditions

- **Where each tradition puts its heat,** on the notes' own boards (lineation / syntax / rhythm / rhyme, 0 cool to 1 hot):
  - **Sufi: rhyme and rhythm,** the two channels prose English cannot carry. The reed prologue runs rhythm 0.95, rhyme 0.9; 2219 rhythm 0.9, rhyme 1.0; the Wine Ode 0.85 and 0.85 (sufi-poets.md §3.1–3.4).
  - **Zen: lineation and syntax,** closed lines laid side by side. Rhyme is the coolest channel: "Deer Park" 0.55, Jia Dao's couplet 0.5, the old pond 0.25. Hanshan is the exception, rhyming perfectly on every even line (0.85) (zen-mountain-poets.md §3.1–3.4).
  - **Shakespeare: the sound.** Lineation 0.8, rhythm 0.8 and rhyme 0.85 surround a cool sentence, syntax 0.35 (shakespeare-sonnets.md §2a).
  - **Bardic: rhyme,** at 0.9–0.95 in all four readings. Rhythm reads lowest on the syllabic poems ("Fuar liom" 0.55), because the heat lives in the syllable count, which a stress channel cannot see (bardic-tradition.md §5.1–5.4).
  - **Caryā: rhyme and refrain.** Song 10 rhymes seven of seven couplets, five of them perfectly, and strikes Ḍombī nine times (how-to-read-a-song.md §3).
- **So the portable heat differs.** Zen heat lives mostly in the channels that cross into English: syntax, parallelism, image. Sufi and bardic heat lives mostly in the channels that do not (metre, rhyme by class), except where it is repetition: the radīf, the returning address. That is why *Cathay* could keep Li Bai's parallel couplet in plain English (zen-mountain-poets.md §4), while the ghazal's heat needed a poet who kept the radīf, the rhyme and the signature together (Agha Shahid Ali, sufi-poets.md §4).
- **The Baked Alaska of each** (hot and cool channels in one poem):
  - **The ghazal:** the hottest rhyme over the coolest narrative, couplets that stand apart (morton-in-depth.md §4a).
  - **Sonnet 29:** hot sound round a cool, thinking sentence whose main verb waits for line 10 (shakespeare-sonnets.md §2a, §2e). Every existing lens runs syntax at 0.45 or hotter, so this gap is the sonnet lens's signature (shakespeare-sonnets.md §6b). **Sonnet 130:** perfect rhyme carrying negative images (morton-in-depth.md §4c).
  - **The Zen quatrain:** the note names none, but its boards set syntax at 0.9 against rhyme at 0.25 in the old pond, and "Deer Park" keeps "the one chime the quatrain allows itself" (zen-mountain-poets.md §3.2, §3.4). The contrast is a hot frame round a cold sound, with the images switched off and on ("not seen", then heard).
  - **The bardic poem:** the gleeman's hot heroic couplet against the street's speech ("She tuk her dip"): a high form in low words, with the laugh where the pentameter stretches (bardic-tradition.md §2.6, §5.4). "Fuar liom" sets hot rhyme against a rhythm regulated only at the rhyme (bardic-tradition.md §5.2).
  - **The caryā:** the notes point to hot refrain and rhyme around images whose literal sense is absurd (how-to-read-a-song.md §3; charyapada-sound.md B8). The board does not measure strangeness, so that contrast is for the ear.
- **The correction: a hot return is right for a poem leaning toward stillness.** Morton reads the mantra as repetition in the service of the death drive, and Freud's "Nirvana principle", the drive toward quiescence, as possibly more than a metaphor. So "for a song whose goal is nirvāṇa, 'hot' is not a fault to cool down" (morton-in-depth.md §4d). The Sufi note shows the same arc from its side: dhikr in the radīf, "at times uprising, at other times becoming a monotonous hammering", ending in "complete silence" (sufi-poets.md §1.2). The keen's close "is uniform" (bardic-tradition.md §4.4). Morton's note asked whether *fanāʾ* meets this reading; the Sufi note does not decide (morton-in-depth.md §4a).
- **What that changes.** Temperature matching stays the rule (how-to-read-a-song.md §2), but the board's advice must know which way a poem leans. A version that matches a hot refrain should never be told to cool it. The whelm check's "may overwhelm" (morton-in-depth.md §5 (i), 14) should not fire on a refrain the source itself repeats.
- **A disagreement to settle song by song: para rhyme at the charnel ground.** The Owen lens hears cold para rhyme as right for the cremation grounds and threats (western-lenses.md; how-to-read-a-song.md §3). Morton's essay says the Dzogchen yogi finds the charnel ground intimate rather than repellent, so cold rhyme "may be the wrong temperature where the song means intimacy" (morton-in-depth.md §4d). Both can hold. Choose by what the song does at that point, threaten or embrace, and say which in `kept`.
- **Where the engine's ear misreads heat:**
  - It reads Sonnet 29's syntax at 0.95 against a trained 0.35. It counts commas and *and* as parataxis and cannot see nine lines hanging from one *When* (shakespeare-sonnets.md §2i).
  - It pairs lines as caryā couplets, so a sonnet's ABAB reads as unrhymed (morton-in-depth.md §2, Rhyme).
  - It hears 1600 rhymes with a modern ear. This is the one direct disagreement between two notes. Morton's note says Sonnet 116 "cools exactly on its love sounds" (morton-in-depth.md §4c). The Shakespeare note finds every such pair in Crystal's index of rhymes that were full in Shakespeare's pronunciation (shakespeare-sonnets.md §2i, §3). Both are true, of different ears. The board should report both: "off to us, full in 1600" (shakespeare-sonnets.md §6c, 7).
  - It under-reads syllabic verse, whose heat is in the count (bardic-tradition.md §5.2), and it does not yet count parallelism, the Chinese couplet's rhyme of grammar (zen-mountain-poets.md §2.1).

## 3. The edition's rules, tested

The rules as the edition states them (prompts/tasks/version.md; how-to-read-a-song.md §1, §3; precedents-song-translation.md A7), each tried against the five traditions.

### 3.1 The readings go in the notes, never the line

- **For, from a poet.** Ibn ʿArabī wrote the *Tarjumān* "in the erotic style". He kept its "Divine influences" out of the lines, first in a preface and then in a separate commentary (sufi-poets.md §1.3, §5.5).
- **For, from the translators.** Nicholson kept every reading of the Wine Ode in footnotes, and Whinfield's "Love signifies…" is a footnote (sufi-poets.md §3.1, §5.5). Pound's note to "The Jewel Stairs' Grievance" does in prose what the poem does by allusion (zen-mountain-poets.md §4). Mangan's "Dark Rosaleen" never decodes the woman who is Ireland (bardic-tradition.md §6).
- **For, from the practices.** The capping phrase forbids the doctrine word. Munidatta supplies readings; the Zen master withholds them (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.6, §5.4). Sonnet 29 flags its simile ("Like to"), so the lark means by moving, not by code (shakespeare-sonnets.md §2f).
- **Against, first: some poems put the key in the line.** The wine drunk "before the vine was made"; the reed's cry that is "fire, not wind"; "isn't this the describing of God?" "It is." To keep those is to keep the images (sufi-poets.md §5.5).
- **Against, second: some forms are commentary.** The *Masnavī* stops for explanation inside its own couplets. A *Masnavī* lens may carry the teaching the source states in its lines, and only that (sufi-poets.md §5.5).
- **The mirror error.** Barks removed what the lines name: the Houris, *kufr* and *islām*. Lewis: his versions "leave out material that might seem repetitious" and "teleport the poems of Rumi out of their cultural and Islamic context" (sufi-poets.md §4, §5.5).
- **No reading is the answer.** Nicholson finds that Ibn ʿArabī's own explanations "overshoot the mark", and that the commentator al-Nābulusī "brings in philosophical theories" (sufi-poets.md §5.5). Blyth made haiku a form of Zen further than any Japanese commentator Wilkinson knows (zen-mountain-poets.md §4). So every note says whose reading it gives.
- **Refined rule.** *Add nothing the line does not name, and remove nothing it names. A reading goes in a note that says whose reading it is. What the poem glosses in its own lines stays in the line.*

### 3.2 Keep the strange strange

- **For.** Decoding an image is a measurement that ends its many states (morton-in-depth.md §3, §4d). Hess's pointer hub (charyapada-sound.md B8); Davis's joke (sufi-poets.md §2.6); Meyer's half-said thing (bardic-tradition.md §1.2). Weinberger on an ornate "Deer Park": Wang could have written it that way; "He didn't." ◐ (zen-mountain-poets.md §3.2).
- **A qualification.** Strangeness belongs to the source's surface; it is not a style. The Persian surface is coherent and the caryā's is often absurd. Keep each as fluent, or as strange, as it is (sufi-poets.md §5.4).
- **Identity is part of the strangeness.** Song 1 opens by identity: "The body is a fine tree with five branches". A sonnet version must not add *like* to it, because that reads the riddle inside the line (shakespeare-sonnets.md §6e). Pound turned Li Bai's juxtaposition into a simile ("Mind like a floating wide cloud") and lost the cut (zen-mountain-poets.md §4).
- **Refined rule.** *An identity stays an identity and a juxtaposition stays a juxtaposition: add no "like", "as" or "because" between images the source sets side by side.*

### 3.3 Same shape

- **For.** One English line per half-line is right for Persian (sufi-poets.md §2.1). Waley kept one stress per Chinese character and the stop at the couplet's end (zen-mountain-poets.md §4). The bardic quatrain is closed (bardic-tradition.md §3.1). The English sonnet is an Italian shape kept and bent (shakespeare-sonnets.md §4c).
- **The limit.** Shape by count does not cross. Forcing 5-7-5 syllables in English yields "repetitive gibberish" (Wilkinson, in zen-mountain-poets.md §4), and syllabic strictness is lost (bardic-tradition.md §6). "Same shape" means the same units in the same order: lines, couplets, the refrain's place, the signature's place, the turn's place.
- **Where a lens would break it.** The rubāʿī turns a couplet and its refrain into four lines, so it belongs in the Practice room, not in a full version (sufi-poets.md §5.1). The sonnet's volta never moves a caryā's turn: song 10 turns in its last line, which is 130's place, not 29's (shakespeare-sonnets.md §6e).
- **Refined rules.** *Turn where the song turns, as hard* (how-to-read-a-song.md §3; version.md rule 6). *Hold back what the source holds back:* 29's main verb waits for line 10, and a version keeps the delay by English means (shakespeare-sonnets.md §2e). *Keep couplets as closed, or as open, as the source's.*

### 3.4 The refrain the same every time

- **For.** The King James Bible renders Psalm 136's refrain identically all 26 times (precedents-song-translation.md A7). The radīf is identical by definition, and a literal "say nothing" at every couplet's end keeps the sun (sufi-poets.md §2.3, §3.2). Ali's "Tonight" holds its radīf through thirteen couplets (sufi-poets.md §4). Cutting "repetitious" lines cuts heat: Barks did it, and so did Mehrotra, who "threw a lot out" (sufi-poets.md §4; charyapada-sound.md B4).
- **The qualification.** Not every return is identical. The keen's address returns "changed by a word or two" (*Mo chara go daingion thu!*; *Mo chara 'as mo stor thú!*) (bardic-tradition.md §5.3). Shakespeare's *state* returns with a new meaning (shakespeare-sonnets.md §2d). Hanshan chains 寒山道 into 寒山路 (zen-mountain-poets.md §3.1).
- **Refined rule.** *Repeat exactly what the source repeats exactly. Where the source changes a return, change the English as much and in the same place. Never vary a return for elegance, and never drop one as repetitious.* Fox's loosening of the leading word (precedents-song-translation.md A3) applies to words struck across a whole text, not to a refrain.

### 3.5 The signature kept

- **For.** The bhaṇitā, the takhalluṣ, Hanshan's self-naming and Zozimus's call each sit in a fixed slot, and each carries into English (charyapada-sound.md A3; sufi-poets.md §4, §5.4; zen-mountain-poets.md §1.1; bardic-tradition.md §2.3).
- **The grammatical person is the device.** Versions keep the person, and keep the tag's recurring English, "says" (sufi-poets.md §5.4; version.md rule 1). The ghazal lens's own-voice exercise ("the last couplet addresses you by a pen name") is for a poem of one's own; in a version of a caryā the third person stays (sufi-poets.md §5.3, §5.4). "O Ḍombī" where the song says "hey" puts a pulpit between singer and listener (how-to-read-a-song.md §3).
- **Other names in the slot.** Rumi's "Shams" puts the teacher's name where the poet's would go. A caryā parallel exists only where a song names the guru, which needs checking song by song (sufi-poets.md §5.4).
- **Absence is kept too.** Sonnet 29 does not sign. A sonnet version keeps the song's signature where the song has it and lets it do the sonnet's work of self-reference (shakespeare-sonnets.md §6e). To add a signature is to add a line the source lacks.
- **Refined rule.** *Keep the signature in its slot and in its grammatical person. Third person stays third ("Lūyī says"), an apostrophe stays an apostrophe, another's name stays another's. Add none where the source has none.*

### 3.6 Rules the notes add

- **No added "I".** Fletcher's "Deer Park" adds "I hear" and a question the Chinese never asks. Weinberger marks every "I" and "we" that translators add ◐. The Zen note's rule: "keep the 'not', keep the sound, and add no one to hear it" (zen-mountain-poets.md §3.2, §3.4). The engine can flag it with the absent-I test (zen-mountain-poets.md §5.2). Where the source has an "I", as in song 10's "I will kill Ḍombī", it stays.
- **Keep the poem's own paradoxes and silences.** The Sufi poems end in silence, and an English that explains "runs against the poem's own last move" (sufi-poets.md §3.4). It must "unsay as hard as it says" (sufi-poets.md §4). The Zen touchstones turn on a "not" (zen-mountain-poets.md §3.4), and 116 and 130 are made of negation (morton-in-depth.md §4c). version.md already says "Paradox stays paradox" (rule 3).
- **Decide a word by the source's scene.** Wang Fuzhi's objection is the translator's rule: go back to the gloss, not to the thesaurus (zen-mountain-poets.md §3.3). Where the source's syntax points two ways, keep both if English can. If it cannot, the note says which way the English took (shakespeare-sonnets.md §1c, §6e).

### 3.7 The refined rules, plainly

1. Add nothing the line does not name, and remove nothing it names.
2. A reading goes in a note that says whose reading it is. What the poem glosses in its own lines stays in the line.
3. Keep the strange as strange, and the plain as plain, as the source's surface is.
4. An identity stays an identity. Add no "like", "as" or "because" between images the source sets side by side.
5. Keep the same units in the same order: one English line per half-line, couplets as closed as the source's, the refrain and the signature in their slots.
6. Turn where the song turns, as hard. Hold back what the source holds back.
7. Repeat exactly what the source repeats exactly. Vary a return only as much as the source varies it, and in the same place. Never drop one as repetitious.
8. Keep the signature in its slot and in its grammatical person. Add none.
9. Add no "I", "you" or "we" the source lacks. Keep every one it has.
10. Keep the poem's own paradoxes, negations and silences. Never end on an explanation.
11. Decide a word by the source's scene, from the gloss. Where the syntax points two ways, keep both or say which.

## 4. The shape of the practice

- **The principle.** Each of these traditions was learned by making, inside a practice with a fixed shape. Morton set homework in hot and cool versions (morton-in-depth.md §1). The grammar school ran daily variation and double translation (shakespeare-sonnets.md §4a). The bardic school set a subject and a form, then a whole day composing in the dark, for six or seven winters (bardic-tradition.md §4.1). Koan training asks for a capping phrase after every koan (zen-mountain-poets.md §1.6). *Samāʿ* needed its repertoire (sufi-poets.md §1.1–1.2). A poet-translator's practice needs the same three scales: the day, the week and the project.

### 4.1 Daily

- **Read aloud.** One touchstone, whole, at its own pace, noting what it makes the breath and jaw do (morton-in-depth.md §2, Rhythm; how-to-read-a-song.md §4, exercise 5). Over a month, learn one by heart: Sonnet 29, as Dench did (shakespeare-sonnets.md §5a), or thirty lines of couplets by ear, as Moran did (bardic-tradition.md §7.3, exercise 5).
- **One exercise.** A short session on one Practice-room exercise, rotating the traditions:
  - Zen: tuīqiāo, the cut, the capping phrase (zen-mountain-poets.md §5.3).
  - Sufi: radīf, takhalluṣ, unsaying (sufi-poets.md §5.3).
  - Shakespeare: copia, strike it three times, the feminine turn (shakespeare-sonnets.md §6d).
  - Bardic: close on the first word, the aicill drill (bardic-tradition.md §7.3).
  - Morton: say it without it, switch an image on and off (morton-in-depth.md §5 (ii)).
  - Once a week, compose in the dark instead (bardic-tradition.md §7.3, exercise 1).

### 4.2 Weekly

- **One poem through the five channels.** Read it in Morton's order, structure to narrative (morton-in-depth.md §1), then revise it one channel per draft, the maker's own method, which the Workshop keeps (POETRY-PLAN.md §6). Keep the rejected words, circled, as Wang Anshi did (zen-mountain-poets.md §5.3, exercise 1).
- **One answering poem.** A *naẓīra* to a caryā, a reply on its rhyme words, an answer to a bard, or Wyatt's move with the change owned (sufi-poets.md §5.3; zen-mountain-poets.md §5.3; bardic-tradition.md §7.3; shakespeare-sonnets.md §6d). It names what it answers, and it is printed after its source, never instead of it (sufi-poets.md §5.3, exercise 1).

### 4.3 By project

- **A caryā version.** One song through one lens, couplet by couplet, held to the refined rules and to version.md, with `kept` and `letGo` for every couplet (prompts/tasks/version.md rule 7).
- **The maker's own poem.** One of the maker's own poems carried through the five channels over several weeks, drafts side by side. Response comes in Lerman's order: statements of meaning, the poet's questions, neutral questions, opinions only when asked (POETRY-PLAN.md §6).

### 4.4 The rooms: what each tradition gives them, and what the engine shows

- **Study: the touchstones read on the board.**
  - *What it holds.* Sonnet 29 with 18, 73, 116 and 130, in both the Quarto's and Malone's pointing, with the breath map (shakespeare-sonnets.md §1, §5d). The reed prologue, ghazal 2219 and the Wine Ode (sufi-poets.md §3). Hanshan, "Deer Park", Jia Dao's couplet and the old pond (zen-mountain-poets.md §3). "Int én bec", "Fuar liom", the keen and "The Finding of Moses" (bardic-tradition.md §5).
  - *Its form.* Weinberger's order, which is also the edition's own ladder: the source, the transliteration, the gloss, then each English by date. Each English has its board beside the source's and one line of comment in Weinberger's manner ("adds an I", "keeps 'only'"). The maker's version comes last, and the list stays open (zen-mountain-poets.md §3.2).
  - *The engine shows:* the four channels; the rhyme scheme lettered against every earlier end, with "full in 1600" where it applies (morton-in-depth.md §5 (i), 1; shakespeare-sonnets.md §6c, 7); words struck again with their positions (shakespeare-sonnets.md §6c, 5); the turn with its position label (morton-in-depth.md §5 (i), 3); the addressee's arrival and the main-clause delay (shakespeare-sonnets.md §6c, 3–4); the on / half-on / off image strip and negative imagery (morton-in-depth.md §5 (i), 5–6). Where the form has them, also parallelism, the cut, the radīf, the *dúnadh* and *aicill* (zen-mountain-poets.md §5.2; sufi-poets.md §5.2; bardic-tradition.md §7.2). A reading is a "smart list" with no thesis (morton-in-depth.md §1), so the room shows lists and questions (morton-in-depth.md §5 (iii)), never a score.
- **Practice: the exercises.**
  - *What it holds.* The exercises each note proposes (morton-in-depth.md §5 (ii); sufi-poets.md §5.3; zen-mountain-poets.md §5.3; shakespeare-sonnets.md §6d; bardic-tradition.md §7.3), grouped by the constants of §1:
    - the line that comes back: radīf, the dhikr line, close on the first word, strike it three times;
    - the signature: takhalluṣ, the gleeman's call;
    - the answering poem: *naẓīra*, a reply on the rhyme words, answer a bard, Wyatt's move, double translation;
    - the double language: the capping phrase, Hyde-style prose;
    - silence: say it without it, occupatio, unsaying;
    - composing: in the dark, write on a rock, renga for two;
    - revision: tuīqiāo, copia, one channel per draft;
    - memory: learn it by heart, perform it, the street voice.
  - *The engine shows* the one measurement that says an exercise was done: the radīf detector at 1.0 (sufi-poets.md §5.3, exercise 2); exactly one cut (zen-mountain-poets.md §5.2); the *dúnadh*'s grade, syllable regularity and the *deibhidhe* test (bardic-tradition.md §7.2); the reply-rhyme class, *cì yùn* to *hè shī* (zen-mountain-poets.md §5.2); substitutions by name for the feminine turn (shakespeare-sonnets.md §6c, 1); poem-eye candidates for a one-word revision (zen-mountain-poets.md §5.2). The engine cannot hear a performance: record it, time each line and mark the breaths by hand (shakespeare-sonnets.md §5e).
- **Your poems: the maker's own poems, one channel per draft.**
  - *What it holds.* A poem pasted in and read on the board, then its drafts side by side, each named for the one channel it set out to change (POETRY-PLAN.md §6).
  - *The engine shows:* the full board with the space fader (morton-in-depth.md §5 (i), 4) and the whelm check (§5 (i), 14); the pronoun profile, for lyric as "the genre of the other mind" (morton-in-depth.md §2, Narrator); negative imagery and the image strip; words struck again; the turn; the scene-and-feeling ratio line by line (zen-mountain-poets.md §5.2). Between drafts it shows which channel moved, and whether a channel the draft did not mean to touch moved too. It gives advice, never scores (zen-mountain-poets.md §5.2; how-to-read-a-song.md §5), and responses follow Lerman's order with the board as evidence (POETRY-PLAN.md §6).
- **Write: versions of the songs.**
  - *What it holds.* The existing drafting desk: a lens and a heat, couplet by couplet (how-to-read-a-song.md §5).
  - *The engine shows:* the source's board against the version's (how-to-read-a-song.md §2), and a check for each refined rule it can see:
    - the refrain identical where the source's is, and changed only where the source's changes;
    - the signature's slot and person (sufi-poets.md §5.2, 4);
    - an added "I" (zen-mountain-poets.md §5.2);
    - an added "like" between the source's images;
    - the turn's couplet against the source's (shakespeare-sonnets.md §6c, 2);
    - the order of the images;
    - the glossary's forbidden words (version.md rule 2).

    The hot-return correction applies: no advice to cool a refrain the source repeats (§2 above).
- **Shelf: what is kept.**
  - *What it holds.* Versions and answering poems, each printed after its song with its lens and maker named (western-lenses.md). Each answering poem says what it answers and how, by its reply-rhyme class (sufi-poets.md §1.5; zen-mountain-poets.md §5.2).
  - *The engine shows* all the versions of one song in date order with their boards: a growing *Nineteen Ways* (zen-mountain-poets.md §3.2).

## 5. Open questions

- **Morton's untranscribed classes.** Classes 13–17, the interim theory class and the revision class (about nine hours) hold the narrator and narrative tools and the course's theory. They are known only by their archive tags (morton-in-depth.md §1, Open gaps). The class pages for 8–11 are also missing, so every IDX gloss needs checking: the heat of rhyme schemes, the image on or off, the trope as algorithm, hot metaphor and cool metonymy (morton-in-depth.md, Open gaps). What Morton said of Bashō's old pond in class 11 is unknown (zen-mountain-poets.md §3.4). Every Morton quotation marked ◐ must be checked against the audio.
- **Unverified items this synthesis leans on hardest:**

  - **Composing while turning** is hagiography, and the *Encyclopaedia Iranica*'s account of the *Masnavī*'s composition was blocked (sufi-poets.md §1.1, Open gaps).
