# The Irish bardic tradition, from the poet-seers to Zozimus: how the poems were made, and what they give the practice

*Research note for the poetry workshop ([`../POETRY-PLAN.md`](../POETRY-PLAN.md), §5). Compiled 27 Sept 2026.*

**Who is reading.** I read Old, Middle and Early Modern Irish through editions, translations, the *Dictionary of the Irish Language* (eDIL) and the metrical handbooks, not natively. Every scansion and every word-for-word gloss below is mine, made by applying the rules in Meyer's *Primer of Irish Metrics* (1909) and Knott's introduction to Tadhg Dall Ó hUiginn (1922). Where an edition gives its own reading, I follow it and say so. A reader of Irish should check the scansions before anything is built on them.

**Verification marks.** ✔ = I read the passage at the source (for quotes, the words were checked against the fetched text or page image). ◐ = seen only in a search snippet or a secondary summary. UNVERIFIED = I could not confirm it.

**Copyright compliance.** Long quotation is limited to Irish originals and to texts published before 1929: the Clanricarde preface (1722), Martin (1703), Spenser (written 1596), Coyle (1788), Croker (1839, 1844), the *Memoir* of Zozimus (1871), Ferguson (1865), Cormac's Glossary (1868), O'Connell (1892), Yeats (1893, 1902), Hyde (1893), McCall (1894), Dinneen (1900), Synge (1907), Meyer (1909, 1911), Joyce (1910), MacErlean (1910), O'Keeffe (1913), McKenna (1918), Knott (1922, 1926). Two modern editions of medieval poems (Murphy 1956, Bergin 1970) supply one quatrain each, under 25 words. Later translators and scholars (Flower, O'Connor, Kinsella, Heaney, Carson, Clarke) are described, and quoted at most once and never beyond 25 words. Living writers are named, never given pronouns.

## Part 1 — The arc: from the seer to the street

### 1.1 The *fili*: a seer who composes

- **The word.** eDIL defines *fili* as "Orig. seer, diviner", cognate with Welsh *gweled*, 'to see' ✔ [dil.ie/22070](https://dil.ie/22070). *Imbas* is "great knowledge; poetic talent, inspiration; fore-knowledge; magic lore" ✔ [dil.ie/27294](https://dil.ie/27294).
- ***Imbas forosnai*, 'knowledge that enlightens'.** Cormac's Glossary (c. 900; O'Donovan's translation, ed. Stokes, 1868, pp. 94–95) says it "discovers everything which the poet likes and which he desires to manifest" ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/cu31924071173474). The poet chews flesh and offers it to idols; then "he lays his two palms on his two cheeks and (in this manner) he falls asleep; and he is watched in order that no one may interrupt [?] nor disturb him" until the answer comes ✔. Patrick banned the rite, but left *dichetal do chennaib*, the "extempore recital", in which "there is a revelation at once from (the) ends of (the poet's) fingers" ✔.
- **Why it matters here.** The seer composes lying down, eyes covered, guarded from interruption. Seven centuries later the bardic schools teach composition lying down in the dark (§4.1). Nora Chadwick's "*Imbas forosnai*" (*Scottish Gaelic Studies* 4, 1935) set the rite beside bardic training ◐ ([CODECS](https://codecs.vanhamel.nl/Chadwick_(Nora_K.)_1935a)). I did not read her argument, so the link is offered as a resemblance, not a lineage.
- **The curse in rhyme.** The same glossary keeps Néde's satire on the king Caier, which raised blotches on his face: "Evil (*maile*), death (*baire*), short life (*gaire*) to Caier (*caieur*)!" (p. 87) ✔. The poet's weapon is a rhyme run (*maile*, *baire*, *gaire*) and a name struck five times. Stokes's note glosses *glám dícenn* as "an extempore lampoon" ✔.

### 1.2 The early lyric (8th–10th centuries): the clear image

- **Where the poems survive.** The best-known "hermit" quatrains survive as marginalia or as examples in textbooks:
  - "Is acher in gáith in-nocht" and "Domfarcai fidbaide fál" are in the margins of the St Gall Priscian ✔ (Stokes and Strachan, *Thesaurus Palaeohibernicus* II, 1903, p. 290 [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/thesauruspalaeoh02stokuoft));
  - "Messe ocus Pangur Bán" is among the "Poems in the Codex S. Pauli" (St Paul im Lavanttal) ✔ (same, pp. 293–94), beside quatrains of the mad king Suibne ✔.
- **The Blackbird of Belfast Lough is not a marginal note.** "Int én bec" survives as the example of a metre, *snám súad*, in the Middle Irish metrical tracts (Book of Ballymote; Book of Uí Maine): Thurneysen, *Mittelirische Verslehren* III §167 (*Irische Texte* 3.1, 1891, p. 99) ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/11826362bsb); CELT header ✔ [CELT](https://celt.ucc.ie/published/G400005/text001.html). Meyer uses it for the same metre, "3¹ + 3¹ + 3¹ + 3², a stanza of eight verses" ✔ (*Primer*, §51). The monk jotting in a margin fits the St Gall and Pangur poems, not this one: it was kept because a metrist wanted a clean example.
- **Were there hermits?** Meyer (1911) read these poems as the voice of the anchorite movement ✔. Donnchadh Ó Corráin, "Early Irish hermit poetry?" (*Sages, Saints and Storytellers*, 1989, pp. 251–67), argues that the "hermit" is a literary persona made by learned monastic poets ◐ ([CODECS](https://www.vanhamel.nl/codecs/%C3%93_Corr%C3%A1in_1989a)). For the practice the persona is enough: the poem's speaker is someone alone who attends to one creature.
- **Meyer on the method.** In these poems "we get … a succession of pictures and images", and "the half-said thing to them is dearest" (*Selections from Ancient Irish Poetry*, 1911, pp. xii–xiii) ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/selectionsfroman1911meye).

### 1.3 The classical bardic order (c. 1250–1650)

- **Dates and strict metre.** Knott dates the *dán díreach* of the court poets to "the period 1250–1650" (*The Bardic Poems of Tadhg Dall Ó Huiginn*, vol. I, Irish Texts Society 22, 1922, p. lxxxvi) ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/bhfuilaguinndr22huuoft).
- **A hereditary profession.** The Clanricarde preface (1722) says the schools were "open only to such as were descended of poets, and reputed within their tribes" ✔. McKenna gives two such lines ✔ (*Iomarbhágh na bhFileadh*, ITS 20, 1918, pp. ix–xi [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/iomarbhghnabhf20mckeuoft)): Tadhg mac Dáire Mac Bruaideadha's family had "since 1563 provided the professional bard to the O'Briens of Thomond"; Lughaidh Ó Cléirigh's "gave bards to the O'Donnells since 1492", and in 1595 he became their *ollamh*, their chief poet.
- **Praise and fear.** Spenser (*A View of the Present State of Ireland*, written 1596) says the bards are "had in soe high regarde and estimacon amongest them, that none dare displease them for feare to runne into reproach" ✔ [CELT](https://celt.ucc.ie/published/E500000-001/text001.html). He also records the division of labour: the poems are "usuallye sonnge att all feaste meetings, by certen other persons whose proper function that is" ✔. The poet composed; a reciter performed.
- **The sovereignty bride.** Praise is often cast as the land seeking its king. Tadhg Dall's poem to Conn O'Donnell opens "Tógaibh eadrad is Éire" ✔ (Knott 1922, poem 1). Knott translates: "Raise the veil from Ireland; long hath she sought a spouse" ✔ (vol. II, 1926 [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/bhfuilaguinndr02huuoft)). This is the root of the *aisling*'s double register (§1.5).

### 1.4 The collapse

- **The political break.** Kinsale (1601) and the Flight of the Earls (1607) removed the patrons. *An Duanaire 1600–1900: Poems of the Dispossessed* (Ó Tuama and Kinsella, Dolmen, 1981) frames its selection around "the collapse of the Gaelic order" ◐ ([publisher listing](https://colinsmythe.co.uk/books/an-duanaire/)).
- **A peace of despair.** McKenna explains the Contention of the Bards (1616–24) by "a state of peace … a peace of misery and despair … [that] forced the nation back on her own thoughts" ✔ (1918, p. viii).
- **The last generation, in its own words.** Dáibhí Ó Bruadair (c. 1625–98) wrote "Is mairg nach fuil 'na dhubhthuata" (◐: my reading of Gaelic-type OCR), which MacErlean translates "Woe to those who are not gloomy boors" ✔ (*Duanaire Dháibhidh Uí Bhruadair* I, ITS 11, 1910, pp. 130–31 [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/duanaireiiuruada11obru)). One stanza wishes that the money "I wasted in struggling with difficult print … had been spent on acquiring the rudeness of boors" ✔. MacErlean notes that the metre "is very irregular": the strict forms are loosening in the poet's hands ✔.
- **The same in Scotland.** Martin says the Hebridean orators "were in high esteem … until within these forty Years" (*A Description of the Western Islands of Scotland*, 1703, p. 115) ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_a-description-of-the-wes_martin-martin-gent_1703).

### 1.5 The song tradition in Irish (17th–19th centuries)

- **The *amhrán*.** *Amhrán* means 'singing, song' (eDIL *amrán* ✔ [dil.ie/3203](https://dil.ie/3203)). The name covers the accentual song metres that replace syllable-counting (§3.6).
- **The *aisling*.** In the vision poem a radiant woman appears to the poet, and she is Ireland awaiting her rightful king. Aogán Ó Rathaille's "Gile na Gile" opens, in Dinneen's prose (1900): "The Brightness of Brightness I saw in a lonely path, / Melody of melody … / Crystal of crystal" ✔ (*Dánta Aodhagáin Uí Rathaille*, ITS 3, p. 19 [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/dntaaodhagin03orahuoft)). Dinneen already complains that "visions of Ireland, cast into stereotyped form" were over-produced later ✔. The double register is the point: a love poem on the surface, a Jacobite hope beneath.
- **The keen (*caoineadh*).** The keen is the women's lament over the dead, with its cry (§4.4). Its masterpiece is *Caoineadh Airt Uí Laoghaire* (1773), attributed to the widow, Eibhlín Dubh Ní Chonaill (§5.3).
- ***Sean-nós*.** 'Old style': unaccompanied, highly ornamented solo singing in Irish, strongest in the west and south ◐ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sean-n%C3%B3s_singing)).

### 1.6 The Hiberno-English street ballad and the gleemen

- **The street trade.** Dublin had its ballad-printers (Harding, Nugent) and its reciters. McCall (1894) names "our street and village vocalists" and the chapbooks that fed them ✔ (*In the Shadow of St. Patrick's*, pp. 27–28 [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/inshadowstpatri00mccagoog)). The 1871 *Memoir* lists Zozimus's rivals and imitators: M'Bride "the hedge poet", Madden the weaver-poet, Martin from Meath, Kearney the blind fiddler, M'Grane ✔.
- **"Gleeman".** The word is Yeats's. He sets Moran beside MacConglinne, the wandering scholar-satirist of the Middle Irish *Vision of MacConglinne*, and calls Moran "a true gleeman, being alike poet, jester, and newsman of the people" ✔ ("The Last Gleeman", *The Celtic Twilight*, 1893; read in the 1894 Macmillan issue, pp. 67–80 [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/celtictwilightme00yeat)).
- **Where Zozimus stands.** He is the arc's last term and its mirror. He is the poet without a patron, paid by the crowd a halfpenny at a time. He works from memory, and by Yeats's account from darkness. He sets high verse (a bishop's hagiography, Scripture) against the street's voice. Both of the old functions survive in him: praise (O'Connell, Father Maguire) and satire (the Orange cobbler, "Dickey and the Yeoman").

## Part 2 — Zozimus: Michael Moran (c. 1794–1846)

### 2.1 The sources, and how far to trust each

- **The *Memoir* (1871).** *Memoir of the Great Original Zozimus (Michael Moran), the Celebrated Dublin Street Rhymer and Reciter, with his Songs, Sayings, and Recitations*, "by Gulielmus Dubliniensis Humoriensis" (Dublin: M'Glashan & Gill; Joseph Tully, 1871) ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/memoirgreatorig00coylgoog). The preface is dated "Dublin, St. Patrick's Day, 1871", "exactly a quarter of a century" after the death; the author's "earliest recollections of Zozimus" are of 1822 ✔.
  - The pseudonym is Latin for 'William of Dublin, the humorous one'. The *Memoir* mentions "a very near friend" whose 1822 tract, signed "William of Dublin", Zozimus pirated: perhaps a coy self-reference (my inference: UNVERIFIED). The real author is unknown. Peter Thompson (*Irish Times*, 16 Nov 2009) reports John Wyse Jackson's judgement that Gulielmus "is not the most reliable" ◐ ([Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/opinion/an-irishman-s-diary-1.772826)).
- **McCall (1894).** McCall calls the *Memoir* "very apocryphal and unauthentic in some parts". He accepts its account of the death and burial as "really authentic" ✔ (pp. 27, 37). He adds a police-court report and a chapbook sketch, both at second hand ✔.
- **Yeats (1893).** Yeats's essay first appeared in the *National Observer* (6 May 1893) ◐ and then in *The Celtic Twilight*. It follows the *Memoir* closely, sometimes word for word; Yeats says "I copy the record of one who knew him" ✔. Some details are not in the 1871 *Memoir*: the caper sauce, the newspaper read aloud each morning, "I have me meditations", the Homer defence in court, and the "contagious" *Moses*. Their source is UNVERIFIED (a second edition of the *Memoir* was announced ✔; I found none).
- ***The Zozimus Papers* (New York: Kenedy, copyright 1880; printings 1882, 1889).** This comic compilation calls itself "the edited, unedited and pilfered works of Michael Moran" ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/zozimuspapersser00mora). It repeats the *Memoir*'s texts and is not independent evidence.
- **The DIB entry.** It gives c. 1794–1846 and the Coyle origin of the name. It adds that he lacked "the poetic faculty and even an ear for music", a paraphrase of the *Memoir* ◐ ([dib.ie](https://www.dib.ie/biography/moran-michael-zozimus-a5961)).

### 2.2 The life: what the record says

- **Birth and blindness.** The *Memoir* says he was born "about the year 1794" in Faddle Alley, off Black Pitts, in the Liberties, and that "although born with sight, from a severe attack of illness he became blind, when only a fortnight old" ✔ (p. 5). Yeats repeats both ✔.
- **The date is not certain.** The *Memoir* prints a copy of the Glasnevin burial register (made 6 Dec 1870). It reads: "Michael Moran, of Patrick Street, Aged 43 years … interred … 5th day of April, 1846" ✔ (p. 33). Forty-three puts his birth near 1803, not 1794. Registers often misstate age, so the question is open. It should not be printed as settled either way.
- **Death and burial.** The *Memoir* records his death on Friday 3 April 1846 at 15 Patrick Street, then numbered 14½ ✔. A curate of St Nicholas of Myra, Francis Street, found him "lying on a straw pallet on the floor, and the room crowded with ballad-singers" ✔. He was buried on Palm Sunday, 5 April, in the "Poor Ground", A G 30, South, at Glasnevin ✔ (pp. 30–33). McCall gives No. 16 for the house ✔ (p. 37).
- **The memorial.** Thompson (2009) says the Dublin City Ramblers put up a marker "some time in the later 1960s" ◐. Another report gives a headstone unveiled on 6 April 1988 ◐ ([Come Here To Me](https://comeheretome.com/2017/08/07/at-last-a-marker-for-bang-bang/)). Both may be true; UNVERIFIED.
- **A legend that corrects the record.** "The Last Words of Zozimus" first appeared in the comic weekly *Zozimus* ✔. It asks for burial "In the O'Connell Circle" and is dated 1848 ✔. The *Memoir* prints it and warns that its writer "seems wholly unacquainted with the facts, and is totally in error as to the … place of abode and time of his death" ✔ (p. 29). Its lines are now quoted as his epitaph ◐ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zozimus)). They have also been used to argue that he died after O'Connell (1847) ◐ (Thompson). The record (a register copy of 1870) outweighs the pastiche.

### 2.3 How he performed

- **Stations and hours.** He began "about dusk, but on Saturdays especially", with Essex Bridge as "his grand centre". He halted "every five or eight minutes" to take coins ✔ (*Memoir*, p. 4). Other stations: Carlisle, Wood Quay, Church Street and Bloody Bridges; Dame, Capel, Sackville, Grafton and Henry Streets; Conciliation Hall ✔ (p. 7).
- **Dress and body.** A long frieze coat with a cape, "a stout blackthorn stick, secured to his wrist by a leather thong, and finished by an iron ferrule" ✔ (p. 4). "His face upturned displayed his sightless eyes" ✔. The shrug of the shoulders, "the heavy tramp of his foot" ✔ (p. 7).
- **The voice.** "A stentorian deep guttural voice, and a peculiar squeak and lisp of great power" ✔ (p. 7). The voice was his instrument, and it was imitable: mimics performed him at the Rotundo balls ✔ (p. 27). An amateur, Mr E. Rogers, outfaced him on the bridge ✔ (pp. 18–20; Yeats retells it, unnamed, as "a certain actor").
- **The frame: he signs the song.** At his station he would spread his arms and call ✔ (p. 7):

  > Ye sons and daughters of Erin, attend,
  > Gather round poor Zozimus, yer friend;
  > Listen, boys, until yez hear
  > My charming song so dear.

  This is the gleeman's bhaṇitā: the singer names himself inside the rhyme. The comic patter follows ("am I standin' in puddle, am I standin' in wet?") and the crowd answers "Go on with 'St Mary.' Go on with 'Moses'" ✔ (p. 11). Before the big recitation he would "pause and seem absorbed in a deep reverie, as if waiting for the favouring afflatus of the muse", place his hand on his forehead, mutter, and bow ✔ (p. 11).
- **Memory.** The *Memoir* credits "a very extraordinary memory, tact, and power of voice". He recited "poems, ballads, and essays, read to him, which he 'learned by heart'" ✔ (p. 5). In 1822 he was heard reciting a new pamphlet "line for line, and letter for letter", and selling it at a penny ✔ (p. 23). Yeats adds the morning newspaper read aloud to him ✔.
- **Improvisation.** A gentleman bet him sixpence that he could not rhyme "bridge" ("brudge"), and he answered at once: "Ah, kind Christian, do not grudge / The sixpence promised on the brudge" ✔ (p. 20).
- **The law and the straw.** A song attributed to him ("The Address of Zozimus to his Friends", first printed in the *Memoir*) mentions "my straw and my book". The footnote gives the hawkers' formula for dodging the law: "I will not sell my book … but I will sell my straw, and make a present of my book … it is the rale Repale of the Union Straw" ✔ (p. 25). This is the street's own double language.
- **The court.** McCall reprints a newspaper report (via *The Shamrock*) of Moran charged with obstruction. In it he invokes "the an-shent bards" and Homer, and recites a Repeal song; the case was dismissed ✔ (pp. 28–31). Yeats has the Homer plea too ✔. Both are second-hand; the newspaper is unidentified.

### 2.4 The name "Zozimus"

- **The chain, link by link.**
  1. **The poem.** Anthony Coyle, Catholic Bishop of Raphoe (1782–1801) ◐ ([DIB](https://www.dib.ie/biography/coyle-anthony-a2131); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Coyle)), printed "Life of St. Mary of Egypt, &c." in *Collectanea Sacra; or, Pious Miscellany in Verse and Prose* (Strabane, 1788), Book I, pp. 19–26 ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/collectaneasacr00coylgoog). It opens: "Th' imperial throne when Theodosius held, / In Palestine an holy Hermit dwell'd" ✔.
  2. **The saint.** In Coyle's poem the hermit-priest Zosimus is humbled by finding Mary: an Egyptian who went to Alexandria at twelve, lived "seventeen years a prostitute to man", was held back from the church door at Jerusalem, repented, and lived forty-seven years in the desert. A year later he brings her the Eucharist at the Jordan; the Lent after, he finds her dead, and "The lion even his assistance gave, / And with his claws dug up a spacious grave" ✔.
  3. **The nickname.** The *Memoir* says: "Our Irish hero having for years chanted Bishop Coyle's verses on 'St. Mary and Zozimus,' he was called by that name, and gloried in 'his four great ancestors'" (the saint, a martyr, a pope and a bishop of that name) ✔ (p. 8). Yeats agrees: he was "soon nicknamed Zozimus" ✔.
- **Corrections to the legend.**
  - The name was given by the crowd, not chosen. He then took it up in his own call ("Gather round poor Zozimus"). So the plan's "the name the rhymer took from his own best-known recitation" is right in substance, but "took" should read "was given, and wore".
  - Yeats's "Bishop Zozimus" is not in Coyle, who calls him "Hermit" and "Priest", nor in the *Memoir* ("a holy priest and abbot") ✔.
  - The *Memoir* says Coyle's original "made about 250 pages, and was condensed" for Moran. Yeats repeats this ("condensed from the much longer work"). It is not so: Coyle's poem runs eight pages, about 209 lines by my count of the scan, and the *Memoir*'s text has about 204 (± a few; OCR count). Moran recited nearly all of it.
- **What memory did to the text.** The two texts differ in small ways, and the differences are a lesson in oral transmission (the first ✔ against both page images; the others ✔ against the OCR of both books):
  - **A sense reversed.** Coyle has "'Twas not the Cross, whereon Christ's blood was spilt" (p. 22); the *Memoir* has "'Twas on the cross whereon Christ's blood was spilt" (p. 13).
  - **A couplet broken.** Coyle's "'Tis God (says she) that teacheth man indeed; / You see the school where I have learn'd my Creed" loses its second line. The *Memoir*'s "indeed" is left without a rhyme.
  - **A line rewritten.** Coyle's "To sanctify his pious life, was he" becomes "His life he gave to austerity", which is easier to say.
  - **The coda survives intact.** Coyle's mnemonic couplet comes through unchanged: "At twelve she fell; for sev'nteen years astray / And forty sev'n wept all her sins away" ✔.
  (The *Memoir*'s text may come from a chapbook rather than from Moran's mouth; UNVERIFIED.)

### 2.5 The repertoire

- **"St Mary of Egypt".** Coyle's poem, above: heroic couplets, a dialogue of saint and sinner, recited as a set piece.
- **"The Finding of Moses".** The *Memoir* says it "would appear to be all his own composition; some of his imitators introduced loose words" ✔ (p. 20). Its text is in §5.4. The *Memoir* also prints a long, earnest "Finding of Moses in the Nile" in the heroic style, said to be "an early effort, when the poetaster's brain was full of Bishop Coyle's" ✔ (p. 21). It ends on Repeal: "The Saxon rulers gone—Old Ireland shall be free!" ✔.
- **"Contagious to the Nile".** The famous malapropism is not in the 1871 *Memoir*. Its versions read "In Egypt's kingdom, upon the banks of Nile" and, in an American sketch it quotes, "In Egypt's land, upon the banks of Nile" ✔ (pp. 21, 34). The archive.org full-text index finds "contagious to the Nile" only in Yeats (1893) and later ◐ (a negative search, not proof). Yeats also calls the parody Moran's own reworking of "a poem of his own called Moses, which went a little nearer poetry without going very near" ✔. So print the line as Yeats's text, or as tradition, never as the 1871 record.
- **"St Patrick Was a Gentleman" (check attribution).** Moran sang it; the *Memoir* calls it "the first song we remember hearing Zozimus sing" ✔ (p. 9). He did not write it. Croker (*Popular Songs of Ireland*, 1839, pp. 21–22) says the song began as three verses, "the impromptu joint production of the late Mr. Henry Bennett and Mr. Toleken, of Cork". The two sang it "in alternate lines at a masquerade in that city, where they appeared as ballad-singers in the winter of 1814 or 1815" ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/popsongsireland00crocrich). So two gentlemen dressed as ballad-singers wrote the song the real ballad-singer made famous.
- **Others in the *Memoir*.** Topical pieces: "Maguire's Triumph" (1838), recited "to great pecuniary advantage" (authorship not stated), and songs on O'Connell's new Corporation and his trial ✔. Satire: "Dickey and the Yeoman" ("At the dirty end of Dirty lane, / Liv'd a dirty cobler") ✔, "dirty" struck three times in two lines. "The Song of Zozimus" ("Gather round me boys, will yez / Gather round me?") ✔, with which Yeats closes his essay ✔.

### 2.6 His technique, in one paragraph

He had no book. He learned by ear what was read to him and kept a whole hagiography of about two hundred couplets in his head, with the drift of any oral text. He set the heroic couplet, in Yeats's phrase "the intolerable cadence of the eighteenth century", against the street's speech ("She tuk her dip"). He broke its decorum on purpose, with bathos, malapropism, Dublin place-names in Egypt, and patter to a crowd he could not see. He framed every piece with his own name and a call to gather. The street was his stage and his censor: he talked his way out of a police court, was bested once by an imitator and outlived the rest, and (in a song attributed to him) knew the hawkers' trick of selling a penny straw and giving the book away.

## Part 3 — The machinery of Irish verse

### 3.1 Syllables, not stresses

- **The base rule.** Meyer: most Irish verse from the eighth to the seventeenth century requires "(1) a fixed number of syllables in each verse, (2) rhyme, or consonance, in the final word of each verse or couplet". He adds: "There is no rhythm (regulated stress), except in the rhyming words at the end; and in the debide-metres … even the rhyme is unrhythmical" ✔ (*A Primer of Irish Metrics*, 1909, §5 [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/primerofirishmetr00meye)).
- **Against Morton.** This is the opposite of English. Morton: "five syllables is not pentameter; five stresses is" ([how-to-read-a-song.md](how-to-read-a-song.md)). An Irish line is a count, like the caryā's mātrās; an English line is a pulse. What survives into English has to choose one (§6).
- **Where the system came from.** Meyer, following Thurneysen, derives it from fifth- and sixth-century Latin hymn metres ✔ (§5 note; 1911, p. xiii). Every stanza "must make independent sense" ✔ (§6): the quatrain is closed, as the caryā couplet is.

### 3.2 The metres of *dán díreach* (Knott 1922, pp. lxxxvi–lxxxviii ✔)

- **Rules common to all.** "The last two words of each stanza must alliterate. Each line must end with a fully stressed word. The conclusion of the poem should recall the opening word" ✔.
- ***Deibhidhe*.** "The commonest of the *dán díreach* metres, being by far the easiest of them all (with all due respect to Francis O'Molloy and his echoers)" ✔. Four lines of seven syllables; *rinn* and *airdrinn* between the lines of each couplet; at least two internal rhymes in the second couplet; two alliterating words in each line ✔. *Rinn* and *airdrinn*: the second line's end-word is exactly one syllable longer than the first's and rhymes on its unstressed tail (Meyer §33 note ✔), so a stressed syllable rhymes with an unstressed one. Meyer calls this rhyme "unrhythmical" ✔ (§16).
- ***Rannaíocht mhór*.** Seven syllables, every line ending in a monosyllable. The line-ends of all four lines consonate (§3.3); lines 2 and 4 rhyme. Two internal rhymes between lines 3 and 4. "The last word of the 3rd line rimes with a word in the 4th", which is *aicill*. Two alliterating words in each line ✔.
- ***Rannaíocht bheag*.** The same, with two-syllable line-ends ✔.
- ***Séadna*.** Lines of 8 and 7 syllables alternating: lines 1 and 3 end in two-syllable words, lines 2 and 4 in monosyllables, and 2 and 4 rhyme. The end of line 3 rhymes with the stressed word before the last word of line 4, which is *aicill* again ✔. In all *séadna* metres *aicill* is obligatory ✔ (Meyer §52 note). Meyer's example is a quatrain on the carrying power of Colum Cille's voice: "Son a gotha Coluim Chille, mór a binne ós cach cléir" ✔ (§53).
- ***Óglachas*.** An imitation of *dán díreach* with imperfect rhyme (*comhardadh briste*); "any *dán díreach* metre may be imitated in this style" ✔. Knott notes that most of the Contention's poems are *óglachas* ◐ (her sentence is damaged in the OCR).

### 3.3 Irish rhyme on Morton's solar system

- ***Comhardadh* (rhyme).** "Rhyme proper commences with the stressed vowel of the word, and consists in the agreement of all the vowels both in quantity and quality, while the rhyming consonants must belong to the same class" ✔ (Meyer §11). eDIL glosses *comardad* as 'equality, rivalry' ✔ [dil.ie/10745](https://dil.ie/10745).
- **The later consonant classes** (Meyer §13 ✔): (1) voiceless stops *p, c, t*; (2) voiced stops *b, g, d*; (3) voiceless spirants *f, ch, th*; (4) voiced spirants *bh, dh, gh, mh* with *l, n, r*; (5) doubled *ll, m, nn, ng, rr*; (6) *s* alone. The older poetry had three looser classes ✔ (§12).
- ***Úaithne* (consonance).** eDIL: "correspondence between words of which the vowels agree in quantity only, the consonants in class, and the final consonants also in quality". O'Molloy's Latin for it is *unio* ✔ [dil.ie/42692](https://dil.ie/42692).
- **Placing each device on the solar system:**

| Irish device | what chimes | Morton's planet | heat |
|---|---|---|---|
| *dúnadh*, full (*saigid*) | the poem's first word again at its end | absolute (the sun), across the widest span | hottest |
| *comhardadh slán* | the same vowels, consonants of the same class (*Aodh* : *-bhraon*) | perfect, by class: hotter than English perfect in pattern, a little looser in sound | hot |
| *deibhidhe* rhyme | a stressed syllable rhymes with an unstressed tail | perfect in sound, off in stress: a rhyme that trips (English *Hugh* : *curfew*) | warm-hot |
| *aicill* | a line-end answered inside the next line | perfect, displaced inward | hot |
| *comhardadh briste* (*óglachas*) | the vowels agree, the consonants do not | vowel rhyme (Earth, Mars) | warm |
| *úaithne* | the vowel may change; consonant classes and quality hold | off rhyme, and at its edge para rhyme | mild to cold |
| *uaim* (alliteration) | first sounds; any vowel with any vowel (Meyer §4 ✔) | outside the system | barely warm, but obligatory in every line |

- **Two lessons for the engine.** Owen's para rhyme, the coldest chime, is a required ornament in Irish: in *rannaíocht mhór* all four line-ends must consonate. And alliteration, which Morton leaves outside the system, is here a rule of every line, so its heat comes from frequency, not strength.

### 3.4 *Aicill*, internal rhyme, and the linking chime

- ***Aicill*.** eDIL: the "rhyme of the end-word of one line with a word in the beginning or interior of the next line"; Meyer's gloss is "'anticipation'" ✔ [dil.ie/764](https://dil.ie/764). Meyer adds that it "is obligatory in the second half of all non-*debide* metres whenever there is no consonance between the word in caesura and the end-rhyme" ✔ (§19).
- **Heaped internal rhyme.** The piling-up of internal rhyme is called *breccad* ('speckling') ✔ (§20).
- **Linking stanzas.** Linking the last word of a stanza to the first of the next by alliteration (*fidrad freccomail*, later *conachlann*) is "a practice much used in longer poems as an aid to memory" ✔ (§22). The chain of chimes is also a memory device, as the ধ্রু is.

### 3.5 The *dúnadh*: the poem as a circle

- **The rule.** Meyer: "The concluding word … of every poem must repeat either the whole or part of the first word (or first stressed word) of the poem", in three grades: *saigid* (the whole word), *ascnam* (half of it), *com-indsma* (its beginning) ✔ (§28). eDIL: "closing or ending a poem with the word with which it begins"; a tract warns "take heed to the ways of closing your poems" ✔ [dil.ie/19230](https://dil.ie/19230).
- **Verified cases.**
  - "Pangur Bán" opens *Messe* and ends "formu mud céin am **messe**" ✔ (*Thes. Pal.* II, 293–94).
  - Tadhg Dall's "Lifford Castle" opens *Ionmhuin* ('beloved') and ends "… dá gcleachtair uaibh **ionmhuine**" ✔ (Knott 1922, poem 5). Knott translates the close "ye are wont to hold dear" (1926) ✔.
  - Editors print the opening word in capitals after the last quatrain to mark the closure (IONMHUIN in Knott; *Fuar* in Bergin's *Fuar liom*) ✔.
- **In Morton's terms.** The *dúnadh* is absolute rhyme stretched across the whole poem: the hottest chime at the widest distance. The caryā's refrain comes back after each couplet; the *dúnadh* comes back once, and the poem is shut. eDIL's other sense of *dúnad* is 'encampment, fort' ✔ [dil.ie/19229](https://dil.ie/19229), so the closed poem is also a fortified one.

### 3.6 After 1600: the *amhrán* and its assonance

- **A stress-frame of vowels.** The song metres count stresses and fix the vowel of each stressed syllable. Dinneen (1900, pp. xlii–xliv) analyses Ó Rathaille's poems by "stress-frame": one is "a five-stressed homogeneous O-poem", "Gile na Gile" is "five-stressed … U A" ✔. To show the metre he writes an English stanza on the same frame ✔:

  > In sórrow and cháins we pláin like Greéce ólden,
  > By fóreigners sláin in gráves our chíefs móulder,

  The pattern repeats one vowel sequence in the same stressed slots in every line. This is Morton's vowel rhyme made into a rhythm.
- **The binding stanza.** An *amhrán* often ends with a "binding" (*ceangal*) stanza in another metre that "summarise[s] the chief ideas of the poem" ✔ (Dinneen, p. xlv).
- **The keen.** The keen runs on accentual short lines with a single stressed vowel carried through a run of lines (§5.3). Croker quotes Lloyd's rule (*Archaeologia Britannica*, 1707): four feet to a line, of which "the fourth ought to correspond with the terminations of all the following versicles" ✔ (*The Keen of the South of Ireland*, 1844, p. x [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/keenofsouthofire00crok)).

### 3.7 Hiberno-English syntax: the carrier of Irish into English

- **Joyce's claim.** "The great majority of our idiomatic Hibernian-English sayings are derived from the Irish language" ✔ (P. W. Joyce, *English as We Speak It in Ireland*, 1910, ch. IV, p. 23 [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/englishaswespeak00joycuoft)). His examples ✔: "He fell on the road out of his standing" (Irish 'I am in my standing'); "Is it yourself that's in it?" (the Irish *ann*, 'in existence'); "I am in my lone"; "he lost my knife on me"; "me after turning the Vaster" (the *after*-perfect); "and them eating their dinner" (the *and*-clause, "also from the Irish language").
- **Hyde's literal prose.** Hyde's literal prose "has courageously, though no doubt ruggedly, reproduced the Irish idioms of the original" ✔ (*Love Songs of Connacht*, 1893, preface; read in a later printing [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/abhringrdhchigec00hyde)). His gloss of "My Grief on the Sea" begins: "My grief on the sea, / It is it that is big. / It is it that is going between me / And my thousand treasures" ✔. That is the Irish cleft (*is é atá mór*) kept in English.
- **Synge.** In his preface to *The Playboy of the Western World* (1907) he says he used only "one or two words … that I have not heard among the country people of Ireland", and that "every speech should be as fully flavoured as a nut or apple" ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/playboywesternw00synggoog).
- **Lady Gregory's "Kiltartan".** Yeats's preface to *Cuchulain of Muirthemne* (1902) calls her speech "the beautiful speech of those who think in Irish", with "the vocabulary of the translators of the Bible, joined to an idiom which makes it tender, compassionate, and complaisant, like the Irish language itself" ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/cuchulainofmuirt00greg_0).

## Part 4 — How it was practised

### 4.1 The bardic schools: years of study, composition in the dark

- **The source.** The preface to *Memoirs of the Right Honourable the Marquis of Clanricarde* (London, 1722) is attributed to Thomas O'Sullivan ◐ ([History Ireland](https://historyireland.com/description-of-a-bardic-school/)). I read the passage in the 1722 printing (OCR damaged) ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_memoirs-of-the-right-hon_clanricarde-ulick-de-bu_1722). Where the 1722 OCR fails I used the 1744 reprint ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_memoirs-of-the-right-hon_clanricarde-ulick-de-bu_1744).
- **Entry.** "The qualifications first requir'd were reading well, writing the mother-tongue, and a strong memory" ✔. The school stood in "the solitary recess of a garden … far out of the reach of any noise" ✔.
- **The dark room.** "The Structure was a snug, low Hut, and Beds in it at convenient Distances … No Windows to let in the Day, nor any Light at all us'd but that of Candles, and these brought in at a proper Season only" ✔ (1722).
- **The day's work.** The masters gave each class a subject, "determining the Number of Rhimes, and clearing what was to be chiefly observ'd therein as to Syllables, Quartans, Concord, Correspondence, Termination, and Union" ✔. "Union" is *úaithne*: O'Molloy Latinised it as *unio* (eDIL, above). "Concord" is probably *comhardadh*, "Termination" probably the *rinn* or *dúnadh*; those two matchings are my conjecture. Then: "they work'd it apart each by himself upon his own Bed, the whole next Day in the Dark, till at a certain Hour in the Night, Lights being brought in, they committed it to writing" ✔.
- **The reason given.** "The reason of laying the study aforesaid in the dark, was doubtless to avoid the distraction, which light and the variety of objects represented thereby, commonly occasions" ✔ (1744 text; "variety" is my reading of a damaged word).
- **Length and season.** "It was six or seven years before a mastery … was conferr'd" ✔. Study ran from Michaelmas to 25 March, "the cold season" only ✔.
- **What the art was.** "The greatest beauty of the composition consisting in a certain contexture of corresponding vowels and consonants" ✔.
- **A correction.** Wikipedia's "Irish bardic poetry" has students composing overnight and writing next morning ◐. The 1722 text has them composing all the next day in the dark and writing at night by candle.

### 4.2 Scotland: the stone on the belly

- **Martin's account.** Martin, *A Description of the Western Islands of Scotland* (1703), p. 116 ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_a-description-of-the-wes_martin-martin-gent_1703). The orators ("in their Language call'd Is-Dane", i.e. *aos dána*, the people of art ◐, the OCR is damaged) "shut their Doors and Windows for a days time, and lie on their Backs, with a Stone upon their Belly, and Plaids about their Heads, and their Eyes being covered" ✔. And "they furnish such a Stile from this dark Cell, as is understood by very few" ✔.
- **What the two accounts share.** Both describe the same technique: lying down in darkness (in Martin, with a stone on the belly), a whole day with the subject, and writing (or delivery) only afterwards. It resembles the *imbas forosnai* sleeper of §1.1, and, loosely, the Zen practice of sitting before speaking.

### 4.3 The Contention of the Bards (*Iomarbhágh na bhFileadh*, c. 1616–24)

- **The dispute.** Tadhg mac Dáire Mac Bruaideadha (bard to the O'Briens) attacked the verses of Torna Éigeas, a legendary fifth-century poet. His first poem opens "Olc do thagrais, a Thorna": "Poor — spite of your good learning, O Torna! — was your argument in behalf of Leath Mogha yonder against victorious Niall" ✔ (McKenna 1918, poem III). Lughaidh Ó Cléirigh (the O'Donnells' *ollamh*) replied. Poem IV opens "A Thaidhg, ná tathaoir Torna" ('Tadhg, do not revile Torna') ◐, my reading of the Gaelic-type OCR.
- **The stakes.** The north (Leath Chuinn) was set against the south (Leath Mhogha), with genealogy, precedent and verse craft all in play ✔ (McKenna, introduction). Other poets joined; Wikipedia counts some thirty poems ◐.
- **Dating.** O'Curry put the start c. 1604; McKenna reads O'Donnell's words as placing Tadhg's first poem after the death of the second of the Earls (1616). It was over before 1624 ✔ (p. viii).
- **As a practice.** This is the Irish *naẓīra*: a reply in the same form, naming the poet answered, disputing his claim point by point, before an audience that judges. It even answers a dead poet (Torna), as a version answers its source.

### 4.4 The keen: women's improvised lament with its cries

- **Keeners at work.** Croker's *The Keen of the South of Ireland* (1844) ✔, pp. xx–xxvi:
  - an old woman "murmuring, with a monotonous modulation of voice, about a dozen Irish verses, clapping her hands and rocking her body backwards and forwards between each verse";
  - her keen for a hanged nephew returns, verse after verse, to "Cold and silent is thy bed" ✔. Crabbe, shown a version, noted that "the sentences are all of nearly equal length, and the close is uniform" ✔;
  - the professional keener Mrs Harrington, unable to read or write, carried "a vast store of Irish verses". She recited with "her eyes always continued shut, perhaps to prevent interruption to her thoughts", rocking "as if keeping time to the measure of the verse" ✔;
  - Mrs Leary, when memory failed, would "improvise interminably" ✔.
- **The cry as sound.** A writer in the *Dublin Penny Journal* (1838), quoted by Croker, heard a keen from a distance: "I know nothing it resembled so much as the sounds of an Eolian harp" ✔ (p. l). The women began it, the rest swelled it, "and the men joined occasionally their deeper tones" ✔. This is Morton's Aeolian, heard in a human voice.
- **The hostile ear.** Wesley (*Journal*, 31 May 1750): "It was not a song, as I supposed, but a dismal inarticulate yell" ✔ (quoted by Croker). Barnaby Rich (1610) on "To weepe Irish", meaning to weep at pleasure ✔.
- **The cry-word.** eDIL *faíd*: "a cry, outcry, used of human voice (esp. of lamentation), of animals (esp. birds)" ✔ [dil.ie/21068](https://dil.ie/21068). The blackbird's *faíd* over Belfast Lough (§5.1) and the keener's *faíd guil*, 'cry of weeping', are the same word.

### 4.5 The gleeman's memory and the street

See §2.3. In sequence: hear the text read aloud, learn it by heart, keep it for decades; take a station at a bridge at dusk and call the crowd with your own name in the rhyme; test the ground ("am I standin' in wet?") and take the audience's choice ("St Mary! Moses!"); pause in "a deep reverie", hand to forehead, bow; recite, stopping every few minutes to collect; improvise a couplet on a dare; mock the mockers.

The *Memoir* also shows the tradition turning against its master. The imitators learned Moran's voice until "none could tell which was the 'impostherer'" ✔ (p. 19), and M'Grane was "led through Dublin as the real, identical, Irish Zozimus for many years after his death" ✔ (pp. 8–9).

## Part 5 — Close readings on Morton's board

The board has four channels, each from 0 (cool) to 1 (hot), as in [how-to-read-a-song.md](how-to-read-a-song.md). The heats are my estimates, for the ear to check.

### 5.1 An early lyric: "Int én bec" (9th century?)

The text is Murphy's (*Early Irish Lyrics*, 1956, p. 6), via CELT; 16 words ✔. Thurneysen printed the manuscript form in 1891 ✔.

> Int én bec / ro léic feit / do rinn guip / glanbuidi: / fo-ceird faíd / ós Loch Laíg, / lon do chraíb / charnbuidi.

- **My gloss.** 'The little bird / has let a whistle go / from the tip of a beak / clean-yellow: / it throws a cry / over Loch Laíg, / a blackbird from a branch / heaped yellow.' eDIL *gop* 'beak' cites this very line ✔ [dil.ie/26394](https://dil.ie/26394). "Heaped yellow" follows Murphy's *charnbuidi*; Meyer (1909, §51) reads *cárrbuidi* ✔, so the gloss of that word is unsure.

| channel | heat | why |
|---|---|---|
| lineation | 0.95 | Eight lines of exactly three syllables each, as Murphy prints it; each half-quatrain closes on a three-syllable compound in *-buidi*. |
| syntax | 0.8 | Two main clauses side by side. The bird's name is withheld until the last half (*lon*, 'blackbird'), placed in apposition: a small delay, not subordination. |
| rhythm | 0.9 | Syllabic. Six of eight lines end on a stressed monosyllable (*bec, feit, guip, faíd, Laíg, chraíb*), so each closes like a door. |
| rhyme | 0.95 | *glanbuidi* : *charnbuidi* is absolute (the same element twice). *faíd* : *Laíg* : *chraíb* chime on the same diphthong and lenited voiced consonants: *comhardadh* by class (§3.3). *bec* : *feit* chime on *e* plus a stop. |

- **Alliteration.** *Loch Laíg* / *lon*; *chraíb* / *charnbuidi*; *fo-ceird faíd*; *guip glanbuidi*.
- **Words struck again.** 'Yellow' (*buidi*), twice: the beak and the branch. The colour rhymes the bird with its perch.
- **Narrator.** There is no "I". The listener is only an ear and then an eye. The famous "hermit" is not in the poem.
- **The turn.** From sound (*feit*, *faíd*) to sight (*lon do chraíb*), and from the small (*bec*) to the whole lough. It is a turn of perception, not of argument.

### 5.2 A classical quatrain: "Fuar liom an adhaighsi dh'Aodh" (Eochaidh Ó hEódhasa, c. 1600)

The text is Bergin's (*Irish Bardic Poetry*, 1970, no. 29, p. 124), via CELT; the quatrain is 18 words ✔ [CELT](https://celt.ucc.ie/published/G402228/text001.html). It is addressed to Hugh Maguire (Aodh Mag Uidhir), campaigning in winter.

> Fúar liom an adhaighsi dh'Aodh,
> cúis tuirse truime a ciothbhraon;
> mo thrúaighe sein dár seise,
> neimh fhúaire na hoidhcheise.

- **My gloss.** 'Cold I find this night for Aodh; / a cause of grief, the weight of its showers; / my sorrow that, for our comrade: / the venom of this night's cold.' (eDIL *seise* 'comrade, companion' ✔ [dil.ie/36990](https://dil.ie/36990); *neim* 'poison, venom' ✔ [dil.ie/33028](https://dil.ie/33028).)
- **My scansion, by Knott's rules for *deibhidhe*.**
  - **Syllables.** 7 / 7 / 7 / 7. Line 2 reaches seven only if *truime a* elides (*truim' a*), as later verse normally does (Meyer §23 ✔).
  - **Couplet rhyme.** *Aodh* (one syllable, stressed) : *ciothbhraon* (two; the rhyme falls on unstressed *-bhraon*). The vowels agree; *dh* and *n* are both class 4. *seise* : *oidhcheise* works the same way (*-eise*), one syllable longer.
  - **Internal rhymes in the second couplet.** *thrúaighe* : *fhúaire* and *sein* : *neimh*: two, as the metre requires.
  - **Alliteration.** *adhaighsi* / *Aodh* (vowels); *tuirse truime*; *sein … seise*; *fhúaire … hoidhcheise* (silent *fh* and prefixed *h* leave vowels, Meyer §4 ✔). The last two words of the quatrain alliterate, as Knott's rule demands.

| channel | heat | why |
|---|---|---|
| lineation | 0.9 | Four seven-syllable lines; the quatrain closes its sense. |
| syntax | 0.75 | Nominal statements laid side by side, with no main verb in lines 2–4. |
| rhythm | 0.55 | Stress is regulated only at the rhyme, and the couplet rhyme deliberately sets stressed against unstressed. The heat lives in the count, which the board's stress channel under-reads. |
| rhyme | 0.95 | End *comhardadh*, two internal rhymes and alliteration in every line. |

- **Words struck again.** *Fúar* → *fhúaire* ('cold' → 'coldness'), and *an adhaighsi* → *na hoidhcheise* ('this night' → 'of this night'). The quatrain opens and closes on "cold … this night", a small *dúnadh* inside the first stanza.
- **Narrator.** The poet, at home and dry ("liom", "mo thrúaighe"), feels the patron's weather on his behalf.
- **The turn.** It comes late, in the poem's sixteenth quatrain: "Gidh eadh, is adhbhar téighthe" ('Yet it is a cause of warming'). The patron is warmed by the fortresses he has burned (the Irish ✔; the sense is my reading, which Mangan's version bears out). Mangan kept this turn exactly (§6). Bergin's text ends with the cue *Fuar*. Whether the last line itself chimes back to *fúar* I cannot judge: UNVERIFIED.

### 5.3 The keen: the opening of *Caoineadh Airt Uí Laoghaire* (1773)

- **The text.** From the first printing I found, *The Last Colonel of the Irish Brigade* (Mrs M. J. O'Connell, 1892), vol. II, p. 327 ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/lastcolonelofiri02ocon). It was "Taken down from the recitation of Nora-ni-Shindile (Singleton) Irish 'keener' (circa 1800)" and copied from a hedge-schoolmaster's manuscript in 1860. I transcribed it from the Gaelic type into roman letters, keeping the 1892 spelling (checked on the page image):

> Mo chara go daingion thu!
> Lá da bhfacaidh thu,
> Ag ceann tighe'n mhargaidh,
> Do thug mo shúil aire dhuit,
> 'S do thug mo chroidhe taithneamh dhuit;
> D'éaluigheas ó'm charaid leat,
> A bhfad ó bhaile leat;

- **The 1892 English** (prose, from Fr Peter O'Leary's literal version): "Beloved of my steadfast heart! loved with the fondest love from the day I first beheld you ride past the gable of the market-house … I stole away from my kindred with you; I fled from my home with you" ✔ (vol. I, p. 239 [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/lastcolonelofiri01ocon)). The headnote: "Dark Eileen improvised it over her husband's corpse. Portions are missing, and Mr. O'Sullivan states that verses have been interpolated" ✔.
- **My gloss.** 'My friend and firmly [mine] you are! / The day I saw you / at the end of the market-house, / my eye took notice of you, / and my heart took delight in you; / I stole away from my friends with you, / far from home with you.'

| channel | heat | why |
|---|---|---|
| lineation | 0.8 | Short lines of two or three beats; runs of uneven length, each opened by the address that returns. |
| syntax | 0.9 | Parataxis with anaphora (*Do thug mo … 'S do thug mo …*): the lines are a litany. One *when*-clause (*Lá da bhfacaidh*) opens the memory. |
| rhythm | 0.75 | Accentual (Vona Groarke: a "three-beat line" ◐). Speech-length lines with a strong final beat. |
| rhyme | 0.9 | Every line's last stressed vowel is *a* (*daing-*, *fac-*, *marg-*, *air-*, *taith-*, *char-*, *bhail-*): one vowel carried down the run. The pronoun endings pair off as absolute rhyme: *thu, thu … dhuit, dhuit … leat, leat*. |

- **Words struck again.** The address itself. Later runs open "Mo chara 'as mo stor thú!", "Mo grádh thu go daingion!", "Mo cara go buan thu!" ✔ (p. 330, page image; spelling and lenition as printed): the line that comes back, changed by a word or two each time.
- **Narrator.** The widow speaks to the dead man in the second person, in front of the community.
- **The turn.** From memory of first sight to flight with him. The whole poem turns later, from love to the mare's return with an empty saddle, to vengeance.

### 5.4 A gleeman's recitation: "The Finding of Moses" (1871 text)

From the *Memoir*, p. 21, checked on the page image ✔. I omit the middle stanza, which uses a period racial term:

> In Egypt's kingdom, upon the banks of Nile,
> King Pharoah's daughter went to bathe in style;
> She tuk her dip, then walked unto the land,
> And to dry her royal pelt she ran along the strand.
> A bulrush tripped her, whereupon she saw
> A smiling babby in a wad o' straw.
> …
> 'Oh!' says the king, to end this pother,
> 'I'll kick the reptile from Nile to Dodder,
> And then I'll search every hole and nook,
> And likely I'll find him at Donnybrook.'

| channel | heat | why |
|---|---|---|
| lineation | 0.85 | Rhymed couplets, each a closed step of the story. |
| syntax | 0.85 | Narrative parataxis ("She tuk her dip, then walked …"), then direct speech for the punch. |
| rhythm | 0.7 | The heroic pentameter stretched when the joke lands: line 4 swells to about seven stresses ("And to dry her royal pelt she ran along the strand"). The deviation is the laugh. |
| rhyme | 0.9 | Perfect rhymes throughout (*Nile/style, land/strand, saw/straw, nook/Donnybrook*). The comic feminine rhyme *pother/Dodder* is off-rhyme on the page and near-perfect in the mouth. |

- **Words struck again.** *royal* (the royal pelt, and later the royal savage and the royal lady), struck until it cracks; *tuk*, twice. *Nile* returns as *Nile to Dodder*: Egypt's river runs into a Dublin one.
- **Narrator.** The reciter tells the story in the third person, then gives voices to the king and the princess. The frame around it is his own ("Gather round poor Zozimus"). The patter is part of the poem, as the bhaṇitā is part of the caryā.
- **The turn.** Bathos at "A bulrush tripped her", where the sacred slips into slapstick. Then geography: Pharaoh ends up searching Donnybrook, famous for its brawling fair. Yeats's 1893 version ends instead on "'Tare-and-agers, girls, which av yez owns the child?'" ✔ (Yeats), with "contagious" in its first line.

## Part 6 — What survives into English, and why

- **Mangan, "O'Hussey's Ode to the Maguire" (1846) ✔** [CELT](https://celt.ucc.ie/published/E840000-003/text001.html). He made the 76 syllabic lines into long stress-lines packed with chime: "It's showery, arrowy, speary sleet pierceth one through and through". He struck the first word three times ("O, cold, cold, miserably cold is this bleak night for Hugh"), and kept the late turn: "the memory of the lime-white mansions his right hand hath laid / In ashes, warms the hero's heart!" *Carries*: the argument, the turn, a surplus of inner chime. *Lost*: the count, the closed quatrain, the *dúnadh*.
- **Mangan, "Dark Rosaleen" (1846) ✔** [CELT](https://celt.ucc.ie/published/E840000-001/text001.html). A refrain after every stanza ("My Dark Rosaleen! / My own Rosaleen!"), and the woman who is Ireland left unexplained in the line. The *aisling*'s double register survives because the English never decodes it.
- **Ferguson, *Lays of the Western Gael* (1865) ✔** [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/laysofwesterngae00ferguoft). "Put your head, darling, darling, darling, / Your darling black head my heart above" ("Cean Dubh Deelish", p. 216) carries the song's hot repetition and its return: the first stanza comes back at the end. Yet his introduction dismisses bardic praise as "an incongruous union of noble sounds and mean ideas" ✔ (p. 169): he could hear the country song, not the court poem's craft.
- **Hyde, *Love Songs of Connacht* (1893) ✔.** Hyde tried "to reproduce the vowel-rhymes as well as the exact metres of the original poems, … though I fear that the unaccustomed ear will miss most of it" ✔. His literal prose carried more than his verse did: the Irish syntax, into Synge and Gregory.
- **Meyer, *Selections from Ancient Irish Poetry* (1911) ✔.** "In my renderings I have made no attempt at either rhythm or rhyme" ✔ (p. xiii). What survives is the hermit's image, stripped of sound, as in *Cathay*: "A hedge of trees surrounds me, / A blackbird's lay sings to me" ✔ (p. 99).
- **Yeats.** He took the gleeman and the ballad, and championed Gregory's Kiltartan idiom; he did not take the metres. He named the tradition ("The Last Gleeman") and gave Moran his afterlife.
- **Austin Clarke.** Donald Davie (*Poetry Nation* 3, 1974) quotes Clarke's note (from *Pilgrimage*, 1929) that Gaelic assonance "takes the clapper from the bell of rhyme" ◐ ([PN Review](https://www.pnreview.co.uk/cgi-bin/scribe?item_id=4116)). By Davie's paraphrase, Clarke's simple pattern supports the line-end "tonic word" with a vowel-rhyme inside the next line. That is *aicill*, carried into English: the clearest case here of keeping the machinery, not just the mood.
- **Frank O'Connor.** *A Lament for Art O'Leary* (Cuala Press, 1940, illustrated by Jack B. Yeats), praised for keeping the keen's rhythm and cadence ◐ ([listing](https://www.rarebooks.ie/books/literature/a-lament-for-art-oleary-cuala-press-1940/)). I have not seen the text: not quoted.
- **Thomas Kinsella.** *An Duanaire* (with Ó Tuama, 1981) prints the Irish facing plain verse translations ◐. That is the edition's own layout: source and gloss side by side, the English not pretending to be the song.
- **Seamus Heaney.** *Sweeney Astray* (Field Day, 1983), from *Buile Shuibhne* ◐ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweeney_Astray)). In O'Keeffe's 1913 prose, Suibhne, cursed by Rónán at Mag Rath, rises from battle "like any bird of the air"; he "would not shake the dew from the top of the grass". He ends in "the yew-tree that was in the glen" ✔ (*Buile Suibhne*, ITS 12, 1913, pp. 15–17 [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/builesuibhnethef12okee)). The prose-and-verse alternation and the king-poet in the trees carry over.
- **Ciaran Carson.** *The Midnight Court* (Gallery, 2005), from Merriman's comic, couplet-driven parody of the *aisling* ◐, and *The Táin* (2007) ◐. I have not read either; how far Carson keeps Merriman's couplets is UNVERIFIED. If he does, it is the gleeman's line in a modern poet's hands.
- **Keen versions since 1940.** Vona Groarke lists O'Connor (1940), Ó Tuama (1961), Kinsella, Dillon, Paddy Bushe (2016), Paul Muldoon (2018–19) and Doireann Ní Ghríofa (2020) ◐ ([Irish Times, 2023](https://www.irishtimes.com/culture/books/2023/05/01/caoineadh-airt-ui-laoghaire-250-years-of-irelands-touchstone-love-poem/)).
- **What carries, plainly.** The syntax (the cleft, the *after*-perfect, the *and*-clause, "in my lone"). Assonance: vowel rhyme and the *amhrán* stress-frame, which Clarke and Dinneen show can be done. The hermit's clear image, one creature exact. The keen's cry: the returning address and the pronoun rhyme at line-ends. The ballad and the couplet.
- **What is lost, plainly.** Syllabic strictness, since English hears stress, not count. *Comhardadh* by consonant class, which English has no ear for. *Deibhidhe*'s stressed-against-unstressed rhyme, which English hears as a mistake. Obligatory alliteration in every line. And the *dúnadh*, unless a translator keeps it on purpose: none of the versions checked here does.

### 7.2 Measurable features for the English ear (`lib/english.mjs`, CMU dictionary)

- **Dúnadh.** Compare the poem's first content word with its last word: the whole word repeated (*saigid*) scores 1; the last word contains the first word's stressed syllable (*ascnam*) 0.66; they share only onset and vowel (*com-indsma*) 0.33; otherwise 0. Run it per stanza too, to find inner circles like *Fuar liom* q. 1.
- **Aicill.** For each couplet: does the last word of line 1 (or 3) rhyme with any word inside line 2 (or 4), excluding that line's last word? Rhyme here means CMU phones from the stressed vowel on, perfect or vowel rhyme. Report the share of couplets with *aicill*.
- **Internal rhyme density.** For each couplet, count rhyming pairs between non-final content words of its two lines. Divide by the number of couplets. The *breccad* score is the raw count per line.
- **Alliteration density (*uaim*).** Per line, find pairs of content words with the same initial phone, with only function words between them. Any vowel alliterates with any vowel; *s* plus a stop alliterates only with itself (Meyer §4 ✔). Report the share of lines with at least one pair (Knott's rule asks for every line).
- **Syllable regularity.** Per line, count CMU syllables; regularity = share of lines equal to the modal count, minus a penalty for spread. Deibhidhe test: the couplet's second end-word is exactly one syllable longer than the first, and its final syllable(s) rhyme with the first word from its stressed vowel.
- **Vowel run (keen, *amhrán*).** For each line, take the vowel of the last stressed syllable; find the longest run of lines sharing it, and the share of lines on the stanza's commonest vowel.
- **Returning address.** Stanza-initial vocative lines ("O …", "My …") that repeat with an edit distance of one or two words.

### 7.3 Exercises for the Practice room

1. **Compose in the dark.** At night, set yourself a subject and a form (seven syllables, four lines, a rhyme word). Lie down with the lights off and eyes covered, and compose in your head. Write nothing until the light comes on, or until first light. Then count the syllables. This follows Clanricarde (1722) and Martin (1703).
2. **Close a poem on its first word.** Take any draft and make its last word repeat its first (*saigid*). Then try half of it (*ascnam*), then only its opening sound (*com-indsma*). Read aloud which closing shuts the poem hardest.
3. **A deibhidhe couplet in English.** Seven syllables to a line. Line one ends on a stressed monosyllable; line two ends on a two-syllable word stressed on its first syllable whose unstressed tail rhymes with it. My own example: *Cold, I think, this night for Hugh; / each lamp puts out for curfew.* Then add two inner rhymes across the second couplet.
4. **A keen with a cry-refrain.** Address the dead in the first line; bring that address back to open every run, changed by one word. Carry one stressed vowel down each run and end lines in pairs on the same pronoun. Leave room for a wordless cry (*och*, *ochón*) as its own line.
5. **Learn a poem by heart; say it in the street voice.** Learn thirty lines of rhymed couplets by ear from someone reading them aloud. Perform them standing, with a call to gather that includes your own name. Then compare with the page and list what memory changed, as Coyle's "not" became "on".
6. **Answer a bard.** Choose a short poem and reply in its metre, naming its poet in your first line and disputing one claim, as Tadhg answered Torna and Lughaidh answered Tadhg.
7. **A Hyde-style prose version.** Translate a caryā couplet word for word into English prose that keeps the source's order and idiom ("It is it that is big"; "and me after crossing"). Then write the same couplet in plain English and hear what the first kept.
8. **Aicill drill.** Write a couplet whose first line's last word is answered by a rhyme inside the second line, not at its end.

### 7.4 Bridges to the other traditions

- **The *fili* beside the *siddha*.** Both are seer-poets whose verse is knowledge: *imbas forosnai*, revelation "from the ends of the fingers", beside the siddha's song of realization. Both stand at an angle to the established religion: Patrick banned the *fili*'s rites, and the siddhas sang against temple and priest. Resemblance only: no contact is implied.
- **The early lyric and Sweeney beside the Zen mountain poets.** One creature, exact, with the self nearly absent ("Int én bec") beside Wang Wei's "Deer Park". Suibhne in the yew and Hanshan named for his mountain are both hermit-poets whose legend outgrew the verse, and both hermits may be personae (Ó Corráin). *Buile Shuibhne*'s prose-and-verse alternation is a haibun shape; so is the caryā with Munidatta's commentary.
- **The *dúnadh* and the keen's cry beside ধ্রু and the *radīf*.** Three placings of the line that comes back: the caryā's refrain after every couplet; the ghazal's *radīf* at every couplet's end; the Irish return once, at the very end (*dúnadh*), or at every run's opening (the keen's address). All three are Morton's absolute rhyme.
- **The *aisling* beside the twilight language.** The radiant woman who is Ireland is a *sandhyā-bhāṣā* figure: the surface is a love poem, the reading is political and devotional. The edition's rule holds: the reading goes in the note, and the line keeps the woman on the lonely path.
- **Mary of Egypt beside Ḍombī.** The harlot turned desert saint, found naked in the wilderness by a proud hermit who learns from her, stands beside the low-caste woman the siddhas court and whom the commentary reads as purified mind. In both, the tradition's holiest figure is a woman from the margin. The line keeps her a woman, and the note gives the reading.
- **The blind gleeman beside the Newar singers.** Moran learned by ear what was read to him and changed it in the carrying ("not" became "on"). Thapa reports that Newar singers of the old Apabhraṃśa songs "are ignorant of the meaning" and "have changed the pronunciation" ([charyapada-sound.md](charyapada-sound.md), A5). The keener Norry Singleton's lament reached print through a hedge-schoolmaster's manuscript. Oral transmission keeps the shape and drifts the words; the edition should expect both.
- **Composition in darkness beside the Zen sitting and the siddha's inward song.** The bardic hut, the Hebridean plaid, the keener's shut eyes and the blind reciter's "perfect echoing chamber" (Yeats) are one technique: withdraw sight so the ear can build. It belongs in the Practice room next to the read-aloud exercises.
