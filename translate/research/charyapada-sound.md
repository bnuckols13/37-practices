# Research brief: the sound of the Charyapada, and how English translators have carried song

*Prepared for the "poetic layer" of the illuminated English Charyapada. Compiled 27 Sept 2026.*

**Verification marks.** ✔ = I read the passage at the source (for quotes, the words were checked against the fetched text). ◐ = seen only in a search snippet or a secondary summary. UNVERIFIED = I could not confirm it.

**Copyright compliance.** This brief quotes no lines from any published English translation of the Charyapada or of the Saraha/Kāṇha dohās (Kværne, Mojumder, Shahidullah, Moudud, Jackson, Schaeffer, Sukumar Sen and others). It quotes translators only where they talk about method, and never more than 25 words. Old Bengali appears only as the public-domain original, in one scansion example.

Some books I read in full-text scans (Jackson, Hess/Singh, Salomon, Ramanujan). For those I cite the publisher page, not the scan.

## Part A — The sound of the Charyapada itself

### A1. Metre: a moraic 16-count, loosely kept

- **What the metres are.** Nilratan Sen's facsimile edition (*Caryāgītikoṣa*, IIAS Simla, 1977), in its Introduction §4 "Metre" (pp. xxxv–xxxvi), names three main forms ✔ [archive.org/details/caryagitikosa](https://archive.org/details/caryagitikosa):
  - the 16-mora **pādākulaka** (4-4-4-4, or 8-8);
  - the 24-mora **dohā** (13-11);
  - the 30-mora **caupaïā** (10-8-12).
- **How often each is used.** Of the 47 surviving songs, 35 are "composed in sixteen moric padakulaka pattern". No. 44 is too short at 12 morae (6-6). The other eleven mix dohā, caupaïā, pādākulaka and other Prakrit patterns ✔.
- **Where they led.** Sen calls these the forerunners of the Bengali **payār** (14 = 8-6), **laghu tripadī** (6-6-8) and **dīrgha tripadī** (8-8-10) ✔.
- **How the morae are counted** (N. Sen) ✔:
  - an open short syllable = 1 mora;
  - a closed syllable = 2;
  - a long open syllable may count as either 2 or 1.
- **Worked example.** Sen scans the opening line of song 1 as *kāā | tarubara ‖ pañca bi | ḍāla*, which is four groups of four morae. He adds that "the rules of versification were very much flexible" in that formative stage ✔.
- **The rhythm is already shifting.** Sen observes that the short four-mora pulses are often replaced by longer caesural units of 8, 10 or 12 morae. He reads this as a new "compact style of pronunciation" taking shape ✔.
- **Sukumar Sen's two-type scheme.** In *Old Bengali Texts* (1948), Sukumar Sen calls the songs a "riming moraic metre which is characteristic of Apabhraṃśa" ✔ [archive.org/details/sen1948oldbengalitexts](https://archive.org/details/sen1948oldbengalitexts). He sees two types:
  - **8+7**: most songs. This developed into payār.
  - **8+8+10/12**: ten songs. This is the proto-tripadī.
- **Bengali Wikipedia's summary.** It describes the songs as mainly payār/tripadī-like, with the 16-mātrā pādākulaka the most common. It adds that "a fixed mātrā rule is not found everywhere" ✔ [bn.wikipedia চর্যাপদ §ছন্দ ও অলংকার](https://bn.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E0%A6%9A%E0%A6%B0%E0%A7%8D%E0%A6%AF%E0%A6%BE%E0%A6%AA%E0%A6%A6). It also quotes Asitkumar Bandyopadhyay: the "first sound" of payār–tripadī is heard in the caryā songs ✔ (same page).
- **Other scholars.**
  - A 1978 review notes that Kværne gives no "thorough discussion of the metre" ✔ [Banerjee, JIABS 1:1](https://archive.org/details/banerjee-satya-ranjan-1978.-book-reviews-an-anthology-of-buddhist-tantric-songs-).
  - Chatterji's *ODBL* (1926) analysed the metre of the songs along with their phonology and grammar ✔ (bn.wikipedia, §ভাষা-বিতর্ক). I did not read Chatterji's actual metrical analysis: UNVERIFIED.

**For design.** The ear-unit is a **four-beat half-line**, and a couplet is two such lines. The metre is elastic, so a loose English four-stress line is a fair equivalent. Syllable-exact imitation is not needed.

### A2. Rhyme: rhymed couplets, earliest in Bengali by default

- **Rhymed couplets throughout.** N. Sen: "All these songs are in rhymed couplets, known as *śloka*" ✔. He adds three details ✔:
  - the rhymes "are not always satisfactory";
  - "usually each couplet constitutes a complete sentence";
  - most songs have ten lines. Of the 46 full songs, 38 have ten lines.
- **Rhyme and alliteration.** Bengali Wikipedia: "almost every pada has end-rhyme (অন্ত্যমিল)", and alliteration and internal rhyme are plentiful ✔ (bn.wikipedia, same section).
- **Is it the earliest Bengali rhyme?** Only by default. These are the earliest Bengali verses we have. But the end-rhymed moraic couplet is an Apabhraṃśa inheritance (Sukumar Sen, above ✔). Jackson likewise says the dohā is marked by "a strong end rhyme, a caesura midline" (*Tantric Treasures*, p. 9 ✔, [OUP](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/tantric-treasures-9780195166415)).
- **What to claim in print.** Say "the oldest rhymes in Bengali, carried over from Apabhraṃśa song". Do not say the Charyapada invented rhyme.

### A3. The refrain (dhruva, ধ্রু), the ten-line song, and the signature line

- **What the manuscript marks.** In Shastri's text and in later editions, "dhru" follows the couplets. Mukherji's 1963 edition prints it after every couplet ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/oldbengalilanguageandtext).
- **Which couplet is the refrain.** N. Sen: "each couplet is marked as dhru … From the Sanskrit commentary it appears, that only the second couplet of each song is meant to be sung as dhruvapada" ✔. Sukumar Sen agrees flatly that the refrain couplet "is always the second couplet" ✔. He also calls the ten-line caryā a "Daśapadī", on the model of Jayadeva's eight-line Aṣṭapadī ✔.
- **Evidence from the commentary.** Munidatta introduces the refrain with formulas such as *dhruvapadena dṛḍhīkurvann āha* ("making it firm by the refrain, he says"). These are visible in N. Sen's text of the commentary ✔ and are cited by Bengali Wikipedia (following Niharranjan Ray) ✔.
- **How it was performed.** Ray (*Bāṅgālīr Itihās*, p. 638) says the custom was to sing the dhruvapada again and again after each unit "to hold the listener". Bengali Wikipedia also notes that the Tibetan translation calls it the "dhu pada" ✔. I found no direct eyewitness evidence of medieval performance. The repetition practice is an inference from the label and the commentary.
- **The song's form in music theory.** Following Banglapedia's "Sangit" article, Bengali Wikipedia classes the caryā as a *tridhātuka prabandha*. It has the udgrāha, dhruva and ābhoga sections but lacks the melāpaka ✔ (bn.wikipedia; Banglapedia itself ◐).
- **Comparison with Newar caryā and dhrupad.** Widdess (1992) reportedly contrasts Newar caryā, where the refrain falls in the second couplet, with dhrupad, where the first line is the refrain ◐ ([Academia snippet](https://www.academia.edu/1237008/Buddhist_dhrupad_Cary%C4%81_in_Nepal)).
- **A comparable living practice.** Hess says of North Indian bhajan singers that "people almost invariably take one half-line as a refrain to be repeated regularly—in general after each rhymed unit" ✔ (*Bijak*, Appendix B, p. 163, [OUP](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-bijak-of-kabir-9780195148763)).
- **The signature line (bhaṇitā).** A caryā song "invariably contains the name of the author … generally … in the last couplet" (Sukumar Sen ✔). N. Sen notes a pattern that starts with Lui and is followed by many poets: the name appears in both the second (refrain) couplet and the last one ✔. This *bhaṇai X* ("X says") line is the ancestor of the later Bengali *bhaṇitā*.

  English Wikipedia's table agrees ✔ [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charyapada). Bengali Wikipedia gives Baṅgāla as song 33, which looks like a typo. Use 43.
- **What is known.** N. Sen ✔:
  - no tāla is specified in the manuscript;
  - "Many of these rāgas are now obsolete; and names of some others might have been changed";
  - tape recordings made in Nepal by Arnold Bake and S. B. Das Gupta show the tunes "considerably changed towards the classical trends".
- **Sukumar Sen's view.** The names match those in Jayadeva's *Gītagovinda*, and they are "the earliest mentions of Indian melodies before the Muslim contact" ✔. That is his claim. It establishes shared names, not recoverable melodies.
- **Why caution is needed.**
  - English Wikipedia (citing Ray, p. 637): some rāgas are extinct, and some names "may actually be variant names of popular Rāgas we know today" ✔.
  - A modern Hindustani **Paṭamañjarī** exists in two different forms (Bilāval and Kāfī thāṭ). It survived mainly in high-style kīrtan. By about 1940 it was nearly extinct, and Nazrul Islam's *Hārāmaṇi* radio series revived it ✔ [onushilon.org](http://onushilon.org/music/rag/patomonjori.htm). The name has survived, but nothing shows the melody is the 10th-century one.
  - The Daily Star (2026) puts it plainly: "ragas and raginis themselves are impermanent; they evolve" ✔ [Daily Star](https://www.thedailystar.net/slow-reads/focus/news/the-songs-the-charyapada-awaken-4147761).
  - Widdess (2004) documents change in "the structural form of the rāga" between the medieval Indian phase and the Newar phase ✔ (as summarised by [Prajapati 2019, p. 14](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/bitstream/handle/1773/43429/Prajapati_washington_0250E_19586.pdf)).

**For design.** Present the rāga as a *heading and a mood cue*, never as a reconstructable tune.

### A5. The living tradition: Newar caryā/cacā songs and dance

- **Thapa's account.** Shanker Thapa (Tribhuvan University) describes the practice as follows ✔ ["Cacā Songs and Newār Buddhists"](https://tibetanbuddhistencyclopedia.com/en/index.php?title=Cac%C3%A2_Songs_and_New%C3%A2r_Buddhists%3A_Ritual_Singing_in_Vajray%C3%A2na_Buddhism_of_Nepal):
  - cacā is sung mainly by Vajrācāryas, and by consecrated Śākyas and Urāy in some rites;
  - singers are accompanied by *tāḥ* cymbals, *damaru* pellet drum and hand-bell;
  - "At the beginning and the end of singing, they take hold of the Rāga (Rāga Kāyegu)";
  - songs attributed to Saraha, Kāṇha, Nāropā, Tilopā and Dārika are still sung. These old songs are in Apabhraṃśa, the singers "are ignorant of the meaning", and they "have changed the pronunciation";
  - Vajrācāryas have also composed hundreds of cacā in Newari, collected in Ratna Kaji Vajracharya's *Cacā Munā* (1999);
  - he quotes the Hevajra Tantra: "Song replaces mantra, dance represents meditation".
- **Widdess's argument.** Widdess, "Caryā and Cacā: Change and Continuity in Newar Buddhist Ritual Song", *Asian Music* 35/2 (2004) [ProQuest record](https://www.proquest.com/openview/9ebfba6216ecccebffd48ead6e768f36/1). As summarised by Prajapati ✔:
  - the Newar chachā is "a living continuation of charyā music". The argument rests on name, verse structure, function and cryptic language;
  - this challenges Ratna Kaji Vajracharya's claim that cacā is wholly indigenous;
  - Widdess traces three phases: Indian Buddhist, Newar ritual, and modern "Revival";
  - in the revival phase chachā "becomes a national art-form for foreign consumption".
- **Widdess 1992.** Widdess, "Buddhist dhrupad? Caryā in Nepal", *Dhrupad Annual* VII (1992), pp. 85–105. Scroll quotes him calling caryā a precursor of dhrupad ✔ [Scroll](https://scroll.in/magazine/1020101/an-ambedkarite-is-interweaving-buddhas-teachings-with-dhrupad-in-a-leap-of-artistic-faith).
- **Are the Charyapada songs themselves sung in Nepal?** N. Sen says "the caryā-songs are still being sung in the Buddhist monasteries of Nepal", citing recordings "of some of these songs" ✔. Check this against Widdess before putting it in print.
- **The "Nava Charyapada".** Shashibhushan Dasgupta collected more than 100 later caryā songs in Nepal and the Terai. Calcutta University published them in 1989, edited by Asitkumar Bandyopadhyay ✔ (bn.wikipedia; scan listed at [archive.org/details/naba_charyapada_CU_pub](https://archive.org/details/naba_charyapada_CU_pub)).
- **Jackson on the Newar lineage.** The Newar vajrācāryas "to this day perform rituals and sing songs that tradition traces to" the siddhas ✔ (*Tantric Treasures*, p. 4).
- **The feast setting.** Miranda Shaw (*Passionate Enlightenment*) describes revellers who "improvise 'songs of realization' (caryagiti)" at the tantric feast ✔ (quoted at [en.wikipedia Songs of realization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songs_of_realization)).
- **Dance and diaspora.** Prajwal Ratna Vajracharya (Dance Mandal, Portland) was trained by his father, Ratna Kaji Vajracharya, and teaches caryā nṛtya and caryā gīti ✔ [dancemandal.com](https://www.dancemandal.com/about-us/) and [Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/article/vajrayogini-dance/).
- **Recordings.**
  - Arnold Bake's Nepal recordings (1931 onwards; British Library) ◐ [BL blog](https://blogs.bl.uk/music/2012/11/arnold-bakes-nepalese-recordings.html). The link returned 404 when fetched.
  - The CD *Charya: The Tantric Musical Tradition of the Kathmandu Valley* (Nicoletti, with Rameshwor Maharjan, 2013) ◐ [Derby repository](https://derby-repository.worktribe.com/output/803621).
- **Names I could not verify.** Tej Ratna Kansakar, Prabhakar Vajracharya and Ranjana Vajracharya: no sources found. UNVERIFIED.

### A6. Modern performances in Bangladesh and West Bengal

- **The Bhābānagar/Vabnagar Foundation.** Led by Saymon Zakaria in Dhaka, this is the major revival.
  - Its ICH NGO Forum report lists **five performance modes** ✔ [ichngoforum.org](https://www.ichngoforum.org/ich-ngo-forum-symposium-2024/revival-of-ancient-bengali-buddhist-mystic-songs-of-charyapada/):
    1. the old text sung to folk melodies;
    2. modern-Bengali versions sung to folk tunes;
    3. old and contemporary Bengali together, sung to folk tunes;
    4. contemporary Bengali sung to the Charyapada rāga;
    5. the old Bengali sung "following the ragas indicated at the top of the ancient manuscripts".
  - The same report records the critics' objections: the language is archaic, the Baul-Fakir singers lack classical training, and folk tunes are "inappropriate" ✔.
  - The Daily Star counts 578 weekly sessions at Suhrawardy Udyan as of 26 Nov 2025. It says the songs are sung with "contemporary folk melodies, native pronunciation, and the idiom of modern-day people". ### A7. Reading the IAST aloud (short guide)

Bengali Wikipedia's phonology section follows Chatterji ✔; English Wikipedia's *Old Bengali* page follows Shaw 1984 ✔ [en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Bengali).

- **a** is open, "somewhat near modern ā", as in *father* said short. It is not the modern Bengali rounded [ɔ].
- **ā, ī, ū** are long in spelling, but the length distinction "had weakened". The metre lets a long open vowel count as 1 or 2 morae (N. Sen).
- **Adjacent vowels.** Sound each vowel separately: *kāā* is kā-ā, *cīe* is cī-e. Adjacent vowels were kept, and final pairs slid toward diphthongs.
- **Nasal vowels.** *ṃ / ~ / m̐* (candrabindu) nasalise the vowel, as in French *on*. For example, *hiẽ* has a nasal e.
- **Sibilants.** *ś ṣ s* are used interchangeably in the manuscript. Say one sibilant for all three; the English Wikipedia page notes widespread /s/.
- **n and ṇ** are also interchangeable, so say *n*.
- **Word-initial y-** had become *j*.
- **Aspirates and retroflexes.** Aspirates (*kh, gh, th, dh, bh*) have audible breath. Retroflexes (*ṭ ḍ ṇ*) are said with the tongue curled back. *ḍ/ṛ* between vowels is a flap.
- **h** is kept inside words (*kāhṇa*, *karaha*).
- **Stress and pulse.** Put a light beat at the start of each four-mora group, with a caesura mid-line.

## Part B — Precedents for translating Indian devotional and mystic song

### B1. Tagore's *Gitanjali* (1912) and Radice (2011)

- **What Tagore did.** Tagore rendered his songs as English prose. The 1912 title page itself reads "a collection of prose translations made by the author" ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/gitanjalisongoff00tagouoft).
- **What was lost.** Many sources were rhymed Bengali sonnets, "seven rhyming couplets", that became "single-paragraph prose poems" ✔. Yeats's paragraphing and commas slowed the rhythm ✔ [The Wire, Anjan Basu, 2025](https://m.thewire.in/article/books/revisiting-rabindranath-tagores-english-gitanjali-and-its-bengali-original).
- **Radice's critique of the published version.** Radice on the manuscript versus the print: "The published version is much more incantatory, and has become a prayer for public use" ✔ (same article).
- **Radice's 2011 retranslation.** He translated the Bengali originals afresh and "attempted to reflect the various styles and forms … sonnets, ballads, songs". His versions alternate with Tagore's manuscript English ✔ (van Bijlert & Bangha, Oxford Bibliographies, [PDF](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:c7c8293e-3ba9-445c-b6eb-e5ccfd6e3507/files/rxk81jm332)).
- **Radice's own statements of method** ✔ [Scroll, 14 Nov 2024](https://scroll.in/article/1075590/lucky-to-learn-and-study-bengali-william-radice-1951-2024-on-why-and-how-he-translated-tagore):
  - aim "to find an equivalent form in English that will not be the same as the Bengali";
  - "I tend to use half-rhyme in translations as it leads to fewer distortions of meaning for the sake of form";
  - on register: for Tagore's classical diction, "an equivalently classical (Latinate) vocabulary";
  - on rhythm: rhythms "that are Indian rather than English" have entered his English.
- **Schelling's verdict on Tagore's English.** Tagore's "biblical-sounding paragraphs sure didn't sing in English" ✔ [Tricycle, 2018](https://tricycle.org/magazine/translating-speech-gods/).

### B2. Dimock & Levertov, *In Praise of Krishna* (1967)

- **How they worked.** It was a scholar-and-poet pairing, arranged by Bonnie Crown of the Asia Society. "Dimock sent Levertov rough translations … which she spruced up" ✔ [IITK Book Excerptise](https://www.cse.iitk.ac.in/users/amit/books/dimock-1967-in-praise-of.html). The book is now published by the University of Chicago Press ✔ [UChicago Press](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/I/bo3633241.html).
- **The same patron.** Bonnie Crown also supported Ramanujan's *Speaking of Śiva*, and Ramanujan thanks Dimock ✔.
- **What I could not find.** The translators' own note on method was not accessible, because the archive.org copies are restricted. UNVERIFIED. Jackson names Dimock–Levertov, Ramanujan, Hess–Singh, Bly and Schelling among his models ✔ (*Tantric Treasures*, Acknowledgments).

### B3. A. K. Ramanujan

- **Speaking of Śiva (1973), Translator's Note, p. 13** ✔ [Penguin](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/34926/speaking-of-siva-by-trans-ak-ramanujan/9780140442700):
  - "not to match the Kannada with the English, but to map the medieval Kannada onto the soundlook of modern English";
  - the method works "in rhythm and punctuation, in phrase-breaks, paragraphs and lineation";
  - its liberties are "towards a close structural mimicry, a re-enactment in English";
  - "A translation has to be true to the translator no less than to the originals."
- **Showing the machinery.** In the Introduction (pp. 42–43) he prints a vacana with a word-by-word literal version. Letters mark the parallel constructions, to "suggest the departures that the present translation has made" ✔.
- **Samskara, Translator's Note.** "A translator hopes not only to translate a text, but hopes (against all odds) to translate a non-native reader into a native one. The Notes and Afterword …" ✔ (quoted with page reference at [NPTEL](https://archive.nptel.ac.in/content/storage2/courses/109104050/lecture14/14_7.htm)).
- **"On Translating a Tamil Poem."** Two phrases reach me only through secondary quotation ◐ [Poetly](https://poetly.substack.com/p/what-he-said-in-the-wilderness):
  - the translator is "an 'artist on oath'";
  - a translation "must not only represent, but re-present, the original".

### B4. Kabir: Hess & Singh, Mehrotra, and Bly as a warning

- **Hess & Singh, *The Bijak of Kabir*** (1983; OUP 2002) ✔ [OUP](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-bijak-of-kabir-9780195148763). The main points:
  - **How it was made.** The two co-translators studied the text "word by word" in Banaras. Hess then finished the English ✔ (Preface).
  - **Listeners and readers.** "Kabir's original audience was composed entirely of listeners. His present audience is composed largely of readers" ✔ (p. xii).
  - **Word choice by rhythm.** She renders *sant* as "seeker" or "saint" "depending on which fits better into the rhythm" ✔.
  - **Appendix B, "A Note on Meter and Rhyme" (pp. 162–64).** It gives mātrā counts and the teka refrain practice, then works two songs through four tiers ✔:
    1. transliteration;
    2. word-by-word interlinear gloss;
    3. literal translation;
    4. a pointer to the finished version.
  - **Appendix A, "Upside-down Language" (pp. 135ff.).** This develops her essay "The Cow Is Sucking at the Calf's Teat" (*History of Religions* 22, 1983) [doi](https://doi.org/10.1086/462928). She links *ulaṭbāṃsī* explicitly to the siddhas' *sandhābhāṣā* ✔. She writes: "Upside-down language should make you feel like a fool: that is part of its function" ✔.
- **Arvind Krishna Mehrotra, *Songs of Kabir*** (NYRB, 2011) [NYRB](https://www.nyrb.com/products/songs-of-kabir). From an interview ✔ [Eclectica, 2013](https://www.eclectica.org/v17n1/iyer.html):
  - "I threw a lot out, lines I found to be repetitive or formulaic";
  - he translated "in the same spirit in which they were written or, more accurately, sung and transmitted";
  - his version is "one more version … in American English, for that's the demotic we speak".
  - From his introduction, he approaches Kabir "as an anonymous medieval singer would approach a pada" ◐ (quoted in a [review](http://middlestage.blogspot.com/2011/05/on-arvind-krishna-mehrotras-new.html)).
- **Bly as a cautionary case.** Tagore's 1915 English Kabir, and "Robert Bly's new versions adapted from Tagore", rest on verses compiled by a Bengali collector in the early 1900s ✔ (Hess, *Bijak*, Introduction). Mehrotra: "Bly, who does not know Hindi, got his Kabir out of Tagore" ✔. The lesson: never make versions from versions, and always name the text the English comes from.

### B5. The siddha precedents: Jackson, Schaeffer, Kværne

- **Roger R. Jackson, *Tantric Treasures*** (OUP 2004), "About the Translation", pp. 48–50 ✔ [OUP](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/tantric-treasures-9780195166415):
  - **Layout of each verse:** number → transliterated Apabhraṃśa (or Tibetan) → his translation → concordance → notes.
  - **Why print the original.** The Apabhraṃśa is there partly so the reader has "an echo of an echo of what Saraha, Kāṇha, and Tilopa might have sung".
  - **Line length.** Lines are broken "in a manner that reflects the length of the line in the original": a long line becomes three English lines, a short one two.
  - **Rhyme.** "I have not attempted to replicate the end rhyme of the original" (with a joke that Blake could have done it).
  - **Fixed terms.** The same English term is used for the same word every time: *citta* is always "mind".
  - **Register.** Plain, colloquial English. He inserts "you" and turns relative–correlative sayings into "if you…" conditionals.
  - **Notes.** Only what the verse clearly implies, so as not to "constrict the siddhas within a particular interpretive grid".
  - **Imagined performance.** He opens the book with a scene of a yogin singing to a drum, his "melodies rough, but his lyrics … sharp and aphoristic" (p. 3) ✔.
- **Kurtis Schaeffer, *Dreaming the Great Brahmin*** (OUP 2005). It translates Saraha's dohās from the Tibetan, with a Tibetan commentary ◐ [OUP](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/dreaming-the-great-brahmin-9780195173734). A reviewer called the translations clear and simple but faulted the omission of the Tibetan text ◐. I could not read his method statement: UNVERIFIED.
- **Per Kværne, *An Anthology of Buddhist Tantric Songs*** (Oslo 1977; repr. 2010) [HathiTrust](https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/000944935).
  - It is a line-by-line scholarly prose translation. Munidatta's glosses sit in brackets inside the English: Banerjee refers to "the bracket of his translation" ✔.
  - Hess found his "very knowledgeable translations … interrupted by many parenthetical additions and comments" and smoothed them when quoting ✔ (*Bijak*, note to Appendix A).
  - There is no metrical analysis (Banerjee ✔). The songs are not rendered as verse. Treat that last point as ◐, since I did not see the book itself.

### B6. Andrew Schelling (with Anne Waldman)

- **On older translations.** Schelling says British-era translations "make the tradition sound fatigued, ornate, and overfed". He looks instead for poems full of "overtones and undertones, not just literal meanings" ✔ [Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/translating-speech-gods/).
- **Songs of the Sons and Daughters of Buddha.** Schelling and Waldman aim "to free these elders—buried under piety, interpretation, poor translation and neglect—to breathe again". They add descriptive titles and justify them in an afterword ◐ ([Asian Review of Books](https://asianreviewofbooks.com/songs-of-the-sons-and-daughters-of-buddha-enlightenment-poems-from-the-theragatha-and-therigatha-translated-by-andrew-schelling-and-anne-waldman/), quoting the book).

### B7. Baul songs in English

### B8. Handling deliberate double meaning ("twilight language")

- **Dasgupta** (*ORC*, Appendix E, pp. 477–79) ✔:
  - the term: Shastri's "twilight language" versus Vidhushekhara Shastri's *sandhā-bhāṣā*, "intentional language";
  - the caryā songs, unlike the Tantras, "are full of enigmas in addition to the technicalities";
  - such images, "when interpreted literally, … yield the most absurd meaning".
- **Bharati** (*The Tantric Tradition*, 1965, ch. "On Intentional Language", pp. 164–71; developed from *JAOS* 81, 1961, [doi](https://doi.org/10.2307/595657)) ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/bharati-agehananda-1965.-the-tantric-tradition):
  - Bharati sorts sandhā terms into two kinds:
    - **afferent**: an object-word that intends the absolute (*lalanā*, "woman", meaning nirvāṇa);
    - **efferent**: a doctrinal word that intends a physical thing (*bodhicitta* meaning semen).
  - Terms such as *vajra* are not sandhā when they carry their standard meaning.
  - He adds two possible purposes: a **mnemonic** one and a **facetious** one, teasing the orthodox.
- **Kværne** (quoted by Hess, *Bijak* App. A ✔):
  - the songs work through "the systematic ambiguity of their imagery". One image, the tree, is glossed by Munidatta as body, as mind, and as sahaja;
  - the vernacular itself matters: "expressing the highest truth in the lowest terms";
  - Kværne lays out the four readings of the term (twilight, intentional, enigmatic, secret) ✔ (Hess's note quoting Kværne pp. 37–38).
- **Hess** ✔:
  - glossaries mislead because they suggest "a one-to-one relationship between a 'mystery' word and its meaning";
  - she proposes seeing a symbol as "a charged pointer hub connected by spokes" to several meanings, taking up Eliade's "polyvalence";
  - interpretation must stay "indirect, fragmentary, playful".

## Lessons for the Charyapada's poetic layer: 15 named techniques

1. **Couplet-as-breath (Daśapadī frame).** Render each song as its couplets, usually five. Each couplet should be one English sentence and each half-line one phrase. Keep N. Sen's observation that one line is often "unusually longer" instead of evening it out. *(N. Sen; Sukumar Sen.)*

2. **Four-beat pulse.** Aim for about four stresses per English line, standing in for the 4+4+4+4 pādākulaka. Allow a mid-line pause. Never pad or cut sense to hit the count; the source itself is "very much flexible". *(N. Sen; Radice's "equivalent form".)*

3. **Slant-rhyme the couplet.** In the poetic layer, close each couplet with a half-rhyme or assonance where it costs no meaning. Leave it unrhymed where it would. The originals' own rhymes "are not always satisfactory", which licenses looseness. The literal layer stays unrhymed, as Jackson's does. *(Radice; N. Sen; Jackson.)*

4. **Refrain return (dhruva).**
   - Set the second couplet as the refrain and mark it typographically, for example with an indent and a "↺" glyph.
   - Offer a *Sung view* that repeats it after each couplet.
   - Label this plainly as an editorial reconstruction based on the manuscript's "dhru" marks and Munidatta's *dhruvapadena …* formula.
   - Never write the repetitions into the literal text.

   *(N. Sen; Sukumar Sen; Ray; Hess on teka.)*

5. **Signature line (bhaṇitā).**
   - Always keep "Lui says" / "Kāṇha says" as a spoken tag.
   - Where the name also appears in the refrain couplet, keep it there too. This is Lui's model.
   - Render *bhaṇai* consistently, for example as "says", so readers hear the formula recur across the fifty songs.

   *(Sukumar Sen; N. Sen.)*

6. **Rāga as mood-heading, never melody.** Print the rāga name as the song's heading ("Paṭamañjarī"), with a one-line note in the illumination:
   - the name comes from the manuscript;
   - no tāla is given;
   - the melody is lost;
   - modern rāgas with the same name (for example kīrtan Paṭamañjarī) are later forms.

   Never map a rāga to a modern scale or to an "emotion" table as fact. *(N. Sen; onushilon; Daily Star; Widdess via Prajapati.)*

7. **Echo of an echo (sayable original).**
   - Show the IAST line beside the English, with light scansion: a caesura "‖" and optional four-mora groupings.
   - Link a one-screen pronunciation key (A7).
   - This lets a reader *voice* the Old Bengali, which is Jackson's stated reason for printing it.

   *(Jackson; N. Sen's scansion; Old Bengali phonology.)*

8. **Keep the absurd surface.** Translate enigmatic images literally and vividly, and let them be strange: the tree, the boat, the Ḍombī, the mouse, the milk. Decoding belongs in the commentary layer, never inside the poem line. Readers should feel the productive bafflement the songs intend. *(Dasgupta; Hess; Kværne.)*

9. **Spoke notes (polyvalent glossary).** For each recurring image, list *all* the readings Munidatta gives, and where, as spokes rather than a single equivalent. Optionally tag Bharati's direction: *afferent* (a concrete word pointing to the absolute) or *efferent* (a doctrinal word pointing to the body). Never present a one-to-one code. *(Hess; Kværne; Bharati.)*

10. **Low words for high truth (register rule).**
    - Use plain, bodily, contemporary English.
    - No *thee/thou*, no King James cadences, no "biblical-sounding paragraphs".
    - The songs are in the vernacular on purpose: "the highest truth in the lowest terms".

    *(Kværne; Schelling on Tagore; Jackson; Mehrotra.)*

11. **Structural mimicry.** Preserve the source's parallelisms, repetitions, questions and word-order paradoxes, even when idiomatic English would smooth them. When a line departs from the literal, a toggle can show Ramanujan-style parallelism letters over the literal gloss. *(Ramanujan, *Speaking of Śiva*.)*

12. **Four-tier ladder.** Expose four stacked views per couplet:
    1. IAST;
    2. word-by-word interlinear, drawn from the glossary links;
    3. the existing literal line;
    4. the poetic line.

    The reader can climb from sound to sense to song. *(Hess, Appendix B; Jackson's layout.)*

13. **Fixed-term lexicon across layers.** Lock English equivalents for technical terms (*citta*, *sahaja*, *mahāsukha*, *śūnya* …). Apply them in both the literal and poetic layers, so images and doctrine rhyme across songs. Vary only non-technical words for rhythm, as Hess does with *sant*. *(Jackson; Hess.)*

14. **No silent cuts, no versions of versions.**
    - The poetic layer may reorder within a couplet, but it must never drop lines, images or refrains.
    - That is Mehrotra's avowed singer's licence, which is wrong for a scholarly edition.
    - Always generate from the Old Bengali and the commentary, never from a prior English. That was Bly's error.
    - For songs 24, 25 and 48, state that the poetic layer rests on the Tibetan.

    *(Mehrotra; Hess on Bly.)*

15. **Performance frame and living voices.**
    - Headnotes should tell readers that these were sung in gatherings: the feast, and later Newar ritual with cymbal and drum.
    - Link verified modern performances (Vabnagar/Bhābānagar, Newar caryā) as *living interpretations*, not reconstructions.
    - Vabnagar's five modes are a model for offering several English "performances" of one text: literal, poetic, and sung-view.

    *(Thapa; Widdess; ICH NGO Forum; Shaw.)*
