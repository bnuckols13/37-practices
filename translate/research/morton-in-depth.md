## 1. The course as a whole

There are 19 recordings, about 24½ hours in all, uploaded by Morton within a day of each class under CC BY-NC-ND 3.0 ✔ (C1–CR metadata). Class 2's description calls it the second "in a series of 20 classes" ✔.

- **The gap.** Classes 1–12 are transcribed (about 15½ hours); 13–17, CI and CR are not (about 9 hours) (Index ◐). So the course's **narrator and narrative tools, and its one theory class, are known only by their tags.** For the engine that is the largest hole: the board measures structure and texture but only counts the narrator's calls. The other gap is the class pages for 8–11, which I did not have: every gloss below marked IDX is mine and needs checking against them.
- **Morton's order of reading.** It is fixed and it runs outward from the page (C1 ◐; already in the engine):
  - **Structure:** space, then stanza, then lineation, then punctuation and syntax;
  - **Texture:** rhythm, then rhyme;
  - **Perception:** images, their clusters, then figures and tropes;
  - **Narrator:** who speaks, and who you must become;
  - **Narrative:** plot and story, frequency and duration.

  The first paragraph of any reading is about marks and blankness (C2 ◐). Each channel gets a temperature. What the engine does not yet ask for is the last step: **patterns of patterns** (C1, IDX). Two channels are shown talking to each other, and that conversation is what Morton grades as the A range (C1 ◐). A reading is a "smart list", with no thesis and no conclusion (C1 ◐).
- **The course is taught by making.** Every channel comes with a small homework in which the student writes a hot version and a cool version, or rearranges a few words (C1, C3–C5 ◐). The final projects include writing a sonnet (C1 ◐). The Notes also list a "node" among the final projects, which is surely the transcript mis-hearing "ode".
- **How far to trust the Drive documents.** The Notes are close to the class. Even so, they carry the transcript's mis-hearings: *Om Mani Padme Hum* comes out as an English phrase (C7), and "node" stands for "ode" (C1). The Guide smooths things over and makes slips of its own. It calls *minds / finds* in Sonnet 116 an off rhyme, but it is a perfect rhyme. It gives "Ozymandias" as ABAB ABAB before a turn, but the poem runs ABABACDC EDEFEF. So check any claim from either document against the poem.

## 2. Every tool, sorted

Tags: **[IN ENGINE]** already in `how-to-read-a-song.md` or `lib/reading.mjs`; **[MEASURABLE]** can be computed from text (how, briefly; full algorithms in §5); **[EXERCISE]** a making exercise, as an instruction; **[READING]** a question a reader asks. The Index lists 192 terms across classes 1–11 (C1: 27, C2: 23, C3: 15, C4: 20, C5: 17, C6: 15, C7: 24, C8: 16, C9: 11, C10: 15, C11: 17). The important ones follow, weighted toward what the engine lacks.

### Space (C2; Morton's "faders" for space, lineation and syntax, C4)

- **Squiggles and blankness** (C2 ◐) [MEASURABLE]. The poem organises the page around it. Morton treats the blank as a thick medium, not an empty void, and ranks the prose block (Ashbery's "The System") hottest and Cummings's scatter coolest (C2 ◐). *Measure:* the **space fader** the engine lacks: ink density, the spread of line lengths, and how regular the stanzas are.
- **Bernstein's blank poem** (C1–C2 ◐) [EXERCISE]. *Print the song with its refrain lines left blank but their space kept. Read the gaps aloud as silences of the same length.*
- **The re-mark** (C2, IDX; *Ecology without Nature* ◐) [MEASURABLE]. A re-mark is the extra mark that tells you these are marks: a speech bubble drawn round squiggles (C2 ◐). In a caryā, the double daṇḍa and the ধ্রু sign are re-marks. *Measure:* count the non-verbal marks (numbers, refrain signs, dashes, indents), source and English side by side.
- **Repetition as death: hot and cold space** (C2 ◐; in print in Glossator ✔, see §3) [READING]. Hot space tends toward the regular and the dead; cool space toward the irregular and the living. Ask of the page: *which way does its shape lean?*
- **Metaphor and paraphrase** (C2, IDX) [READING]. In the Notes, a paraphrase is a metaphor that pretends not to be one (C2 ◐). For a translator this is the point: **every accurate English is a metaphor of its song.** Ask: *what does this paraphrase claim the song is like?*
- **Two words, three ways** (C1 homework ◐) [EXERCISE]. *Take two words from the song, e.g. "row" and "Ḍombī". Set them on the page three ways: touching, on two lines, far apart. In two lines each, write what each arrangement does.*

### Lineation (C1, C3; enjambment C2, C4, C10)

- **Hot and cool lineation; consistency and inconsistency** (C3) [IN ENGINE].
- **Quantum coherence and the qubit** (C3 ◐) [READING]. A cool arrangement holds two readings at once. "horse / foot" is both one thing and two (C3 ◐). A hot arrangement collapses to one reading. Morton: "if you're cool enough, you occupy more than one place at one time" (C3 ◐). Ask: *does this break keep two readings alive?*
- **Amplitude, frequency and cadence** (C3 ◐) [MEASURABLE]. Morton borrows the vocabulary of waves. *Measure:* frequency = how often a line shape recurs; amplitude = how far a line's length strays from the groove.
- **Caesura, "a cut"** (C3 ◐) [MEASURABLE]. *Measure:* where the mid-line pauses fall. A pause that always falls mid-line is hot. This is the bridge to the Japanese cutting word (§4b).
- **The present as a blank** (C3, IDX) [READING]. In "Easter Wings" the thinnest line is where the poem dies and turns (C3 ◐). Ask: *where is the poem's narrowest point, and what happens there?*
- **Reveal it line by line** (C3 ◐) [EXERCISE]. *Cover the poem with a sheet and draw it down one line at a time, saying what you expect before each line.* The engine's "blank sheet" (exercise 4) only looks at the whole page. This is the half it lacks: the poem happening in time.
- **Reading in and reading out; no symbolism** (C3) [IN ENGINE]. In the Notes the rule is stated flatly: nothing symbolizes anything (C3 ◐).
- **Enjambment and end-stopping** (C2, C4) [IN ENGINE] (share of end-stopped lines).
- **Enjambment that hesitates** (C10, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. Our gloss: a break that falls inside a phrase (after "the", "of", "my") makes the voice hang over the gap. *Measure:* in a line that is not end-stopped, is the last word a light word? Hesitant breaks cool the lineation.

### Syntax (C1, C4)

- **The phrase as the atom; parataxis and hypotaxis** (C4) [IN ENGINE].
- **Signs of parataxis** (C4) [MEASURABLE]. The signs are *and*, commas and semicolons. The semicolon leans slightly cool, a pair that is almost two sentences (C4 ◐). *Refinement:* count a semicolon as half a paratactic join.
- **Meta-parallel; parallel lineation and parataxis** (C4, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. When syntax and lineation line up, the heat doubles: Blake's "Lamb". When they cross, you get conversation: Williams (C4 ◐). *Measure:* the share of line ends that are also clause ends.
- **Icy fire** (C4) [READING]. Opposite temperatures side by side, Petrarch's oxymoron. The Notes find it in Williams's close, sweet and cold at once (C4 ◐). Ask: *where do opposites touch?*
- **Men … de** (C4) [READING]. The Greek balancing pair, "on the one hand … on the other": the built, cool period. Ask: *is any sentence balanced on a hinge?*
- **Aperture** (C4 ◐) [READING]. Beginning is a feeling, not a position. Ask: *how long does the poem take to begin?*

### Rhythm (C5, C6, C7)

- **Stress, groove and deviation; catalexis; English as a creole; four, five and seven beats; mouthfeel** (C5) [IN ENGINE].
- **Beat as a relationship; relative stress** (C5, C7 ◐) [IN ENGINE]. `scan` lets runs of strong words settle.
- **Pyrrhic and spondee** (C6) [MEASURABLE]. *Measure:* report the "xx" and "//" pairs inside a pentameter.
- **Pentameter, the metre of thinking** (C5) [MEASURABLE]. The engine says it but does not measure it. *Measure:* how regular a pentameter runs (§5).
- **Top-down scansion: "too high an altitude"** (C6, IDX) [READING]. Scan up from the voice, not down from a template. The engine's dictionary-first scan already works this way.
- **Rhythm as atmosphere, the "vibe"** (C6 ◐) [READING]. This is the classroom form of the ambient poetics of *tone* (§3). Ask: *what weather is this poem?*
- **Free verse: rhythm without a metrical plan** (C6 ◐) [IN ENGINE]. The groove is found statistically across lines (the mode of the beat counts).
- **The poem as dictator; coexistence, "the enjoyment of the other"** (C6 ◐) [IN ENGINE as prose] → [EXERCISE] below. The poem commandeers your breath, jaw and lungs. Reading is agreeing to live beside someone else's pleasure (C6 ◐).
- **Whelming** (C5) [IN ENGINE as prose] → [MEASURABLE] (a whelm check, §5).
- **Horror** (C6) [READING]. Texture that makes the flesh creep, the bodily ground of para rhyme. Ask: *where does the poem make your skin react before you understand it?*

### Rhyme (C1, C7, C8)

- **The solar system; absolute, perfect, vowel, off, para; eye rhyme; masculine and feminine** (C7) [IN ENGINE].
- **The galaxy of assonance; subtle alliteration** (C7, C8 ◐) [MEASURABLE]. Rhyme is only one region of all sound-likeness. *Measure:* the share of stressed vowels in a line or couplet that recur.
- **Internal rhyme** (C1, C8) [MEASURABLE]. *Measure:* run `rhymeWords` on every pair of stressed words inside a line and across adjacent lines, not only on the ends.
- **Rhyme performs thought; the rhyme scheme; the sonnet as a movement of thought; the turn** (C7) [IN ENGINE as prose] → [MEASURABLE]. The board pairs lines by the caryā's couplets, so **a sonnet's ABAB reads as unrhymed.** *Measure:* letter the scheme against every earlier end, and find the turn (§5).
- **The heat of a rhyme scheme** (C8, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. *Measure:* lines per rhyme sound. The Italian octave spends 8 lines on 2 sounds; the English sonnet spends 14 on 7. Also measure the heat of each quatrain, to see where a scheme cools. Which scheme Morton calls hotter is on the class 8 page, which I have not seen.
- **Laying rhyme off; "the bondage of rhyme"** (C8, IDX) [EXERCISE]. The phrase is Milton's, from the note "The Verse" added to *Paradise Lost* (1668), which frees heroic verse from "the troublesom and modern bondage of Rimeing" ✔ ([Dartmouth Milton Reading Room](https://milton.host.dartmouth.edu/reading_room/pl/note/text.shtml)). *Rhyme a couplet, then lay the rhyme off and pay the heat back somewhere else: a struck word, a firmer beat.*
- **Sound memory; sound sequence and modulation** (C8, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. Our gloss: how far back the ear still holds a sound. *Measure:* the distance in lines from a sound to its return: 1 in couplets, 2 in quatrains and terza rima, up to 3 in an Italian octave.
- **Terza rima; the ballad stanza** (C8) [MEASURABLE]. Detect the forms: ABA BCB CDC chains forward; 4·3·4·3 beats rhymed xaxa.
- **Noise and sound; the prepared piano; the drone** (C7 ◐) [READING]. In the Notes, Cage's prepared piano is para rhyme and La Monte Young's hour-long drone is absolute rhyme (C7 ◐). Ask: *which planet is this couplet's music?*
- **Mad libs: hot and cool as an algorithm** (C7, IDX) [EXERCISE]. *Keep a poem's frame of repeated words and fill its blanks with new words three times. What keeps its heat is the frame.*
- ***Om Mani Padme Hum*: the mantra as the sun** (C7; Index ✔ that it was read, Notes ◐) [IN ENGINE as an example]. Deeper in §4d.

### Perception: imagery (C1, C9–C12)

- **Sensory forcing; images in order; keep the strange strange** [IN ENGINE].
- **Whether the image is on or off; quantized imagery; quasi-on** (C9, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. Our gloss: an image is summoned or it is not. Some images are only half summoned: an image inside a simile, a question, a wish, a conditional or a negation. *Measure:* a strip of states for each line, ● on, ◐ quasi-on, ○ off.
- **Hot imagery; the rhythm of imagery** (C9, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. *Measure:* images per line, and how steady the count is. One image to a line (*Cathay*) is hot.
- **Emptiness** (C9, IDX) [READING]. In print Morton defines emptiness as "not the absence of something, but the nonconceptuality of reality" (*Realist Magic* p. 74 ✔). Ask: *is the empty place in this poem a hole, or an openness with no concept attached?*
- **Negative imagery: positive and negative present imagery; negation as the test** (C1, C10, C11, IDX; the Notes' version of C1 ◐) [MEASURABLE]. The image you are told not to picture is pictured anyway: "don't think of a pink elephant" (C1 ◐). The man "who wasn't there" of "Antigonish", read in classes 9 and 10, is the classic case. *Measure:* the images inside a negator's scope.
- **Apophasis; negative theology (via negativa); occupatio; the privative prefix** (C1, C10) [MEASURABLE] and [EXERCISE]. Class 10 read *The Cloud of Unknowing* and the hymn "Immortal, Invisible, God Only Wise" ✔ (Index). The hymn's praise is made of privatives, im-, in-, un- (our observation). Occupatio is saying a thing by saying you will not say it. *Measure:* negators, privatives, and "not / never + say, tell, speak, name".
- **Subjunctive and indicative** (C10, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. Mood switches an image half on. This feeds the on/off strip.
- **Image clusters** (C10) [MEASURABLE]. *Measure:* group the marked images by field (water, fire, body, sky) using the glossary's tags.
- **Brightness; brightness and contrast** (C10, C11, IDX) [MEASURABLE, roughly] and [READING]. Morton's printed source is Longinus's *brilliance* (to ekphanestaton) in *Realist Magic* pp. 132–33 ✔. There, brilliance is what hides an object, as the sun hides dim lights. Ask: *what is the brightest thing in the poem, and what does its light hide?*
- **Ekphrasis** (C11) [EXERCISE]. In print, ekphrasis is "a bristling vividness that interrupts the flow of the narrative" (*Realist Magic* p. 133 ✔). It suspends time like the bullet time of *The Matrix*, which class 11 also brings up ✔ (Index). *Describe one object (a manuscript leaf, a boat, a skull cup) so closely that the song stops moving for two lines.*
- **Imagery and time** (C11, IDX) [READING]. An image takes time to arrive. Morton reads Hopkins's "tumbled over rim in roundy wells" as an adjective that lasts as long as the stone's fall (*Realist Magic* pp. 26–27 ✔). Ask: *how long does this image take to appear?*
- **Under erasure; scratching** (C11, IDX) [READING]. An image crossed out and still visible. Ask: *what is cancelled here but still seen?*
- ***Obscurum per obscurius*; the law of non-contradiction** (C11, IDX) [READING]. Explaining the dark by the darker, which is the riddle's move. And images that are A and not-A at once: Morton's "dialetheia" (*Realist Magic* p. 73 ✔). Ask: *does the image explain, or deepen, what it answers?*

### Tropes (C9, C11)

- **The trope as an algorithm, "spin"** (C11, IDX) [EXERCISE] and [MEASURABLE]. Class 11 read Prusinkiewicz and Lindenmayer's *The Algorithmic Beauty of Plants* ✔ (Index), the book of rewriting rules that grow a plant. Our gloss: a trope is a turn (*tropos*) that can be run as a rule. *Write one metaphor as a rule ("every X becomes a kind of boat") and run it over five lines.*
- **Hot metaphor, cool metonymy** (C11, IDX; with "metaphor and metonymy in Jakobson and Lacan", C11) [MEASURABLE, roughly]. The theory is Jakobson's two poles (§3): metaphor joins by likeness (selection), metonymy by nearness (combination). Likeness repeats a form, so it runs hot; nearness runs along a chain, so it runs cool. *Measure:* markers only. Similes are marked by *like*, *as*, *as if*; metaphors by "X is Y"; apostrophe by *O* + a name.
- **Metaphor as *translatio*; simile; epic simile; synecdoche** (C11) [READING]. *Translatio*, "carrying across", is the Latin for metaphor and later for translation. Ask the translator: *what did this English carry across, and what did it leave on the bank?*
- **Tropes as the flowers of rhetoric** (C9, IDX) [READING]. An "anthology" is a gathering of flowers.
- **"Stealing eggs"** (C8–C9). Unglossed: a gap.

### Narrator (C1, C4, C6, C8; C13–14 untranscribed)

- **Point of view and subject position** (C1) [IN ENGINE, partly]. The engine counts calls, commands and the bhaṇitā.
- **Lyric, "the genre of the other mind"** (C8, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. The Guide's version: lyric shows a mind through its pronouns ◐. *Measure:* the pronoun profile, line by line (I/me, you/thou, he/she, we, it), and where it shifts.
- **Coexistence** (C6 ◐) [EXERCISE]. *Read aloud, whole and at its own pace, a poem you dislike. Then write down only what it made your mouth and breath do.*
- **Innocence** (C4 ◐) [READING]. *In-nocens*, "harmless": the speaker who cannot be accused. Ask: *who in this poem cannot be blamed, and what does that let them say?*
- **Quotation marks; the reaction shot** (C4, C8) [READING]. Ask: *whose words are these, and whose face do we watch as they are said?*
- **Ideology; subject position** (C14 tags) UNVERIFIED: not transcribed.

### Narrative and genre (C1, C4, C7, C8, C10; C15–16 untranscribed)

- **The turn from A to not-A; genre as a horizon of expectation** (C7) [IN ENGINE].
- **Negation, the minimal unit of thinking** (C8, IDX) [READING]. Our gloss: the turn *is* a negation. Ask: *what does this poem say no to, and where?*
- **Story and plot; frequency and duration** (C1, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. These are Genette's terms. Frequency is how many times an event is told. A refrain tells the same thing again and again, the repetitive frequency. *Measure:* count repeated lines and half-lines as narrative frequency.
- **Backwards causation: "happening twice"** (C10, IDX) [READING]. In print: "to happen, it has to happen twice" (*Realist Magic* p. 91 ✔). Ask: *which image becomes an event only when it comes back?*
- **Resolution and closure; aperture** (C4, C8) [MEASURABLE]. *Measure:* does the last line close? Count an end-stop, a rhyme and the refrain's return.
- **Lyric, epic, romance; genre and form** (C7 ◐). The sonnet's form is 14 lines; its genre is a movement of thought. **Epigram, epitaph, epigraph** (C8); **elegy and eulogy** (C10); **aubade** (C4) [MEASURABLE, for form only]. Detect sonnet, ballad stanza, terza rima, ghazal and *rubāʿī* from the line count and the scheme.

## 3. The theory under it

### Jakobson: where "hot" comes from

- **The formula.** "The poetic function projects the principle of equivalence from the axis of selection into the axis of combination" (Jakobson, "Linguistics and Poetics", in Sebeok, ed., *Style in Language*, MIT Press 1960, pp. 350–77, at p. 358) ◐. Nigel Fabb gives the page ([Strathclyde](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/16673/)). Richard Clarke's lecture notes give the same wording from a reprint. Two independent quotations agree, but I did not see the original.
- **What it means.** In ordinary speech we choose one word from a set of equivalents (*child, kid, youngster*) and move on. Poetry keeps choosing from the same set along the line. Jakobson spells out what gets matched: "Syllables are converted into units of measure, and so are morae or stresses" ◐ (via Clarke). Stress equals stress, pause equals pause, sound equals sound.
- **So hot is Jakobson's equivalence turned up** (our reading). Absolute rhyme, the refrain and the mantra are equivalence at its maximum. Cool is combination left to itself: contiguity, the next thing and then the next. Read this way, Morton's two poles are Jakobson's two axes: **hot is selection laid on the line, cool is the line left free.** It also means the engine's mātrā count for Old Bengali and its stress count for English are one measure, not two: Jakobson names morae beside stresses.
- **Metaphor and metonymy.** In the same essay, where likeness is laid on nearness, "any metonymy is slightly metaphorical and any metaphor has a metonymical tint" ◐ (via Clarke). The two poles come from Jakobson's 1956 essay on aphasia (in *Fundamentals of Language*), which I did not read: UNVERIFIED at source. Class 11's "hot metaphor, cool metonymy" is this theory in Morton's temperatures.
- **What it adds to translating.** Jakobson makes rhyme and metre *semantic*. Sound equivalence pulls meanings together, so a translation that keeps the sense and drops the sound-links has cut ties the poem made between its words. The engine's rule of **temperature matching** is Jakobson's point put as a practice.

### "An Object-Oriented Defense of Poetry" (*New Literary History* 43.2, 2012, pp. 205–24; [doi:10.1353/nlh.2012.0018](https://doi.org/10.1353/nlh.2012.0018))

- **What I could verify.** The bibliographic details ✔ (Crossref; Morton's CV). One sentence at p. 222, through Matthew J. Dodson's MA thesis (Oregon State, 2014, [PDF](https://ir.library.oregonstate.edu/downloads/h128nh407)) ◐: to write poetry is to "force the reader to coexist with fragile phrases, fragile ink, fragile paper". Dodson also reports that Morton turns Shelley's *Defence of Poetry* (quoted at p. 216) toward the physical surface of things ◐. The article sits in one issue with Graham Harman's "The Well-Wrought Broken Hammer" and Jane Bennett's response ◐.
- **Its argument, from Morton's book-length version.** *Realist Magic* (Open Humanities Press, 2013, [open access](https://www.openhumanitiespress.org/books/titles/realist-magic/)) ✔ develops the same ideas:
  - "I argue that causality is wholly an aesthetic phenomenon" (p. 19);
  - "The aesthetic dimension is the causal dimension" (p. 20);
  - art is therefore the study of how things affect each other, not decoration on top of them (pp. 19–20);
  - Shelley's Aeolian lyre (pp. 91–92, 209) is the model of reading: the wind is heard only as the strings modulate it;
  - "What makes a poem a poem is that we don't know what it means yet" (p. 209, glossing Shelley);
  - "I make the poem real for me by misreading it" (p. 208).
- **What it adds to reading.** Read in these terms, the course's claims are not metaphors. A poem really does act on your jaw and lungs (C6). Form really is the thing, and there is no content underneath (C1 ◐). Reading is attunement to an object that stays partly withdrawn.
- **What it adds to translating.** A translation is one object's reading of another, a "translation" in Morton's technical sense (*Realist Magic* pp. 110, 133 ✔: the frog's croak becomes *brekekekex*; ekphrasis "generates its own kind of object"). No version exhausts the song, and each is a new thing with its own causal force. That is the ground for printing several Englishes side by side. It also grounds the rule to keep the strange strange: settling an image's meaning is a measurement that ends its many states (compare the quantum passage, *Realist Magic* p. 209 ✔, with C3 ◐).

### The ambient poetics of *Ecology without Nature* (Harvard UP, 2007)

- **The list, confirmed as six:** rendering, the medial, the timbral, the Aeolian, tone and the re-mark ◐. It is listed at p. 34 and discussed at pp. 35–49. Two secondary sources agree: Chaiyon Tongsukkaeng's PhD thesis (Sheffield, 2015, [White Rose](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/11616/)) and a [2017 blog summary](https://headforthecountry.wordpress.com/2017/03/30/ambient-poetics-in-timothy-mortons-ecology-without-nature/) quoting Morton with page numbers. Morton used the term earlier in "Why Ambient Poetics?" (*The Wordsworth Circle* 33.1, 2002) and "'Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star' as an Ambient Poem" (*Romantic Praxis*, 2001) ✔ (Morton's CV). So the course's first poem is one Morton had already read as an ambient poem.
- **Each element, and where it shows up in class** (glosses from the secondary sources ◐; the links to classes are ours):
  - **Rendering** (p. 35): art that makes you feel you are *in* a real place. In class: the space around the poem, "objects in mirror are closer than they appear" (C3).
  - **The medial** (p. 37): writing that shows the page or the channel it is on. In class: squiggles, the paper, the blank (C2).
  - **The timbral** (p. 39): sound in its body rather than its meaning. In class: mouthfeel, the colour of rhyme (C5, C7).
  - **The Aeolian** (p. 41): sound with "no obvious source", processes going on without a speaker. In class: rhythm as weather (C6). This is the bridge to the Aeolian lyre in *Realist Magic*.
  - **Tone** (pp. 43–44): what "accounts materially for that slippery word *atmosphere*", in body and environment at once. In class: the "vibe" (C6).
  - **The re-mark** (pp. 48–49): the mark that makes us notice marks, background turned to foreground. In class: C2's term, taken from Derrida.
- **What it adds.** Ambient poetics is the course's theory of **space and texture**, the channels that come before meaning. For translation it names what a gloss cannot carry: the page, the timbre, the weather of a song. The caryā pulls against the Aeolian: where Aeolian sound has no source, the bhaṇitā names its singer, a re-mark that signs the song as made.

## 4. Bridges for the other research notes

### (a) Sufi poetry

- **The *radīf* is absolute rhyme after perfect rhyme.** In every couplet a changing rhyme word (*qāfiya*) is followed by the same word or phrase. So the ghazal strikes Morton's sun and an inner planet together, every time. Its couplets stand apart in sense. That makes the ghazal Morton's Baked Alaska: the hottest rhyme over the coolest narrative. The engine can find it: see *radīf* in §5.
- ***Dhikr* and the mantra.** The repeated divine name is the mantra's pole. Morton's printed account of that pole is Buddhist, not Sufi: "Repetition is a feature of the death drive. Mantras are viral clones of sacred words, uttered millions of times" (*Glossator* 7, p. 88 ✔). The Sufi note should decide whether "passing away" (*fanāʾ*) meets that reading. It is a question, not a finding.
- **Apophasis.** Michael Sells, *Mystical Languages of Unsaying* (University of Chicago Press, 1994) ✔ (archive.org catalogue record). There, apophasis "literally means 'speaking away'": discourse that keeps turning back on its own names. It has a chapter on Ibn ʿArabī ("Identity Shift and Meaning Event") ◐. Morton teaches the same move in classes 1 and 10 as negative imagery and the via negativa. In print Morton ties apophatic negation to Nāgārjuna: "negation is the work of death, peeling away and rejecting, spitting out" (*Glossator* 7, p. 84 ✔). Rumi signing himself *Khāmūsh*, "Silent" (per POETRY-PLAN), is an apophatic signature. Measure it with the negative-imagery tools.
- **Morton's own caution.** Morton names the risk of "the perilous edge of New Age syncretism", the idea that "Sufi and Tibetan yogi speak alike" (*Glossator* 7, p. 74 ✔). The comparison note should compare *devices*, as this study does, and not claim one experience behind them.

### (b) Zen poetry

- **Emptiness is not a negative image.** Morton's emptiness is nonconceptuality, not absence (*Realist Magic* p. 74 ✔). Wang Wei's "empty mountain" can therefore be read two ways. It is a *negative* image ("no one is seen": you picture the person who isn't there, as in "Antigonish"). It is also an *empty* one (the mountain with no concept on it). The Zen note should keep both, and our on/off strip can show where each falls.
- **The cut.** Morton's caesura is literally "a cut" (C3 ◐), and "the present as a blank" (C3) is a reading of a gap in time. The *kireji* is a caesura made into a word, the cut that switches from one image to another. *Measure:* caesura map, dash, the image strip either side of it.
- **Bashō's old pond** (C11, Index ✔). What Morton said about it is in the untranscribed class page: UNVERIFIED. Morton's printed nearest neighbour is the opening of *Realist Magic*'s "Magic Birth" chapter (p. 110 ✔). There a pond full of croaking frogs is heard, and the croak is "translated" into *brekekekex*: a nonhuman sound becoming words. The sound of the water in Bashō's poem can be read by that light: *imagery and time* (C11), an image switched on after a long "off".
- **Becoming like a stone.** Morton writes that in meditation "one really does become like a stone, as is said in the Zen meditation manuals", citing D. T. Suzuki (*Glossator* 7, p. 83 ✔). Useful for Hanshan's rock-written poems, as a reading, not a claim of influence.
- **The parallel couplet** is Jakobson's equivalence at work, and Morton's *parallel lineation and parataxis* (C4). Measure it as parallelism between two half-lines (§5).

### (c) Shakespeare's sonnets (read against the public-domain text; the observations are ours unless marked)

- **The two sonnets Morton pairs in class 7 are both built on negation.**
  - **Sonnet 116** defines love by what it is not. It has nine negators and a privative ("unknown") in fourteen lines: "Let me *not*", "Love is *not* love", "O *no*!", "*never* shaken", "Love's *not* Time's fool", "alters *not*", "I *never* writ, *nor no* man". It is apophasis in a love poem.
  - **Sonnet 130** praises by denial: "*nothing* like the sun", "*no* such roses", "I *never* saw a goddess go". Every blazon image (sun, coral, snow, wires, roses, perfume, music, goddess) is switched on in order to be switched off.
  - That class 10 (negative imagery) returns to 130 ✔ (Index) suggests Morton saw this; the class page will say.
- **The heat of the scheme.**
  - **130** rhymes perfectly throughout: sun/dun, red/head, white/delight, cheeks/reeks, know/go, sound/ground, rare/compare. That is hot rhyme carrying negative images, a Baked Alaska.
  - **116** cools exactly on its "love" sounds: love/remove, come/doom and the closing proved/loved are eye or off rhymes. Its other rhymes are perfect: minds/finds, mark/bark, cheeks/weeks, and the feminine shaken/taken. The Notes report Morton hearing the couplet's warmth as only apparent (C7 ◐). 116's heat moves inside the line instead: "love … love", "alters … alteration", "remover … remove".
- **The turn.**
  - 130 turns at "And yet" (line 13): the English sonnet's trip.
  - **Sonnet 29**, the plan's centrepiece, turns twice: at "Yet" (line 9, the Italian place) and at the couplet.
  - Lettered against every earlier end, 29 is not ABAB CDCD EFEF GG but **ABAB CDCD EBEB FF**. "State" ends lines 2 and 10, an absolute rhyme joining the first quatrain to the third. The first "state" rhymes with *fate*, the second with heaven's *gate*: the same word, rhymed first with doom and then with heaven, is the poem's whole movement (our reading). The engine should letter schemes this way.
- **The sonnet as a movement of thought** (C7 ◐): from A to not-A. With class 8's "negation, the minimal unit of thinking" (IDX), the turn is a negation. That is why 116 and 130, poems made of negation, suit the lesson.

### (d) The Charyapada

- **Twilight language against "no symbolism."**
  - Morton bans reading symbols *in* (C3 ◐). The caryās are made of images the tradition reads as codes (see [`charyapada-sound.md`](charyapada-sound.md) B8).
  - The edition's resolution is already Morton-compatible: the image works first as an image, "on" in the line, and Munidatta's reading is a re-mark printed beside it.
  - In Morton's model, a cool image holds several states at once, and decoding it is a measurement that ends them (C3 ◐; *Realist Magic* p. 209 ✔). Hess's "charged pointer hub" (charyapada-sound.md B8) is the same structure seen from the tradition's side.
- **Morton's own reading of a mantra.**
  - In class 7 *Om Mani Padme Hum* is the sun of the solar system, absolute rhyme (Index ✔; the Notes ◐).
  - In print, mantra is repetition in the service of the death drive, recited 1.2 million times in the Vajrayāna preliminaries (*Glossator* 7, p. 88 ✔). Morton reads Freud's "Nirvana principle", the drive toward quiescence, as possibly more than a metaphor (pp. 78–79 ✔).
  - So in Morton's own vocabulary the ধ্রু refrain's heat is the song leaning toward quiescence. **For a song whose goal is nirvāṇa, "hot" is not a fault to cool down.**
- **The charnel ground.** Morton calls it the "favored spot of tantric yogis" (*Glossator* 7, p. 83 ✔). The Dzogchen yogi, Morton writes, finds it intimate rather than repellent (p. 88 ✔). That is a reading for the caryās' cremation grounds, and a check on the Owen lens: the cold para rhyme may be the wrong temperature where the song means intimacy.
- **Commentary.** Morton's essay is about auto-commentary: Dzogchen liturgies that carry their own gloss inside the text (*Glossator* 7, pp. 74–76 ✔). The caryā is arranged the other way: the song, then Munidatta's separate Sanskrit commentary, with the bhaṇitā as the song's only self-reference. The edition's layout (song, then notes) follows the caryā's arrangement, not the liturgy's.
- **Lineage, carefully.** Morton writes as a practitioner in the Tibetan Mahāmudrā and Dzogchen world. The essay cites Chögyam Trungpa's *Sadhana of Mahamudra*, whose title invokes "the Ocean of Siddhas of the Practice Lineage" ✔ (p. 75 n.7). That world traces itself to the Indian siddhas ◐ (not checked here). No Morton text on the caryās themselves was found.

## 5. What it gives the practice

### (i) New measurements for `lib/reading.mjs` and `lib/english.mjs`, ranked

1. **Rhyme-scheme lettering.** Give each line end a letter, testing it against *all* earlier ends with `rhymeWords`: off rhyme or hotter shares the letter; para and eye rhymes are marked. Name the form: couplets, sonnet, ghazal AA BA CA, *rubāʿī* AABA, terza rima. Report heat per quatrain. This is the precondition for any sonnet or ghazal reading. Today the board pairs caryā couplets only.
2. **Radīf and absolute rhyme.** Take the longest common word-suffix of the even lines (or of all lines). If it is at least one word, recurs three or more times, and the rhyme words before it differ, it is a *radīf*. Also list every word that ends two lines (Sonnet 29's "state").
3. **The turn.** For each line boundary, score +2 if the next line opens with *but, yet, and yet, so, then, now, thus, still, O*; +1 at a scheme-block boundary (8|6, 12|2); +1 if the negator density or the pronoun set changes across it. Report the top boundary as "Italian (9)", "English (13)" or "early".
4. **The space fader** (a fifth channel, as in Morton's C4 faders). Compute space = 0.4·(1 − spread of characters per line) + 0.3·(stanza sizes all equal) + 0.3·(ink ÷ (lines × longest line)). Read it from the text as laid out, keeping indents and blank lines. Add `space` to `CHANNELS` and to each lens's board.
5. **Negative imagery.** Mark negators (*not, no, nor, never, none, nothing, nobody, nowhere, without, neither, -n't*) and privatives (*un-, in-/im-, -less* on a dictionary base). A negator's scope runs to the next punctuation. Negative images are marked things or nouns inside a scope. Flag occupatio (*not/never* + *say, tell, speak, name, mention*). For the source, count the same in the gloss.
6. **On / off / quasi-on strip.** Each image is *off* in a negator's scope; *quasi-on* after *like, as, as if*, in a question, after *if, would, might, could, were*, or after a wish verb; otherwise *on*. Print it as ●◐○ per line.
7. **Parallelism between half-lines or lines.** Compute 0.4·(1 − edit distance of the two `scan` patterns ÷ the longer) + 0.3·(share of words in common) + 0.3·(same first word or same last word). Serves the Psalm lens, the Chinese couplet and the caryā's two halves.
8. **Enjambment that hesitates.** A line that is not end-stopped hesitates if its last word is in `LIGHT` (article, preposition, possessive, conjunction, auxiliary). Each hesitation lowers lineation heat by a small step. List the lines.
9. **Caesura map.** Record the positions of mid-line , ; : — – . ? ! and runs of spaces, as a fraction of the line's length. Caesura heat = 1 − spread of the positions.
10. **Pentameter regularity.** Take the share of five-beat lines, and the distance of each `scan` pattern from x/x/x/x/x/, allowing an opening inversion. Report the "//" (spondee) and "xx" (pyrrhic) pairs.
11. **Internal rhyme and assonance.** Run `rhymeWords` on pairs of stressed words within a line and across adjacent lines. Measure assonance as the share of stressed vowels that recur in a line or couplet.
12. **Images: rhythm, clusters, brightness.** Count images per line and their steadiness. Group them by glossary field. Mark the brightest line with a small lexicon of light, fire, colour and shining. This one is rough and should be labelled so.
13. **Trope markers.** Count simile (*like, as … as, as if*), copular metaphor ("X is Y", both nouns) and apostrophe (*O* + name). Counts only; no verdicts.
14. **Whelm check.** Four or more channels at 0.85 or above: "may overwhelm". Every channel at 0.25 or below: "may underwhelm". Otherwise the board says nothing.

### (ii) Exercises for a Practice room (each a short, repeatable instruction)

1. **Two words, three ways.** Two words from the song, three arrangements, two lines on each.
2. **Reveal it line by line.** Cover the poem and draw the sheet down a line at a time, saying what you expect before each line.
3. **Say it without it.** Describe a thing in four lines using only what it is not (Sonnet 130, the hymn of privatives). Then write it positively and ask which image is brighter.
4. **Occupatio.** Praise something in four lines by refusing to speak of it.
5. **Switch an image on and off.** Write one image three times: indicative (on), inside *as if* or *might* (half on), negated (off).
6. **The rhyme orbit.** Close one couplet six ways: absolute, perfect, vowel, off, para, alliteration only. Choose one and say why.
7. **Lay the rhyme off.** Take the rhyme out of a rhymed couplet and pay the heat back with a struck word or a firmer beat.
8. **Radīf.** Five couplets ending on the same word or phrase, with a changing rhyme just before it.
9. **Fourteen lines that turn.** Write a thought that goes from A to not-A at line 9, then write it again turning at line 13. Say how the thinking changed.
10. **Hesitate.** Break one sentence at its most hesitant point (after "the") and at its least (after a full clause). Read both aloud.
11. **The cut.** Two images in three lines with one hard cut (a dash) between them. Move the cut and read again.
12. **Run the trope.** Write one metaphor as a rule and let it generate five lines.
13. **Ekphrasis.** Describe one object from the song so closely that the song stops for two lines.
14. **Mad libs.** Keep a poem's frame of repeated words and refill it three times. Keep whichever keeps the heat.
15. **Coexist.** Read aloud, whole, a poem you dislike. Note only what it made your breath and jaw do.
16. **One channel per draft** (the maker's own method, POETRY-PLAN §6). Revise only the space, then only the rhythm, and so on.

### (iii) Questions for a Study room

- **Space:** Where is the blank, and what does the page do around the poem? Which marks tell you these are marks?
- **Lineation:** Where is the narrowest point? Does any break keep two readings alive, or make the voice hesitate?
- **Syntax:** Where do the line ends and clause ends coincide, and where do they cross?
- **Rhythm:** What weather is this poem? Does it whelm you, under, over or just right?
- **Rhyme:** Where does the scheme cool, and what does it cool before?
- **Imagery:**
  - Which images are on, which half-on, which switched on only to be switched off?
  - What is the brightest thing, and what does its light hide?
  - How long does the key image take to arrive?
  - Is the empty place a hole or an openness?
- **Tropes:** If you ran this metaphor as a rule, what would it make next? Is the poem joining things by likeness or by nearness?
- **Narrator:** Whose mind is this, and what must you become to say it aloud? Can you live beside its pleasure?
- **Narrative:** Where does it say no (A to not-A)? Which image only becomes an event when it comes back?
- **For a translation:** Which channel did the English trade, and for which? What did its metaphor of the song carry across, and what did it leave on the bank?
