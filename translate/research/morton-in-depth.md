Class 2's description calls it the second "in a series of 20 classes" ✔.

- **The gap, now mostly closed.** Classes 1–12 were transcribed first (about 15½ hours, Index ◐). The second half is now transcribed too, privately: classes 13–17, CI and CR, about 9 hours (see *Transcripts* above). So the **narrator and narrative tools and the one theory class are no longer known only by their tags**: §2b sets them out. Two things remain open. The small model mis-hears names and verse, so the verse Morton reads aloud needs the same check. Second, the class pages for 8–11 are still missing, so every gloss below marked IDX is mine and needs checking against them. For the engine the hole has moved: the course's narrator and narrative tools are now known, but the board still counts only the narrator's calls, commands, persons and turn (§5 (i), 15–23).
- **Morton's order of reading.** It is fixed and it runs outward from the page (C1 ◐; already in the engine):
  - **Structure:** space, then stanza, then lineation, then punctuation and syntax;
  - **Texture:** rhythm, then rhyme;
  - **Perception:** images, their clusters, then figures and tropes;
  - **Narrator:** who speaks, and who you must become;
  - **Narrative:** plot and story, frequency and duration.

  In the second half Morton renames space **physical architecture**, and says narration comes last because it emerges from the first three levels (CI 00:16:09; C16 01:06:12; §2b). The order inside structure is looser in class than in C1: at C16 01:06:12 syntax comes before lineation. The first paragraph of any reading is about marks and blankness (C2 ◐). Each channel gets a temperature. What the engine does not yet ask for is the last step: **patterns of patterns** (C1, IDX; stated in class at C13 00:29:13 ◐, §2b). Two channels are shown talking to each other, and that conversation is what Morton grades as the A range (C1 ◐). A reading is a "smart list", with no thesis and no conclusion (C1 ◐).
- **The course is taught by making.** Every channel comes with a small homework in which the student writes a hot version and a cool version, or rearranges a few words (C1, C3–C5 ◐). The final projects include writing a sonnet (C1 ◐). The Notes also list a "node" among the final projects, which is surely the transcript mis-hearing "ode".
- **How far to trust the Drive documents.** The Notes are close to the class. Even so, they carry the transcript's mis-hearings: *Om Mani Padme Hum* comes out as an English phrase (C7), and "node" stands for "ode" (C1). The Guide smooths things over and makes slips of its own. It calls *minds / finds* in Sonnet 116 an off rhyme, but it is a perfect rhyme. It gives "Ozymandias" as ABAB ABAB before a turn, but the poem runs ABABACDC EDEFEF. So check any claim from either document against the poem.

## 2. Every tool, sorted

Tags: **[IN ENGINE]** already in `how-to-read-a-song.md` or `lib/reading.mjs`; **[MEASURABLE]** can be computed from text (how, briefly; full algorithms in §5); **[EXERCISE]** a making exercise, as an instruction; **[READING]** a question a reader asks. The Index lists 192 terms across classes 1–11 (C1: 27, C2: 23, C3: 15, C4: 20, C5: 17, C6: 15, C7: 24, C8: 16, C9: 11, C10: 15, C11: 17). The important ones follow, weighted toward what the engine lacks.

### Space (C2; Morton's "faders" for space, lineation and syntax, C4)

- **Squiggles and blankness** (C2 ◐) [MEASURABLE]. The poem organises the page around it. Morton treats the blank as a thick medium, not an empty void, and ranks the prose block (Ashbery's "The System") hottest and Cummings's scatter coolest (C2 ◐). *Measure:* the **space fader** the engine lacks: ink density, the spread of line lengths, and how regular the stanzas are.
- **Bernstein's blank poem** (C1–C2 ◐) [EXERCISE]. *Print the song with its refrain lines left blank but their space kept. Read the gaps aloud as silences of the same length.*
- **The re-mark** (C2, IDX; *Ecology without Nature* ◐) [MEASURABLE]. A re-mark is the extra mark that tells you these are marks: a speech bubble drawn round squiggles (C2 ◐). In a caryā, the double daṇḍa and the ধ্রু sign are re-marks. *Measure:* count the non-verbal marks (numbers, refrain signs, dashes, indents), source and English side by side.
- **Repetition as death: hot and cold space** (C2 ◐; in print in Glossator ✔, see §3) [READING]. Hot space tends toward the regular and the dead; cool space toward the irregular and the living. Ask of the page: *which way does its shape lean?*
- **Metaphor and paraphrase** (C2, IDX) [READING]. In the Notes, a paraphrase is a metaphor that pretends not to be one (C2 ◐). For a translator this is the point: **every accurate English is a metaphor of its song.** Ask: *what does this paraphrase claim the song is like?*
- **Two words, three ways** (C1 homework ◐) [EXERCISE]. *Take two words from the song, e.g. "row" and "Ḍombī". Set them on the page three ways: touching, on two lines, far apart. In two lines each, write what each arrangement does.*

### Lineation (C1, C3; enjambment C2, C4, C10)

- **Hot and cool lineation; consistency and inconsistency** (C3) [IN ENGINE].
- **Quantum coherence and the qubit** (C3 ◐) [READING]. A cool arrangement holds two readings at once. "horse / foot" is both one thing and two (C3 ◐). A hot arrangement collapses to one reading. Morton: "if you're cool enough, you occupy more than one place at one time" (C3 ◐). Ask: *does this break keep two readings alive?*
- **Amplitude, frequency and cadence** (C3 ◐) [MEASURABLE]. Morton borrows the vocabulary of waves. *Measure:* frequency = how often a line shape recurs; amplitude = how far a line's length strays from the groove.
- **Caesura, "a cut"** (C3 ◐) [MEASURABLE]. *Measure:* where the mid-line pauses fall. A pause that always falls mid-line is hot. This is the bridge to the Japanese cutting word (§4b).
- **The present as a blank** (C3, IDX) [READING]. In "Easter Wings" the thinnest line is where the poem dies and turns (C3 ◐). Ask: *where is the poem's narrowest point, and what happens there?*
- **Reveal it line by line** (C3 ◐) [EXERCISE]. *Cover the poem with a sheet and draw it down one line at a time, saying what you expect before each line.* The engine's "blank sheet" (exercise 4) only looks at the whole page. This is the half it lacks: the poem happening in time.
- **Reading in and reading out; no symbolism** (C3) [IN ENGINE]. In the Notes the rule is stated flatly: nothing symbolizes anything (C3 ◐).
- **Enjambment and end-stopping** (C2, C4) [IN ENGINE] (share of end-stopped lines).
- **Enjambment that hesitates** (C10, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. Our gloss: a break that falls inside a phrase (after "the", "of", "my") makes the voice hang over the gap. *Measure:* in a line that is not end-stopped, is the last word a light word? Hesitant breaks cool the lineation.

### Syntax (C1, C4)

- **The phrase as the atom; parataxis and hypotaxis** (C4) [IN ENGINE].
- **Signs of parataxis** (C4) [MEASURABLE]. The signs are *and*, commas and semicolons. The semicolon leans slightly cool, a pair that is almost two sentences (C4 ◐). *Refinement:* count a semicolon as half a paratactic join.
- **Meta-parallel; parallel lineation and parataxis** (C4, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. When syntax and lineation line up, the heat doubles: Blake's "Lamb". When they cross, you get conversation: Williams (C4 ◐). *Measure:* the share of line ends that are also clause ends.
- **Icy fire** (C4) [READING]. Opposite temperatures side by side, Petrarch's oxymoron. The Notes find it in Williams's close, sweet and cold at once (C4 ◐). Ask: *where do opposites touch?*
- **Men … de** (C4) [READING]. The Greek balancing pair, "on the one hand … on the other": the built, cool period. Ask: *is any sentence balanced on a hinge?*
- **Aperture** (C4 ◐) [READING]. Beginning is a feeling, not a position. Ask: *how long does the poem take to begin?*

### Rhythm (C5, C6, C7)

- **Stress, groove and deviation; catalexis; English as a creole; four, five and seven beats; mouthfeel** (C5) [IN ENGINE].
- **Beat as a relationship; relative stress** (C5, C7 ◐) [IN ENGINE]. `scan` lets runs of strong words settle.
- **Pyrrhic and spondee** (C6) [MEASURABLE]. *Measure:* report the "xx" and "//" pairs inside a pentameter.
- **Pentameter, the metre of thinking** (C5) [MEASURABLE]. The engine says it but does not measure it. *Measure:* how regular a pentameter runs (§5).
- **Top-down scansion: "too high an altitude"** (C6, IDX) [READING]. Scan up from the voice, not down from a template. The engine's dictionary-first scan already works this way.
- **Rhythm as atmosphere, the "vibe"** (C6 ◐) [READING]. This is the classroom form of the ambient poetics of *tone* (§3). Ask: *what weather is this poem?*
- **Free verse: rhythm without a metrical plan** (C6 ◐) [IN ENGINE]. The groove is found statistically across lines (the mode of the beat counts).
- **The poem as dictator; coexistence, "the enjoyment of the other"** (C6 ◐) [IN ENGINE as prose] → [EXERCISE] below. The poem commandeers your breath, jaw and lungs. Reading is agreeing to live beside someone else's pleasure (C6 ◐).
- **Whelming** (C5) [IN ENGINE as prose] → [MEASURABLE] (a whelm check, §5).
- **Horror** (C6) [READING]. Texture that makes the flesh creep, the bodily ground of para rhyme. Ask: *where does the poem make your skin react before you understand it?*

### Rhyme (C1, C7, C8)

- **The solar system; absolute, perfect, vowel, off, para; eye rhyme; masculine and feminine** (C7) [IN ENGINE].
- **The galaxy of assonance; subtle alliteration** (C7, C8 ◐) [MEASURABLE]. Rhyme is only one region of all sound-likeness. *Measure:* the share of stressed vowels in a line or couplet that recur.
- **Internal rhyme** (C1, C8) [MEASURABLE]. *Measure:* run `rhymeWords` on every pair of stressed words inside a line and across adjacent lines, not only on the ends.
- **Rhyme performs thought; the rhyme scheme; the sonnet as a movement of thought; the turn** (C7) [IN ENGINE as prose] → [MEASURABLE]. The board pairs lines by the caryā's couplets, so **a sonnet's ABAB reads as unrhymed.** *Measure:* letter the scheme against every earlier end, and find the turn (§5).
- **The heat of a rhyme scheme** (C8, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. *Measure:* lines per rhyme sound. The Italian octave spends 8 lines on 2 sounds; the English sonnet spends 14 on 7. Also measure the heat of each quatrain, to see where a scheme cools. Which scheme Morton calls hotter is on the class 8 page, which I have not seen.
- **Laying rhyme off; "the bondage of rhyme"** (C8, IDX) [EXERCISE]. The phrase is Milton's, from the note "The Verse" added to *Paradise Lost* (1668), which frees heroic verse from "the troublesom and modern bondage of Rimeing" ✔ ([Dartmouth Milton Reading Room](https://milton.host.dartmouth.edu/reading_room/pl/note/text.shtml)). *Rhyme a couplet, then lay the rhyme off and pay the heat back somewhere else: a struck word, a firmer beat.*
- **Sound memory; sound sequence and modulation** (C8, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. Our gloss: how far back the ear still holds a sound. *Measure:* the distance in lines from a sound to its return: 1 in couplets, 2 in quatrains and terza rima, up to 3 in an Italian octave.
- **Terza rima; the ballad stanza** (C8) [MEASURABLE]. Detect the forms: ABA BCB CDC chains forward; 4·3·4·3 beats rhymed xaxa.
- **Noise and sound; the prepared piano; the drone** (C7 ◐) [READING]. In the Notes, Cage's prepared piano is para rhyme and La Monte Young's hour-long drone is absolute rhyme (C7 ◐). Ask: *which planet is this couplet's music?*
- **Mad libs: hot and cool as an algorithm** (C7, IDX) [EXERCISE]. *Keep a poem's frame of repeated words and fill its blanks with new words three times. What keeps its heat is the frame.*
- ***Om Mani Padme Hum*: the mantra as the sun** (C7; Index ✔ that it was read, Notes ◐) [IN ENGINE as an example]. Deeper in §4d.

### Perception: imagery (C1, C9–C12)

- **Sensory forcing; images in order; keep the strange strange** [IN ENGINE].
- **Whether the image is on or off; quantized imagery; quasi-on** (C9, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. Our gloss: an image is summoned or it is not. Some images are only half summoned: an image inside a simile, a question, a wish, a conditional or a negation. *Measure:* a strip of states for each line, ● on, ◐ quasi-on, ○ off.
- **Hot imagery; the rhythm of imagery** (C9, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. *Measure:* images per line, and how steady the count is. One image to a line (*Cathay*) is hot.
- **Emptiness** (C9, IDX) [READING]. In print Morton defines emptiness as "not the absence of something, but the nonconceptuality of reality" (*Realist Magic* p. 74 ✔). Ask: *is the empty place in this poem a hole, or an openness with no concept attached?*
- **Negative imagery: positive and negative present imagery; negation as the test** (C1, C10, C11, IDX; the Notes' version of C1 ◐) [MEASURABLE]. The image you are told not to picture is pictured anyway: "don't think of a pink elephant" (C1 ◐). The man "who wasn't there" of "Antigonish", read in classes 9 and 10, is the classic case. *Measure:* the images inside a negator's scope.
- **Apophasis; negative theology (via negativa); occupatio; the privative prefix** (C1, C10) [MEASURABLE] and [EXERCISE]. Class 10 read *The Cloud of Unknowing* and the hymn "Immortal, Invisible, God Only Wise" ✔ (Index). The hymn's praise is made of privatives, im-, in-, un- (our observation). Occupatio is saying a thing by saying you will not say it. *Measure:* negators, privatives, and "not / never + say, tell, speak, name".
- **Subjunctive and indicative** (C10, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. Mood switches an image half on. This feeds the on/off strip.
- **Image clusters** (C10) [MEASURABLE]. *Measure:* group the marked images by field (water, fire, body, sky) using the glossary's tags.
- **Brightness; brightness and contrast** (C10, C11, IDX; defined in class in C13 and CR, §2b) [MEASURABLE, roughly] and [READING]. Morton's printed source is Longinus's *brilliance* (to ekphanestaton) in *Realist Magic* pp. 132–33 ✔. There, brilliance is what hides an object, as the sun hides dim lights. Ask: *what is the brightest thing in the poem, and what does its light hide?*
- **Ekphrasis** (C11) [EXERCISE]. In print, ekphrasis is "a bristling vividness that interrupts the flow of the narrative" (*Realist Magic* p. 133 ✔). It suspends time like the bullet time of *The Matrix*, which class 11 also brings up ✔ (Index). *Describe one object (a manuscript leaf, a boat, a skull cup) so closely that the song stops moving for two lines.*
- **Imagery and time** (C11, IDX) [READING]. An image takes time to arrive. Morton reads Hopkins's "tumbled over rim in roundy wells" as an adjective that lasts as long as the stone's fall (*Realist Magic* pp. 26–27 ✔). Ask: *how long does this image take to appear?*
- **Under erasure; scratching** (C11, IDX) [READING]. An image crossed out and still visible. Ask: *what is cancelled here but still seen?*
- ***Obscurum per obscurius*; the law of non-contradiction** (C11, IDX) [READING]. Explaining the dark by the darker, which is the riddle's move. And images that are A and not-A at once: Morton's "dialetheia" (*Realist Magic* p. 73 ✔). Ask: *does the image explain, or deepen, what it answers?*

### Tropes (C9, C11)

- **The trope as an algorithm, "spin"** (C11, IDX; stated step by step in CR, §2b) [EXERCISE] and [MEASURABLE]. Class 11 read Prusinkiewicz and Lindenmayer's *The Algorithmic Beauty of Plants* ✔ (Index), the book of rewriting rules that grow a plant. Our gloss: a trope is a turn (*tropos*) that can be run as a rule. *Write one metaphor as a rule ("every X becomes a kind of boat") and run it over five lines.*
- **Hot metaphor, cool metonymy** (C11, IDX; with "metaphor and metonymy in Jakobson and Lacan", C11; confirmed at CR 01:07:29 ◐, §2b) [MEASURABLE, roughly]. The theory is Jakobson's two poles (§3): metaphor joins by likeness (selection), metonymy by nearness (combination). Likeness repeats a form, so it runs hot; nearness runs along a chain, so it runs cool. *Measure:* markers only. Similes are marked by *like*, *as*, *as if*; metaphors by "X is Y"; apostrophe by *O* + a name.
- **Metaphor as *translatio*; simile; epic simile; synecdoche** (C11) [READING]. *Translatio*, "carrying across", is the Latin for metaphor and later for translation. Ask the translator: *what did this English carry across, and what did it leave on the bank?*
- **Tropes as the flowers of rhetoric** (C9, IDX) [READING]. An "anthology" is a gathering of flowers.
- **"Stealing eggs"** (C8–C9). Unglossed: a gap.

### Narrator (C1, C4, C6, C8; the second half in §2b)

- **Point of view and subject position** (C1) [IN ENGINE, partly]. The engine counts calls, commands and the bhaṇitā.
- **Lyric, "the genre of the other mind"** (C8, IDX) [MEASURABLE]. The Guide's version: lyric shows a mind through its pronouns ◐. In class the phrase is credited to Allen Grossman (C13 01:04:47; CR 00:10:34 ◐). *Measure:* the pronoun profile, line by line (I/me, you/thou, he/she, we, it), and where it shifts.
- **Coexistence** (C6 ◐) [EXERCISE]. *Read aloud, whole and at its own pace, a poem you dislike. Then write down only what it made your mouth and breath do.*
- **Innocence** (C4 ◐) [READING]. *In-nocens*, "harmless": the speaker who cannot be accused. Ask: *who in this poem cannot be blamed, and what does that let them say?*
- **Quotation marks; the reaction shot** (C4, C8) [READING]. Ask: *whose words are these, and whose face do we watch as they are said?*
- **Point of view, subject position, ideology, distance** (C13–C15, C17, CR) [READING] and [MEASURABLE]. Now read from the transcripts: see §2b.

### Narrative and genre (C1, C4, C7, C8, C10; the second half in §2b)

- **The turn from A to not-A; genre as a horizon of expectation** (C7) [IN ENGINE].
- **Negation, the minimal unit of thinking** (C8, IDX) [READING]. Our gloss: the turn *is* a negation. Ask: *what does this poem say no to, and where?*
- **Story and plot; frequency and duration** (C1, IDX; taught in C15–C16, §2b) [MEASURABLE]. These are Genette's terms. Frequency is how many times an event is told. A refrain tells the same thing again and again, the repetitive frequency. *Measure:* count repeated lines and half-lines as narrative frequency.
- **Backwards causation: "happening twice"** (C10, IDX; said in class at C16 01:07:10 ◐) [READING]. In print: "to happen, it has to happen twice" (*Realist Magic* p. 91 ✔). Ask: *which image becomes an event only when it comes back?*
- **Resolution and closure; aperture** (C4, C8) [MEASURABLE]. *Measure:* does the last line close? Count an end-stop, a rhyme and the refrain's return.
- **Lyric, epic, romance; genre and form** (C7 ◐). The sonnet's form is 14 lines; its genre is a movement of thought. **Epigram, epitaph, epigraph** (C8); **elegy and eulogy** (C10); **aubade** (C4) [MEASURABLE, for form only]. Detect sonnet, ballad stanza, terza rima, ghazal and *rubāʿī* from the line count and the scheme.

## 2b. The second half: narrator, narrative, and the theory class (classes 13–17, interim, revision)

### C13 (28 Feb): narrator I, point of view

*Subject:* who or what speaks a poem, and how that speaker emerges from all the other channels; then genres as horizons of expectation.

- **The narrator's axioms** (C13 00:16:15–00:18:17) [READING]. Every poem is narrated. The narrator need not be human, need not be an I, and need not be one. It is never the author, even when it bears the author's name (C14 00:02:03, 00:38:51). Morton's proof is the liar's paradox: the I who says "I am lying" cannot be the I it speaks of (C13 00:18:17). Ask: *who or what speaks, judged only from the page?*
- **Point of view** (C13 00:21:46) [READING]. A narrator does two things. The first is point of view, the answer to *who or what is the narrator?* The second, subject position, is class 14's subject.
- **The narrator as a gestalt** (C13 00:32:43; C14 00:03:45) [READING]. The narrator is seldom stated. It emerges from rhythm, rhyme, syntax, lineation and imagery together, as a dog emerges from a field of dots. Even an I that says who it is has a point of view that emerges from the form (C14 00:03:45). In Blake's "A Poison Tree" the jingling tetrameter and the chain of *and*s make a gleeful idiot of the narrator. The tree, the apple and the garden then make that idiot God (C13 00:27:37, 00:28:44, 00:45:15). *For a translator:* the speaker is made of the channels, so a version that cools the rhythm or subordinates the clauses has changed who speaks.
- **The A range: what kind of whole?** (C13 00:29:13–00:32:43) [READING]. Listing the ingredients earns a B+. The A range shows how the levels act on one another and what single thing they make, which Morton calls a manifold. The narrator is usually where that whole shows. Ask: *which two channels are working together to make this voice?*
- **Say only what the page says** (C13 01:11:27; CR 00:05:51) [MEASURABLE]. Morton takes marks off for calling a narrator "she" when the poem does not say so. A vague narrator is a finding, and can be a strong one. *Measure:* list what the source marks about speaker and hearer (person, number, gender), and flag every mark the English adds (§5 (i), 15).
- **Hot and cool imagery, defined** (C13 00:02:31–00:05:55; CR 00:04:17) [MEASURABLE]. Brightness is vividness, which means detail; contrast is how far the images differ. Hot imagery is high in both. Low brightness with high contrast is the **metonymic list**: things named by a plain property, none described. It is funny when the things are incongruous and elegiac when they are lost (Charlotte Smith's *Beachy Head*, C13 01:09:29). *Measure:* brightness as modifiers per image noun, contrast as distinct image fields per line, and a list as three or more nouns in a row with one modifier each at most.
- **Allusion** (C13 00:43:58) [READING]. A reference inside the poem to another made thing. Ask: *what earlier text does the image lean on, and does the English keep the lean?*
- **Genres as horizons** (C13 00:49:40–01:07:30) [IN ENGINE, as the horizon of expectation]. Morton's working definitions are these:
  - epigram: a short poem on an explicit topic;
  - lyric: a song, "the genre of the other mind";
  - sonnet: a formal movement from A to not-A;
  - tragedy and comedy: the high brought low, the low raised up;
  - elegy: loss; satire: mockery;
  - romance: the hidden leader discovered in the wilderness;
  - epic: the origin of a society; didactic: instruction.

  Genre matters here because it predicts point of view and subject position (C13 00:59:16).
- **Dorothy Parker's "Résumé"** (C13 01:07:30–01:15:20) [READING]. An epigram about suicide, with hot rhythm, hot ABAB rhyme and a metonymic list of ways to die. Its narrator is not Parker and has no stated sex. The narrator hovers between wanting death and refusing it, so this is an anti-suicide poem that comes as near as it can to the other kind (C13 01:13:42). Morton draws the moral that "Realism is the ability to tolerate ambiguity" (C13 01:15:20 ◐). Ask: *does the narrator stand in one place on the poem's own claim?*

### CI (29 Feb): the theory under the course

*Subject:* a make-up session (CI 00:00:00) that rebuilds structure from the ground up and states the philosophy under the method: the poem is a physical thing, things translate one another, and ambiguity keeps a poem alive.

- **Space becomes physical architecture** (CI 00:07:29, 00:16:09; C14 00:01:02) [READING] and [EXERCISE]. Morton renames the first channel because students slid from "space" straight to lineation. A poem is inscribed in something with depth:
  - paper that keeps the mesh it dried on;
  - a screen and the electron gun behind it (Ian Bogost's Atari game poems, *A Slow Year*, CI 00:03:39);
  - chalk;
  - or air and ears: Homer was first heard on the air (CI 00:21:59).

  *Exercise:* move the poem to another surface (Morton's test is the fur of a cat, CI 00:19:29; or a field, or tea leaves) and write what changes. Ask: *does the poem speak of the thing it is written on?* (CI 00:20:43).
- **Plato's medium, Aristotle's made thing** (CI 00:23:17–00:26:09, 00:34:03) [READING]. For Plato, reality lies in a beyond and reaches us through a medium, spoiled on the way, which is why poetry is suspect. For Aristotle there is no beyond. A thing's form (*morphē*) is its substance, and a poem is a thing made (*poiēsis*). Morton takes Aristotle's side: "We're going to be Aristotelians now" (CI 00:24:25 ◐). This grounds §1's rule that there is no content under the form, and the rule of reading out, not in.
- **Every transmission is a translation** (CI 00:26:53–00:31:50) [READING]. From gun to air to eardrum to nerve, "Every aspect of that transmission is a kind of translation" (CI 00:26:53 ◐). Metaphor makes this visible: cypress and flame translate each other (CI 00:27:23). Onomatopoeia shows that language is realist: a gun really says *bang* to a human ear, and something like a deep roar to a bee's. The class says here what §3 found in *Realist Magic*: no version is the thing, and each version is real.
- **Things never quite touch** (CI 00:49:17–00:52:21) [READING]. Wile E. Coyote can never have the Road Runner, since to digest it would end it. Morton calls the cartoon Kantian and extends the gap to chalk and blackboard. A poem that wholly absorbed its paper would destroy it, so it lives in a truce between translating everything and translating nothing. The translator's question: *what does this English leave untranslated, and is that the truce the song keeps?*
- **The poem as a manifold of translations** (CI 00:52:55) [READING]. Each level translates the others: paper the words, lines the syntax, structure the texture, and back again. This grounds patterns of patterns: the levels are in conversation before any reader arrives.
- **A poem built of marks** (CI 00:39:26–00:48:14) [EXERCISE]. Morton draws one vertical stroke on the board: a letter I, the number one, or a line?
  - Repeating it makes rhythm, and a row makes lineation.
  - Lines of unequal length cool it.
  - A speech bubble round the strokes makes a narrator (Woodstock, the bird in *Peanuts*) and an absent image. The bubble is C2's re-mark.

  "The more physical it is, the more ambiguous it is" (CI 00:42:09 ◐). *Exercise:* build a poem from one repeated mark, a step at a time (repeat, row, break, bubble, title), and write what each step switched on.
- **Irony as a gap between levels** (CI 00:47:09) [READING]. Morton defines irony as the use of a gap between two or more levels of meaning. In the bird's poem we know *that* it speaks, not *what* it says. Ask: *which two levels of this poem disagree?*
- **Parataxis as addition, hypotaxis as division** (CI 01:11:52–01:12:57) [MEASURABLE]. Paratactic clauses commute, like sums and products: they can swap places. Hypotactic clauses cannot, like differences and quotients. "Parataxis is hot, because it multiplies the energy" (CI 01:12:57 ◐). *Measure:* a commutation test. A join between two clauses can swap unless the second opens on a subordinator (`SUBORD` in `lib/english.mjs`, plus *that, so that, having*). The share of joins that can swap is a check on the syntax heat [IN ENGINE for the markers].
- **Blake's deceptive simplicity** (CI 01:01:14–01:11:13) [READING]. The "Introduction" to *Songs of Innocence* runs hot in lineation, rhyme and rhythm, but its syntax is hypotactic: "Piping down the valleys wild" hangs under "On a cloud I saw a child". Its last line can be read as either kind of syntax. Ask: *where does a hot surface carry cool syntax?*
- **Thick boundaries and double truth** (CI 01:10:11, 01:16:27) [READING]. Meaning is analog, not on or off, and the boundary between parataxis and hypotaxis is thick. Morton points to Graham Priest's paraconsistent (dialetheic) logic, in which a sentence can be true and false at once (compare the law of non-contradiction, C11, in §2). Ask: *is this line two things at once, and would choosing one lose it?*
- **Why poems last** (CI 01:19:25–01:21:25, 01:24:06–01:26:34) [READING]. Physical systems are fragile, and the poems that last are the weak, ambiguous ones, not the strong. Morton's figure is quantum: an isolated system stays in many states, and measuring it makes it consistent (Aaron O'Connell's visible quantum object, CI 01:24:37). So a poem tries to leave each level alone, and a reading should not reduce one level to another. *For a translator:* this is the course's own ground for keeping the strange strange (§3; C3's qubit).
- **What is not there.** The tags name beauty and object-oriented ontology. The transcript has no argument about beauty (the word occurs only in passing), and the phrase "object-oriented ontology" is never used. The class's claims about objects are the ones above: things translate each other and never fully touch.

### C14 (1 Mar): narrator II, subject position and ideology

*Subject:* the narrator's second job, which is to decide who the poem makes you, the reader, and how that works as ideology.

- **Subject position** (C14 00:06:24, 00:20:09) [READING]. It answers *who or what are you, the reader?* It is the attitude the poem demands of you, and it is objective, built into the text: "the text reads you any way it wants" (C14 00:08:24 ◐). Morton's list of positions includes friend, lover, detached enemy, witness, judge and cynic. In Browning's "My Last Duchess" you are the duke's guest (CR 01:16:22). The test question is "Who do you have to be in order to make the poem make sense?" (CR 01:17:14 ◐).
- **The vanishing point** (C14 00:15:25–00:18:02) [READING]. A perspective picture places its viewer, like the arrow on a map that marks where you stand. Holbein's *Ambassadors* holds two positions: the envoys' wealth seen from the front, and the skull seen only from the side. So one poem can hold two subject positions.
- **Ideology is a position built into words** (C14 00:21:14–00:22:45) [READING]. The same payments, called "welfare" or "social security", place the hearer differently. Ideology is hard to shift because it is not an opinion in the head but a vanishing point in the picture. *For a translator:* the register of an English word already places the hearer.
- **With the narrator, or against?** (C14 00:46:24) [READING]. Morton's rule of thumb: does the narrator want you to see through its eyes, or not? Blake's "The Clod and the Pebble" is hot and symmetrical on every level, so conventional that the stacking itself is suspicious (C14 00:30:02). Its impersonal, generalizing voice makes the reader a cynic who weighs selfless and selfish love as equals (C14 00:47:16). Blake makes you try an attitude on so that you can study it from inside (C14 00:49:40).
- **Imagery as the narrator's mind** (C14 00:55:19–01:05:17) [READING]. "The Tyger" is not about God's power. It portrays a speaker who sees the world as a demonic machine: "Look at the imagery as an expression of the narrator's mind" (C14 00:55:47 ◐). Blake's illumination shows a mild, smiling tiger. The gap between picture and words works like a vaccine: the reader gets a small dose of the attitude and is not recruited to it (C14 01:04:10). Ask: *what kind of mind sees the world in these images?*
- **Distance and reliability: mistrust the I** (C14 00:39:53–00:41:27; C17 00:54:53) [READING]. "The first thing you must do with any first-person narrative is mistrust it" (C14 00:39:53 ◐). Morton's examples are *Frankenstein*'s nested narrators, confessional application essays, and nature writing that insists it is real. The harder a voice works to sound authentic, the less it does, and nested narratives multiply the suspicion. This is Morton's version of Wayne Booth's unreliable narrator (our mapping). Ask: *what does this I want you to believe about it, and why does it insist?*
- **Questions and exclamations** (C14 00:54:10) [MEASURABLE]. The Tyger's narrator asks questions that imply their answers, and exclaims: sure and unsure at once. *Measure:* questions and exclamations per line, and runs of lines that are all questions.
- **An epigram** (C14 01:06:22) [EXERCISE]. *Write an epigram, a short poem on one explicit topic. Then say who narrates it and who it makes you.*

### C15 (6 Mar): narrative I, time as material; plot and story

*Subject:* how a poem hails its reader; then narrative as a poem made of time, told in an order that is not the order of events.

- **Interpellation** (C15 00:03:49–00:09:54) [READING] and [MEASURABLE]. Making a subject position is what Althusser calls *interpellation*, or hailing: the police officer's "Hey, you!", Uncle Sam's pointing finger, the magazine quiz that tells you who you are. "Ideology works by hailing you" (C15 00:07:36 ◐). A student's poem did it by opening on "someone" and turning to "you", the most provocative person there is (C15 00:08:08–00:08:43). *Measure:* hails in the opening lines (§5 (i), 16). The caryā board already counts calls (*ālo, hālo*) and commands, and `pronouns()` says where "you" arrives [IN ENGINE, partly].
- **Point of view against subject position** (C15 00:11:35) [READING]. The two can part. The student poem spoke as an angry victim and made the reader the rich brute. When the two coincide, you cannot tell the narrator's view from the view you are asked to take, and irony follows; Morton says Romantic poems live there. Ask: *does the voice want you to share its view, or to be what it attacks?*
- **Narrative: a poem made of time** (C15 00:13:53–00:16:02) [READING]. "A narrative is a poem that uses time as its raw material" (C15 00:14:52 ◐), as a sculpture uses stone. The "Time Passes" chapter of *To the Lighthouse* has no people in it and is still a narrative.
- **Time has syntax, rhythm and rhyme** (C15 00:16:36–00:18:45, 01:07:27–01:08:31) [READING]. Every channel returns at the scale of events. An action film is paratactic narrative, one event after another at equal weight (hot). *Pulp Fiction* is hypotactic, events nested inside events (cool). Events can rhyme: in *Paradise Lost* the beginning and the end of time pair up, and the fall and the redemption make a kind of para rhyme. Narrative rhythm there is a leap, then a slow focus. As Milton's telling closes in on Satan, its syntax turns paratactic and the heat rises.
- **Events, plot and story** (C15 00:20:03–00:22:46) [MEASURABLE]. For the analyst an event is any clause with a verb (C15 00:21:25). Plot is the order in which the poem tells events; story is the order in which they happened. These are the Russian formalists' *sjužet* and *fabula*, and Genette's *order* (our mapping). Number a story 1–5 and tell it 3, 1, 4, 5, 2: the flashbacks and flash-forwards are the made thing. *Measure:* events and anachronies (§5 (i), 18 and 21).
- **Prolepsis and proleptic irony** (C15 00:31:38–00:37:43, 00:43:31–00:44:38) [READING]. *Paradise Lost* first tells one of the story's last events, death brought into the world. Then it tells the very last, the "greater Man" who restores us. That seats the reader above the story, knowing what Adam and Eve do not: proleptic irony. The main verb, "Sing, Heavenly Muse", is held back, so the grammar waits as the story does. Ask: *what do you know before the people in the poem do?*
- **Time of telling, time of the told** (C15 00:48:40–00:51:19) [READING]. Milton walls off the time of the invocation from the story's eternity, and so stands outside time. A confident guide (Milton's narrator, Dickens's) holds your hand and makes you an admiring, passive reader. Ask: *where does the narrator stand in time, and how far from the story?*
- **The tag: distance and identification** (C15 01:03:38–01:05:37; C16 00:18:40) [MEASURABLE] and [EXERCISE]. When the narrator stops saying *he thought* or *she said to herself*, you cannot tell narrator from character, and you see through the character's eyes.
  - The first inner life in *Paradise Lost* is Satan's, which is why the Romantics sided with him (C15 01:00:22).
  - The tag makes distance; dropping it makes identification (C15 01:04:04).
  - Morton calls the technique focalization, finds an early case in Milton, and credits Jane Austen with making it a method (C16 00:18:40).

  *Our note:* narratology separates two ideas here. Focalization (Genette) is whose perception orients the telling. Free indirect speech is a character's thought given in the narrator's grammar, with the tag removed. Morton's "remove the tag" is the second. *Measure:* tags and distance (§5 (i), 17). *Exercise* (Morton's example is Winnie-the-Pooh, C15 01:04:34): *write one line of thought with its tag and one without, then read both and say whose mind you are in.*
- **Aperture, development, closure** (C15 01:13:18–01:19:08) [MEASURABLE] and [READING]. Aristotle's beginning, middle and end are feelings, not page positions.
  - **Aperture** is the feeling of beginning, which is uncertainty (C15 01:15:23). "It is an ancient Mariner" opens on the emptiest verb and drops you into a story that has already happened. "The studio was filled…" (*Dorian Gray*) and "the goblins" (C16 00:57:50) do the same with a definite article. The feeling that something began before you is analepsis (C15 01:19:08).
  - **Development** is the feeling of the middle: cycling, like a disco record.
  - **Closure** is the feeling of the end: certainty.

  *Measure:* the aperture index (§5 (i), 20).
- **Epic against romance** (C15 00:41:26–00:42:57; CR 01:21:28–01:23:00) [READING]. An epic states its whole span at once, which is weak aperture (*Paradise Lost*). A romance drops you *in medias res* and keeps you guessing, which is strong aperture (*Star Wars*). A soap opera is all development. Ask: *does the poem tell you its end before its middle?*
- **Force a position** (C15 00:01:45) [EXERCISE]. *Write four lines that force the reader into a subject position you name. Morton's was a stupid, very rich playboy.*

### C16 (8 Mar): narrative II, frequency and duration

*Subject:* the two further controls on narrative time, stated as ratios; Homer's and Christina Rossetti's openings; then a revision of structure and texture.

- **Frequency** (C16 00:12:32–00:15:30, 00:19:42) [MEASURABLE]. Frequency is how many times an event is told against how many times it happened, as a ratio. One trip to the tobacconist told three times is 3/1, and three trips told once is 1/3. *Jackie Brown* tells one heist from several angles. These are Genette's repetitive, iterative and singulative (our mapping). *Measure:* the frequency strip (§5 (i), 19).
- **Duration** (C16 00:15:59–00:17:38; CR 01:18:31) [MEASURABLE]. Duration is how long the telling takes against how long the event took. A war can pass in a clause, and a quarter of an hour on a floating plank can fill twenty pages. "Happily ever after" is fifty years in a phrase. *Tristram Shandy* never gets its hero born. *Measure:* scene against summary (§5 (i), 22).
- **Narrative has many dimensions** (C16 00:10:42–00:11:31, 00:18:09) [READING]. Plot and story make one plane, and frequency and duration another. Add points of view and subject positions, and a narrative has many dimensions to shape.
- **Closure as one to one** (C16 00:22:39–00:30:33) [MEASURABLE]. Morton's central claim: as the ratios approach 1/1 (each event told once, in order, in real time), the telling feels fast and final.
  - Chases work this way, and so do the last pages of novels: Willoughby at the door, the police on Dorian Gray's stairs.
  - "If you have ratios approaching one-to-one, it's very hot" (C16 00:30:01 ◐).
  - Tension is regularity. To release it, break the ratio, as "happily ever after" does.
  - The content does not matter: a flower seller told in real time is as tense as Jason Bourne (C16 00:24:17).

  So **the narrative channel has its own temperature, on the same principle as rhythm: regular ratios are hot.** *Measure:* closure (§5 (i), 22).
- **Narrative rhyme** (C16 00:22:07) [IN ENGINE, as the refrain]. An event told over and over is a narrative AA scheme, very hot; told once, it is cold. So a refrain is narrative rhyme as well as sound rhyme (§4d).
- **Aperture and development as ratios** (C16 00:31:04–00:40:44) [MEASURABLE]. At the start you cannot tell which unit of time counts, seconds or years: the ratios are undecidable. In the middle the ratios are steady but inverted. Dorian Gray rereads a book again and again, and the rereading is told once (1/n). That gives development its floating, cyclic feel. To end the middle, tell one thing once, in real time (C16 00:40:17).
- **Homer's proem** (C16 00:47:48–00:51:43) [READING]. The *Iliad*, read in Samuel Butler's prose (1898; our identification), does what *Paradise Lost* does: it names the end in its second sentence. Then the ratios swing. An angry speech is told in full, and "nine whole days" of arrows in a phrase (1/n). Wild ratios inside a stated frame give what Morton calls "hand-holding aperture" (C16 00:51:43 ◐): an uncertain telling of a known story. Ask: *how many speeds does the opening try before it settles?*
- **Christina Rossetti's "Goblin Market"** (C16 00:52:48–00:59:29) [READING]. Its opening is iterative ("Morning and evening", "Evening by evening"): many evenings told once. So the poem begins in development, with a little aperture from the goblins' cry and the definite article. The fruit list is paratactic and metonymic, high in contrast and low in brightness, over hot rhythm and hot rhyme. Morton hears fairy-tale content in a realist form, the Pre-Raphaelite mix. On the name Morton gives "evening by evening", see the slips below.
- **How a lyric narrates** (C16 01:01:09–01:07:10; CR 01:24:06–01:25:25; C13 01:04:47) [READING] and [MEASURABLE]. Every poem has a narrator; only some have a narrative.
  - A lyric is a song centred on a person, "the genre of the other mind", and it may tell no story.
  - A dramatic monologue ("My Last Duchess", "Prufrock") is a speech with narrative elements, from which you infer the story (C16 01:01:38).
  - A poem with one event has no plot: "For something to happen, meaningfully, it has to happen twice" (C16 01:07:10 ◐).
  - "Twinkle, Twinkle" has a narrator but no narrative until you frame it ("Little Lucy opened the curtains and said…"), which makes two events (CR 01:25:25).

  Yet short poems still have aperture, development and closure (the homework below), and a refrain is narrative rhyme. *Measure:* events (§5 (i), 18).
- **Aperture, closure, development** (C16 00:02:07; C17 00:02:22) [EXERCISE]. *Write a four-line story with strong aperture and another with strong closure; then a six-line story with strong development.*

### C17 (13 Mar): narration in long poems

*Subject:* how the openings of Wordsworth's "The Ruined Cottage" and Shelley's *Alastor* build a point of view, then break it.

- **Oblique uncertainty** (C17 00:03:59–00:05:04) [READING]. A beginning is first contact between reader and poem. The finest aperture is not open confusion but uncertainty you cannot be sure of. Wordsworth tells you exactly where and when you are, and nothing about why.
- **One adjective to a noun** (C17 00:14:07–00:18:03) [MEASURABLE]. Wordsworth's "pale steam" and "northern downs" give contrast without vividness. Vivid description, as in ekphrasis, takes control away from the reader. Keats overwhelms you with pleasure; Wordsworth leaves you your wits. *Measure:* modifiers per noun, line by line (C13's brightness).
- **The subject position as a camera** (C17 00:22:05–00:27:11) [READING]. The opening pans from sky to land and makes the reader calm, open and wondering, with no sex assigned; Morton calls Wordsworth proto-cinematic. Then "Pleasant to him" sets a hypothetical man under an oak, and you see through him.
- **Reading instructions inside the poem** (C17 00:25:37–00:35:22) [READING]. The man under the oak looks out "with side-long eye", and the branches make "a twilight of their own": the poem tells you how to read it. Because the instructions are inside the poem, they are part of it too. Plants are tropes: an anthology is a gathering of flowers. Ask: *does the poem tell you how to read it, and do you trust it?*
- **The restart: point of view split from subject position** (C17 00:36:07–00:38:52) [READING]. "Other lot was mine": after a paragraph break, a short line restarts the poem, and the tired walker is not the dreaming man. Morton calls it a dislocation between point of view and subject position (C17 00:37:16). The narrator is not Wordsworth but a well-meaning man who misses the point, and the poem warns you off his view early. On rhythm, "An iron-pointed staff lay at his side" bends the pentameter with a pyrrhic and a spondee (C17 00:09:36).
- **Reading sidelong** (C17 00:40:50–00:44:58) [READING]. The skull in the *Ambassadors* appears only from the side, and the war that ruined Margaret appears only to a sidelong, suspicious reading. Morton borrows Adorno's praise of Proust's "remorseless gentleness" (C17 00:42:55 ◐) for a poem that takes an attitude apart without attacking it. Its reward is coexistence, learning to tolerate ambiguity.
- **A poem that begins twice** (C17 00:45:36–00:58:50; CR 01:20:58) [READING] and [MEASURABLE]. *Alastor* begins three times:
  - a preface condemning its hero;
  - an invocation to "Earth, ocean, air" in a gentle, Wordsworthian voice;
  - a second invocation, to the "Mother of this unfathomable world", full of charnels and ghosts.

  One narrator or two (C17 00:51:20)? Anything that starts twice should make you suspicious (C17 00:54:20). A reader who despises the Gothic voice, as the preface invites, becomes the villain (C17 00:56:22). *Measure:* restarts (§5 (i), 23).

### CR (19 Mar): the method summed up

*Subject:* a review before the exam: imagery and narration in brief, the genres and their forms, tropes as algorithms, and subject position once more.

- **The order, and why narration comes last** (C16 01:06:12–01:06:42; C17 00:00:30; CR 00:02:16) [IN ENGINE].
  - Architecture comes first; then syntax, stanza form and lineation; then rhythm and rhyme; then imagery.
  - Narration comes last: point of view and subject position, then plot, story, frequency and duration if there is a narrative.
  - It comes last because it emerges from the first three levels.
  - Before writing, read the whole poem at every level (CR 00:02:16).
- **Read out, not in** (CR 00:05:18–00:06:22) [IN ENGINE]. "Stick to what's on the page. Don't read into the poem" (CR 00:05:18 ◐). A vague narrator or a vague subject position is reported as vague.
- **What a reading should do** (C13 00:29:13–00:32:43; C17 00:00:00, 00:44:27; C16 01:07:38) [READING]. It shows the levels acting on each other and names the whole they make, and the narrator is usually where that whole shows. Its ethic is tolerance of ambiguity. Every term it uses is defined and given an example.
- **Genres and their forms** (CR 00:07:57–00:49:05, 01:25:25–01:27:32) [MEASURABLE, for form].
  - Elegy comes in blank verse or in ABAB pentameter quatrains.
  - Satire, "the genre of contempt" (CR 00:18:31 ◐), comes in rhymed couplets.
  - Didactic poetry, including the georgic, comes in blank verse.
  - The ballad is a story in lyric form, lines of four and three beats rhymed ABCB.
  - Sonnets are English, Italian (ABBA ABBA CDE CDE) or Spenserian (ABAB BCBC CDCD EE, CR 00:33:47).
  - Loco-descriptive poems describe a place by walking through it; topographical ones say "here … there" (CR 00:44:17–00:45:14).

  *Measure:* add the Spenserian scheme to form detection, since `rhymeScheme()` does not name it yet.
- **Ode, apostrophe and elegy** (CR 00:51:00–00:55:42) [MEASURABLE] and [READING]. An ode is an address shaped like a prayer: invoke the power (in Greek and Latin, three ways), then ask it for something, as Shelley asks the West Wind. Its figure is **apostrophe**, a formal address to a person or thing. Elegy is its opposite: the ode is about its addressee, the elegy about the loss felt by the one who speaks (CR 00:52:36). *Measure:* address and the ode/elegy balance (§5 (i), 16).
- **Tropes as algorithms** (CR 00:56:59–01:08:29) [MEASURABLE]. Morton states each trope as a procedure:
  - metaphor: noun + *is* + noun;
  - simile: the same, with *like*;
  - metonymy: substitute one of Aristotle's four causes (material, efficient, formal, final), as "cancer stick" names a cigarette by its final cause;
  - synecdoche: cut out a part to stand for the whole;
  - apostrophe: choose a person and address them with *O*, *dear* or *hey* (CR 01:05:33).

  Metaphor is brighter and hotter than metonymy (CR 01:07:29). This confirms two IDX glosses in §2 (Tropes) and backs the marker counts of §5 (i), 13.

### What it gives a caryā translator (our links)

- **The bhaṇitā is a tag.** "Lūyī says" steps outside the song and names its singer in the third person: that is distance. Change the tag and you change the distance; "I, Lūyī" closes the gap (C15 01:04:04). The Lūyī named in the song is a narrator, not the historical poet (C14 00:38:51). The version prompt's rule "Keep the signature's person" is this point as practice.
- **Calls are hails.** *Ālo*, *hālo* and the commands interpellate a hearer and fix a subject position. An English that adds a "you" or a "my love", or gives the hearer a sex the gloss lacks, makes the listener someone else. The rule "No 'I' and no hearer the song lacks" covers this.
- **The refrain is narrative rhyme.** Told again after every couplet, it is repetitive frequency, and very hot. It keeps the song cycling in development rather than moving to closure. Cutting or varying it changes the narrative temperature as well as the sound.
- **Many caryās are a speech or a scene.** They have a narrator and little plot. A version should not invent a story the song does not tell: one event is no plot.
- **The song's first architecture is the voice**, as Homer's was the air. Print is the song's second architecture, and the page should say so.

## 3. The theory under it

### Jakobson: where "hot" comes from

- **The formula.** "The poetic function projects the principle of equivalence from the axis of selection into the axis of combination" (Jakobson, "Linguistics and Poetics", in Sebeok, ed., *Style in Language*, MIT Press 1960, pp. 350–77, at p. 358) ◐. Nigel Fabb gives the page ([Strathclyde](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/16673/)). Richard Clarke's lecture notes give the same wording from a reprint. Two independent quotations agree, but I did not see the original.
- **What it means.** In ordinary speech we choose one word from a set of equivalents (*child, kid, youngster*) and move on. Poetry keeps choosing from the same set along the line. Jakobson spells out what gets matched: "Syllables are converted into units of measure, and so are morae or stresses" ◐ (via Clarke). Stress equals stress, pause equals pause, sound equals sound.
- **So hot is Jakobson's equivalence turned up** (our reading). Absolute rhyme, the refrain and the mantra are equivalence at its maximum. Cool is combination left to itself: contiguity, the next thing and then the next. Read this way, Morton's two poles are Jakobson's two axes: **hot is selection laid on the line, cool is the line left free.** It also means the engine's mātrā count for Old Bengali and its stress count for English are one measure, not two: Jakobson names morae beside stresses.
- **Metaphor and metonymy.** In the same essay, where likeness is laid on nearness, "any metonymy is slightly metaphorical and any metaphor has a metonymical tint" ◐ (via Clarke). The two poles come from Jakobson's 1956 essay on aphasia (in *Fundamentals of Language*), which I did not read: UNVERIFIED at source. Class 11's "hot metaphor, cool metonymy" is this theory in Morton's temperatures.
- **What it adds to translating.** Jakobson makes rhyme and metre *semantic*. Sound equivalence pulls meanings together, so a translation that keeps the sense and drops the sound-links has cut ties the poem made between its words. The engine's rule of **temperature matching** is Jakobson's point put as a practice.

### "An Object-Oriented Defense of Poetry" (*New Literary History* 43.2, 2012, pp. 205–24; [doi:10.1353/nlh.2012.0018](https://doi.org/10.1353/nlh.2012.0018))

- **What I could verify.** The bibliographic details ✔ (Crossref; Morton's CV). One sentence at p. 222, through Matthew J. Dodson's MA thesis (Oregon State, 2014, [PDF](https://ir.library.oregonstate.edu/downloads/h128nh407)) ◐: to write poetry is to "force the reader to coexist with fragile phrases, fragile ink, fragile paper". Dodson also reports that Morton turns Shelley's *Defence of Poetry* (quoted at p. 216) toward the physical surface of things ◐. The article sits in one issue with Graham Harman's "The Well-Wrought Broken Hammer" and Jane Bennett's response ◐.
- **Its argument, from Morton's book-length version.** *Realist Magic* (Open Humanities Press, 2013, [open access](https://www.openhumanitiespress.org/books/titles/realist-magic/)) ✔ develops the same ideas:
  - "I argue that causality is wholly an aesthetic phenomenon" (p. 19);
  - "The aesthetic dimension is the causal dimension" (p. 20);
  - art is therefore the study of how things affect each other, not decoration on top of them (pp. 19–20);
  - Shelley's Aeolian lyre (pp. 91–92, 209) is the model of reading: the wind is heard only as the strings modulate it;
  - "What makes a poem a poem is that we don't know what it means yet" (p. 209, glossing Shelley);
  - "I make the poem real for me by misreading it" (p. 208).
- **What it adds to reading.** Read in these terms, the course's claims are not metaphors. A poem really does act on your jaw and lungs (C6). Form really is the thing, and there is no content underneath (C1 ◐). Reading is attunement to an object that stays partly withdrawn.
- **What it adds to translating.** A translation is one object's reading of another, a "translation" in Morton's technical sense (*Realist Magic* pp. 110, 133 ✔: the frog's croak becomes *brekekekex*; ekphrasis "generates its own kind of object"). No version exhausts the song, and each is a new thing with its own causal force. That is the ground for printing several Englishes side by side. It also grounds the rule to keep the strange strange: settling an image's meaning is a measurement that ends its many states (compare the quantum passage, *Realist Magic* p. 209 ✔, with C3 ◐).

### The ambient poetics of *Ecology without Nature* (Harvard UP, 2007)

- **The list, confirmed as six:** rendering, the medial, the timbral, the Aeolian, tone and the re-mark ◐. It is listed at p. 34 and discussed at pp. 35–49. Two secondary sources agree: Chaiyon Tongsukkaeng's PhD thesis (Sheffield, 2015, [White Rose](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/11616/)) and a [2017 blog summary](https://headforthecountry.wordpress.com/2017/03/30/ambient-poetics-in-timothy-mortons-ecology-without-nature/) quoting Morton with page numbers. Morton used the term earlier in "Why Ambient Poetics?" (*The Wordsworth Circle* 33.1, 2002) and "'Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star' as an Ambient Poem" (*Romantic Praxis*, 2001) ✔ (Morton's CV). So the course's first poem is one Morton had already read as an ambient poem.
- **Each element, and where it shows up in class** (glosses from the secondary sources ◐; the links to classes are ours):
  - **Rendering** (p. 35): art that makes you feel you are *in* a real place. In class: the space around the poem, "objects in mirror are closer than they appear" (C3).
  - **The medial** (p. 37): writing that shows the page or the channel it is on. In class: squiggles, the paper, the blank (C2).
  - **The timbral** (p. 39): sound in its body rather than its meaning. In class: mouthfeel, the colour of rhyme (C5, C7).
  - **The Aeolian** (p. 41): sound with "no obvious source", processes going on without a speaker. In class: rhythm as weather (C6). This is the bridge to the Aeolian lyre in *Realist Magic*.
  - **Tone** (pp. 43–44): what "accounts materially for that slippery word *atmosphere*", in body and environment at once. In class: the "vibe" (C6).
  - **The re-mark** (pp. 48–49): the mark that makes us notice marks, background turned to foreground. In class: C2's term, taken from Derrida.
- **What it adds.** Ambient poetics is the course's theory of **space and texture**, the channels that come before meaning. For translation it names what a gloss cannot carry: the page, the timbre, the weather of a song. The caryā pulls against the Aeolian: where Aeolian sound has no source, the bhaṇitā names its singer, a re-mark that signs the song as made.

## 4. Bridges for the other research notes

### (a) Sufi poetry

- **The *radīf* is absolute rhyme after perfect rhyme.** In every couplet a changing rhyme word (*qāfiya*) is followed by the same word or phrase. So the ghazal strikes Morton's sun and an inner planet together, every time. Its couplets stand apart in sense. That makes the ghazal Morton's Baked Alaska: the hottest rhyme over the coolest narrative. The engine can find it: see *radīf* in §5.
- ***Dhikr* and the mantra.** The repeated divine name is the mantra's pole. Morton's printed account of that pole is Buddhist, not Sufi: "Repetition is a feature of the death drive. Mantras are viral clones of sacred words, uttered millions of times" (*Glossator* 7, p. 88 ✔). The Sufi note should decide whether "passing away" (*fanāʾ*) meets that reading. It is a question, not a finding.
- **Apophasis.** Michael Sells, *Mystical Languages of Unsaying* (University of Chicago Press, 1994) ✔ (archive.org catalogue record). There, apophasis "literally means 'speaking away'": discourse that keeps turning back on its own names. It has a chapter on Ibn ʿArabī ("Identity Shift and Meaning Event") ◐. Morton teaches the same move in classes 1 and 10 as negative imagery and the via negativa. In print Morton ties apophatic negation to Nāgārjuna: "negation is the work of death, peeling away and rejecting, spitting out" (*Glossator* 7, p. 84 ✔). Rumi signing himself *Khāmūsh*, "Silent" (per POETRY-PLAN), is an apophatic signature. Measure it with the negative-imagery tools.
- **Morton's own caution.** Morton names the risk of "the perilous edge of New Age syncretism", the idea that "Sufi and Tibetan yogi speak alike" (*Glossator* 7, p. 74 ✔). The comparison note should compare *devices*, as this study does, and not claim one experience behind them.

### (b) Zen poetry

- **Emptiness is not a negative image.** Morton's emptiness is nonconceptuality, not absence (*Realist Magic* p. 74 ✔). Wang Wei's "empty mountain" can therefore be read two ways. It is a *negative* image ("no one is seen": you picture the person who isn't there, as in "Antigonish"). It is also an *empty* one (the mountain with no concept on it). The Zen note should keep both, and our on/off strip can show where each falls.
- **The cut.** Morton's caesura is literally "a cut" (C3 ◐), and "the present as a blank" (C3) is a reading of a gap in time. The *kireji* is a caesura made into a word, the cut that switches from one image to another. *Measure:* caesura map, dash, the image strip either side of it.
- **Bashō's old pond** (C11, Index ✔). What Morton said about it is in the untranscribed class page: UNVERIFIED. Morton's printed nearest neighbour is the opening of *Realist Magic*'s "Magic Birth" chapter (p. 110 ✔). There a pond full of croaking frogs is heard, and the croak is "translated" into *brekekekex*: a nonhuman sound becoming words. The sound of the water in Bashō's poem can be read by that light: *imagery and time* (C11), an image switched on after a long "off".
- **Becoming like a stone.** Morton writes that in meditation "one really does become like a stone, as is said in the Zen meditation manuals", citing D. T. Suzuki (*Glossator* 7, p. 83 ✔). Useful for Hanshan's rock-written poems, as a reading, not a claim of influence.
- **The parallel couplet** is Jakobson's equivalence at work, and Morton's *parallel lineation and parataxis* (C4). Measure it as parallelism between two half-lines (§5).

### (c) Shakespeare's sonnets (read against the public-domain text; the observations are ours unless marked)

- **The two sonnets Morton pairs in class 7 are both built on negation.**
  - **Sonnet 116** defines love by what it is not. It has nine negators and a privative ("unknown") in fourteen lines: "Let me *not*", "Love is *not* love", "O *no*!", "*never* shaken", "Love's *not* Time's fool", "alters *not*", "I *never* writ, *nor no* man". It is apophasis in a love poem.
  - **Sonnet 130** praises by denial: "*nothing* like the sun", "*no* such roses", "I *never* saw a goddess go". Every blazon image (sun, coral, snow, wires, roses, perfume, music, goddess) is switched on in order to be switched off.
  - That class 10 (negative imagery) returns to 130 ✔ (Index) suggests Morton saw this; the class page will say.
- **The heat of the scheme.**
  - **130** rhymes perfectly throughout: sun/dun, red/head, white/delight, cheeks/reeks, know/go, sound/ground, rare/compare. That is hot rhyme carrying negative images, a Baked Alaska.
  - **116** cools exactly on its "love" sounds: love/remove, come/doom and the closing proved/loved are eye or off rhymes. Its other rhymes are perfect: minds/finds, mark/bark, cheeks/weeks, and the feminine shaken/taken. The Notes report Morton hearing the couplet's warmth as only apparent (C7 ◐). 116's heat moves inside the line instead: "love … love", "alters … alteration", "remover … remove".
- **The turn.**
  - 130 turns at "And yet" (line 13): the English sonnet's trip.
  - **Sonnet 29**, the plan's centrepiece, turns twice: at "Yet" (line 9, the Italian place) and at the couplet.
  - Lettered against every earlier end, 29 is not ABAB CDCD EFEF GG but **ABAB CDCD EBEB FF**. "State" ends lines 2 and 10, an absolute rhyme joining the first quatrain to the third. The first "state" rhymes with *fate*, the second with heaven's *gate*: the same word, rhymed first with doom and then with heaven, is the poem's whole movement (our reading). The engine should letter schemes this way.
- **The sonnet as a movement of thought** (C7 ◐): from A to not-A. With class 8's "negation, the minimal unit of thinking" (IDX), the turn is a negation. That is why 116 and 130, poems made of negation, suit the lesson.

### (d) The Charyapada

- **Twilight language against "no symbolism."**
  - Morton bans reading symbols *in* (C3 ◐). The caryās are made of images the tradition reads as codes (see [`charyapada-sound.md`](charyapada-sound.md) B8).
  - The edition's resolution is already Morton-compatible: the image works first as an image, "on" in the line, and Munidatta's reading is a re-mark printed beside it.
  - In Morton's model, a cool image holds several states at once, and decoding it is a measurement that ends them (C3 ◐; *Realist Magic* p. 209 ✔). Hess's "charged pointer hub" (charyapada-sound.md B8) is the same structure seen from the tradition's side.
- **Morton's own reading of a mantra.**
  - In class 7 *Om Mani Padme Hum* is the sun of the solar system, absolute rhyme (Index ✔; the Notes ◐).
  - In print, mantra is repetition in the service of the death drive, recited 1.2 million times in the Vajrayāna preliminaries (*Glossator* 7, p. 88 ✔). Morton reads Freud's "Nirvana principle", the drive toward quiescence, as possibly more than a metaphor (pp. 78–79 ✔).
  - So in Morton's own vocabulary the ধ্রু refrain's heat is the song leaning toward quiescence. **For a song whose goal is nirvāṇa, "hot" is not a fault to cool down.**
- **The charnel ground.** Morton calls it the "favored spot of tantric yogis" (*Glossator* 7, p. 83 ✔). The Dzogchen yogi, Morton writes, finds it intimate rather than repellent (p. 88 ✔). That is a reading for the caryās' cremation grounds, and a check on the Owen lens: the cold para rhyme may be the wrong temperature where the song means intimacy.
- **Commentary.** Morton's essay is about auto-commentary: Dzogchen liturgies that carry their own gloss inside the text (*Glossator* 7, pp. 74–76 ✔). The caryā is arranged the other way: the song, then Munidatta's separate Sanskrit commentary, with the bhaṇitā as the song's only self-reference. The edition's layout (song, then notes) follows the caryā's arrangement, not the liturgy's.
- **Lineage, carefully.** Morton writes as a practitioner in the Tibetan Mahāmudrā and Dzogchen world. The essay cites Chögyam Trungpa's *Sadhana of Mahamudra*, whose title invokes "the Ocean of Siddhas of the Practice Lineage" ✔ (p. 75 n.7). That world traces itself to the Indian siddhas ◐ (not checked here). No Morton text on the caryās themselves was found.

## 5. What it gives the practice

### (i) New measurements for `lib/reading.mjs` and `lib/english.mjs`, ranked

1. **Rhyme-scheme lettering.** Give each line end a letter, testing it against *all* earlier ends with `rhymeWords`: off rhyme or hotter shares the letter; para and eye rhymes are marked. Name the form: couplets, sonnet, ghazal AA BA CA, *rubāʿī* AABA, terza rima. Report heat per quatrain. This is the precondition for any sonnet or ghazal reading. Today the board pairs caryā couplets only.
2. **Radīf and absolute rhyme.** Take the longest common word-suffix of the even lines (or of all lines). If it is at least one word, recurs three or more times, and the rhyme words before it differ, it is a *radīf*. Also list every word that ends two lines (Sonnet 29's "state").
3. **The turn.** For each line boundary, score +2 if the next line opens with *but, yet, and yet, so, then, now, thus, still, O*; +1 at a scheme-block boundary (8|6, 12|2); +1 if the negator density or the pronoun set changes across it. Report the top boundary as "Italian (9)", "English (13)" or "early".
4. **The space fader** (a fifth channel, as in Morton's C4 faders). Compute space = 0.4·(1 − spread of characters per line) + 0.3·(stanza sizes all equal) + 0.3·(ink ÷ (lines × longest line)). Read it from the text as laid out, keeping indents and blank lines. Add `space` to `CHANNELS` and to each lens's board.
5. **Negative imagery.** Mark negators (*not, no, nor, never, none, nothing, nobody, nowhere, without, neither, -n't*) and privatives (*un-, in-/im-, -less* on a dictionary base). A negator's scope runs to the next punctuation. Negative images are marked things or nouns inside a scope. Flag occupatio (*not/never* + *say, tell, speak, name, mention*). For the source, count the same in the gloss.
6. **On / off / quasi-on strip.** Each image is *off* in a negator's scope; *quasi-on* after *like, as, as if*, in a question, after *if, would, might, could, were*, or after a wish verb; otherwise *on*. Print it as ●◐○ per line.
7. **Parallelism between half-lines or lines.** Compute 0.4·(1 − edit distance of the two `scan` patterns ÷ the longer) + 0.3·(share of words in common) + 0.3·(same first word or same last word). Serves the Psalm lens, the Chinese couplet and the caryā's two halves.
8. **Enjambment that hesitates.** A line that is not end-stopped hesitates if its last word is in `LIGHT` (article, preposition, possessive, conjunction, auxiliary). Each hesitation lowers lineation heat by a small step. List the lines.
9. **Caesura map.** Record the positions of mid-line , ; : — – . ? ! and runs of spaces, as a fraction of the line's length. Caesura heat = 1 − spread of the positions.
10. **Pentameter regularity.** Take the share of five-beat lines, and the distance of each `scan` pattern from x/x/x/x/x/, allowing an opening inversion. Report the "//" (spondee) and "xx" (pyrrhic) pairs.
11. **Internal rhyme and assonance.** Run `rhymeWords` on pairs of stressed words within a line and across adjacent lines. Measure assonance as the share of stressed vowels that recur in a line or couplet.
12. **Images: rhythm, clusters, brightness.** Count images per line and their steadiness. Group them by glossary field. Mark the brightest line with a small lexicon of light, fire, colour and shining. This one is rough and should be labelled so.
13. **Trope markers.** Count simile (*like, as … as, as if*), copular metaphor ("X is Y", both nouns) and apostrophe (*O* + name). Counts only; no verdicts.
14. **Whelm check.** Four or more channels at 0.85 or above: "may overwhelm". Every channel at 0.25 or below: "may underwhelm". Otherwise the board says nothing.

**The narrator and narrative channels** (from §2b; ranked among themselves, and numbered on so that 1–14 keep their numbers). Here is what the engine does today:

- for a caryā, it counts calls, commands and the bhaṇitā (`lib/reading.mjs`);
- for any English poem, it reports the persons in each line and where they shift (`pronouns()`), and it finds the turn (`turn()`), both in `lib/poetics.mjs`.

Each item below is one pass over the lines, on the gloss where it can be and on every English. Items 15–17 guard the narrator; 18–22 build a narrative channel; 23 is rare but telling.

15. **Marks added to speaker and hearer.** Record the person, number and gender of speaker and addressee in the gloss and in the English, using `pronouns()` plus a short list of gendered words (*man, woman, girl, boy, lady, lord, king, queen, mother, father, bride, maid*). List every mark the English has and the gloss lacks (C13 01:11:27; CR 00:05:51).
16. **Address, hails and apostrophe.** A line addresses someone if it has *O* or *oh* + a noun or name, a name set off by commas at a line's edge, *you* or *thou* with an imperative, or (in the source) a word in `CALLS`. Report three things:
    - each addressee and the line where it first arrives;
    - whether the first two lines hail the hearer (second person, an imperative, or *someone, whoever, you who*);
    - the ode/elegy balance, lines with *you* ÷ lines with *I* (C15 00:03:49; CR 00:52:36).
17. **Tags and distance.** Count the tags: a subject + a reporting verb (*say, says, said, quoth, tell, cry, ask, think, thought, wonder, dream*), or a bhaṇitā. Count quoted speech. Distance = tags ÷ (tags + lines of untagged speech or thought). Flag untagged questions and exclamations inside third-person lines as possible free indirect speech. Report questions and exclamations per line (C15 01:04:04; C14 00:54:10).
18. **Events.** Split each line into clauses at punctuation and at *and, but, or* and `SUBORD`. A clause is an event if it has a finite verb: a word in `AUX`, a past form (`isParticiple`), or the word after a subject pronoun (`SUBJECTS`). Print events per line; with fewer than two in the poem, say "a narrator, no narrative" (C16 01:07:10; CR 01:24:06). This one is rough, and should be labelled so.
19. **Frequency strip.** Mark each line:
    - **I** (iterative): *every, each, always, often, whenever, again and again*, a noun repeated round *by*, *after* or *and* (*evening by evening*), or a number + *times, days, nights, years*;
    - **R** (repetitive): a refrain, or a line that retells an earlier line's event (`parallelism()` ≥ 0.7);
    - **S** (singulative): anything else.

    Print it as a strip, e.g. I I S S R S R (C16 00:12:32, 00:22:07).
20. **Aperture index.** Score the first two lines:
    - +1 for each definite article or third-person pronoun with nothing before it to refer to;
    - +1 for a bare *it is* or *there was*, for a question, and for a pluperfect;
    - −1 for a line that names the whole span or its end (*first … till*, *brought death*), or for *once upon a time*.

    Report strong (2 or more), weak (0 or less) or mixed (C15 01:13:18; C17 00:04:32; CR 01:21:28).
21. **Order and anachrony.** Tag each clause's tense (past, present, future or modal, pluperfect *had* + participle) and its time words (*then, now, once, before, after, already, long ago, one day, tomorrow*). A pluperfect or *long ago* after past clauses is a flashback. *Will* or *shall*, or a named end before the events, is a flash-forward. Report only the jumps the markers make clear (C15 00:20:03, 00:34:47).
22. **Scene, summary and closure.** A line is summary if a clause names a span (a number + a time unit, *all night, for years, ever after*). It is scene if it has direct speech or two or more successive single events. Print a pace strip. Score closure as the share of scene among the last quarter of the lines, set against the rest: Morton's approach to one to one (C16 00:22:39).
23. **Restarts.** After the first stanza or verse paragraph, flag a line that opens a new invocation (a vocative with a new addressee), or that changes the persons after a full stop and a short line. Two or more beginnings earn the note "begins twice: ask which voice to trust" (C17 00:36:07, 00:54:20).

### (ii) Exercises for a Practice room (each a short, repeatable instruction)

1. **Two words, three ways.** Two words from the song, three arrangements, two lines on each.
2. **Reveal it line by line.** Cover the poem and draw the sheet down a line at a time, saying what you expect before each line.
3. **Say it without it.** Describe a thing in four lines using only what it is not (Sonnet 130, the hymn of privatives). Then write it positively and ask which image is brighter.
4. **Occupatio.** Praise something in four lines by refusing to speak of it.
5. **Switch an image on and off.** Write one image three times: indicative (on), inside *as if* or *might* (half on), negated (off).
6. **The rhyme orbit.** Close one couplet six ways: absolute, perfect, vowel, off, para, alliteration only. Choose one and say why.
7. **Lay the rhyme off.** Take the rhyme out of a rhymed couplet and pay the heat back with a struck word or a firmer beat.
8. **Radīf.** Five couplets ending on the same word or phrase, with a changing rhyme just before it.
9. **Fourteen lines that turn.** Write a thought that goes from A to not-A at line 9, then write it again turning at line 13. Say how the thinking changed.
10. **Hesitate.** Break one sentence at its most hesitant point (after "the") and at its least (after a full clause). Read both aloud.
11. **The cut.** Two images in three lines with one hard cut (a dash) between them. Move the cut and read again.
12. **Run the trope.** Write one metaphor as a rule and let it generate five lines.
13. **Ekphrasis.** Describe one object from the song so closely that the song stops for two lines.
14. **Mad libs.** Keep a poem's frame of repeated words and refill it three times. Keep whichever keeps the heat.
15. **Coexist.** Read aloud, whole, a poem you dislike. Note only what it made your breath and jaw do.
16. **One channel per draft** (the maker's own method, POETRY-PLAN §6). Revise only the space, then only the rhythm, and so on.

For the narrator and narrative (§2b):

17. **Who speaks, from the form alone.** Take a couplet. Join its clauses with *and*, then hang one under the other. Say in a line how the speaker changed, though no word about the speaker did (C13).
18. **Who must the hearer be?** Write four lines that make the reader someone you name, without naming them (Morton's homework named a stupid, very rich playboy). Then write four lines on the same scene that make the reader an onlooker instead. Ask someone else who each made them (C15).
19. **Drop the tag.** Write one line of thought with its tag ("he wondered whether …") and one without. Then read a caryā's last couplet with "Lūyī says" and without it, and say how far the singer stands from his song each time (C15–C16).
20. **Plot the story.** List five events in the order they happened. Tell them 3, 1, 4, 5, 2 in five lines, and say what the reader now knows early and waits for (C15).
21. **Three times, and once.** Tell one event three times, from three angles. Then tell something that happened every evening once, in one line. Say which feels hot and which feels like floating in the middle (C16).
22. **Begin, cycle, end.** Write a four-line story with strong aperture, a four-line story with strong closure, and a six-line story with strong development (C16–C17 homework).
23. **An epigram.** Write a short poem on one explicit topic. Say who narrates it and who it makes you (C14).
24. **Begin twice.** Write the opening of a poem, then a second opening in another voice. Read both and say which voice you trust (C17).
25. **A poem of marks.** Build a poem from one repeated mark, a step at a time: repeat it, make a row, break the row, draw a speech bubble round it, give it a title. Write what each step switched on: rhythm, lineation, a narrator, an image (CI).

### (iii) Questions for a Study room

- **Space:** Where is the blank, and what does the page do around the poem? Which marks tell you these are marks?
- **Lineation:** Where is the narrowest point? Does any break keep two readings alive, or make the voice hesitate?
- **Syntax:** Where do the line ends and clause ends coincide, and where do they cross?
- **Rhythm:** What weather is this poem? Does it whelm you, under, over or just right?
- **Rhyme:** Where does the scheme cool, and what does it cool before?
- **Imagery:**
  - Which images are on, which half-on, which switched on only to be switched off?
  - What is the brightest thing, and what does its light hide?
  - How long does the key image take to arrive?
  - Is the empty place a hole or an openness?
- **Tropes:** If you ran this metaphor as a rule, what would it make next? Is the poem joining things by likeness or by nearness?
- **Narrator:**
  - Whose mind is this, and what must you become to say it aloud? Can you live beside its pleasure?
  - Judging only from the page, who or what speaks? What does the poem leave unsaid about the speaker, and does the English say it?
  - Who do you have to be for the poem to make sense? Does the voice want you to see through its eyes, or against them?
  - Where do the narrator's view and the reader's position part company?
  - Whom does the poem hail, and how does it make you think it means you?
  - Is there a tag ("says", "thought")? What distance does it set, and what happens if you take it away?
  - Should you trust this I? Does the poem begin twice?
  - What kind of mind sees the world in these images?
- **Narrative:**
  - Where does it say no (A to not-A)? Which image only becomes an event when it comes back?
  - Is there a story at all (two events or more), or is this a speech?
  - In what order are things told, and in what order did they happen? What do you know before the people in the poem do?
  - What is told once that happened many times, and what is told many times that happened once?
  - Where does the telling approach real time, and does that feel like an ending?
  - How long does the poem keep you unsure what counts? Where does it cycle?
- **The architecture:** What is the poem inscribed in (paper, screen, air, a singing voice)? Does it speak of that thing?
- **For a translation:** Which channel did the English trade, and for which? What did its metaphor of the song carry across, and what did it leave on the bank? Did it add a tag, a hearer or a sex to the speaker that the song lacks?
