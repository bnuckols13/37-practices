# Carrying sound, wonder and feeling across: precedents for a "poetic layer" of *The 37 Practices of a Bodhisattva*

*Research brief compiled 27 Sept 2026. Every quote was checked, and the marker next to each shows how:*
- **✔** means I read the quote or claim on that page myself.
- **◐** means I saw it only in a search-engine snippet, because the page blocked automated fetching. Treat it as probable but check it by hand.
- **UNVERIFIED** means I could not confirm it.

## 0. The object: what exactly has to be carried

- **Structure (✔, counted from the Tibetan text at [Lotsawa House](https://www.lotsawahouse.org/bo/tibetan-masters/gyalse-thogme-zangpo/37-practices-all-bodhisattvas)):** 175 of the text's lines have 9 syllables. The fourth line of every verse (37 of 37) is a 4-syllable name of the practice followed by the fixed 5-syllable refrain **རྒྱལ་སྲས་ལག་ལེན་ཡིན** (*gyal-sé lak-len yin*), for example ཉན་སེམས་སྒོམ་པ + རྒྱལ་སྲས་ལག་ལེན་ཡིན ("listening-reflecting-meditating | is the heirs'-practice").
- **The key word (✔):** Khenpo Tsültrim Gyamtso: *"In Tibetan, the word for 'practice' literally translates as 'to bring into experience.'"* ([Nalandabodhi](https://nalandabodhi.org/2022/05/27/37-practices-of-a-bodhisattva-recitation/))
- **The site's three translations handle the refrain three different ways (✔, from `verses/1.html` in this repo):**
  - Pearcey (Lotsawa House, 2006) moves the refrain to the front: "The practice of all the bodhisattvas is to study, reflect and meditate…"
  - McLeod (2013) keeps the Tibetan hinge at the end of line 4: "Study, reflect, and meditate — this is the practice of a bodhisattva."
  - The Garchen recitation booklet ends with a separate sentence: "This is the way of a bodhisattva."

  Nobody has yet made that choice deliberately *for sound*.

## A. Hebrew Bible and Psalms

### A1. Robert Lowth, *De sacra poesi Hebraeorum* (lectures at Oxford from 1741; published 1753; English translation by G. Gregory 1787) ✔ [fair-use.org](http://fair-use.org/robert-lowth/lectures-on-the-sacred-poetry-of-the-hebrews/)
- **Problem:** Hebrew metre was lost. Lowth's Lecture III says "the rhythm and mode of scanning [are] totally lost". So what made the text poetry?
- **Technique:** He named *parallelismus membrorum*: the second line answers the first by saying the same thing again, the opposite, or an extension of it. The same lecture points to "a peculiar property in the prose versions of the Hebrew Poetry": parallelism survives even in prose translation.
- **Quote:** *"things for the most part shall answer to things, and words to words, as if fitted to each other by a kind of rule or measure"*. Lowth, as quoted in [Dobbs-Allsopp, *Divine Style*, ch. 4 (OBP 2024)](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0357/ch4.xhtml) ✔.
- **Why it survives translation:**
  - C. S. Lewis says parallelism is *"its chief formal characteristic one that does not disappear (as mere metre does) in translation"* ([Heidelblog, quoting *Reflections on the Psalms*](https://heidelblog.net/2023/05/saturday-psalm-series-parallelism-and-poetic-imagination-in-the-psalms-c-s-lewis-reflections-on-the-psalms/)) ✔.
  - Dobbs-Allsopp: *"semantics (meaning) is the linguistic element that most readily translates"* ([same chapter](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0357/ch4.xhtml)) ✔.
- **Lesson for the 37:** Tokme's lines lean heavily on paired images and paired clauses. If the English layer keeps each pairing inside its line, a structure that sounds like poetry comes across in any language.

### A2. Robert Alter: *The Art of Biblical Poetry* (1985), *The Book of Psalms* (2007), *The Hebrew Bible* (2018) ✔ dates: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Alter)
- **Problem:** Modern versions smooth out, clarify and lengthen the text. That kills its compactness and rhythm.
- **Technique:**
  - He keeps the Hebrew word order and parataxis.
  - He keeps repetition.
  - He matches the Hebrew stress count. In Psalm 119 he writes: *"The lines are rhythmically compact, usually having three accented syllables in the first verset and two in the second. The translation tries wherever possible to replicate this rhythm, but often there is one extra accent in the English."* (Alter's note, quoted by [E. Davis, NYU](https://cs.nyu.edu/~davise/personal/AlterTranslation.html)) ✔
- **Quote:** the "heresy" is *"the use of translation as a vehicle for explaining the Bible instead of representing it in another language"*. Alter's introduction to *The Five Books of Moses* (2004), quoted at [maudnewton.com](https://maudnewton.com/2007/01/biblical-translation-the-seed-of-abraham-or-the-offspring/) ✔. The page number is given as p. xiv in the [BYU Studies review](https://byustudies.byu.edu/article/the-five-books-of-moses-a-translation-with-commentary) ✔.
- **Lesson:** Count stresses, not syllables. Tolerate "one extra accent in the English". Keep all glossing out of the poetic layer, because the commentary layer already does the explaining.

### A3. Buber–Rosenzweig's German Bible (1925–1962) and Everett Fox, *The Five Books of Moses* (Schocken, 1995) ✔ [Fox's preface on Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/The_Five_Books_of_Moses,_by_Everett_Fox,_Translator's_Preface); date: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everett_Fox)
- **Problem:** The Bible *"is certainly an aural"* document, *"read aloud as a matter of course"*. Much of its meaning lives in echo and sound.
- **Technique 1, cola:** The text is printed in lines that are *"based primarily on spoken phrasing"*. For Buber each line was *"simultaneously a unit of breathing and of meaning"*.
- **Technique 2, the *Leitwort* (leading word):** Key words and roots are repeated *"like recurring themes in a piece of music"*, so that *"a leading-word operates on the basis of sound"*.
- **A warning from Fox himself:** he *"loosened the practice, sometimes overdone in B-R, of reproducing a Hebrew root by a single English one wherever it occurs."*
- **Quote:** *"cola divisions do facilitate reading aloud and make it possible for the listener to sense the text's inner rhythm"* ✔ (same source).
- **Lesson:** Break the English lines at breath points. Build a leading-word concordance (*lag len*, *rgyal sras*, *gzhan* "others", *bdag* "self"…) so the echoes across the 37 verses can be heard. Apply it with Fox's flexibility, not Buber's rigidity.

### A4. The King James Bible (1611), Coverdale's Psalter in the Book of Common Prayer, and Cranmer's Litany (1544)
- **Who/when:**
  - The KJV title page reads *"Appointed to be read in Churches"* ✔ [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_James_Version).
  - Coverdale's Psalter passed through the Great Bible into the Book of Common Prayer ✔ [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coverdale_Bible).
  - Cranmer's English Litany of 27 May 1544 was the first authorised vernacular service ✔ [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhortation_and_Litany).
- **Problem:** These were prose translations that a congregation had to *sing*.
- **Technique:**
  - **Pointing:** the editor marks each verse at its mid-point (*) and at the start of the cadence, *"so that the natural stress of the English falls on the melodic stress of the chant"* ✔ ([1928 BCP guide](https://1928commonprayer.org/offices/guide-to-anglican-chant)).
  - **Syllabic setting:** Cranmer wrote to Henry VIII that the music should not be *"full of notes, but, as near as may be, for every syllable a note"* ✔ ([Adoremus, letter text](https://adoremus.org/1999/11/quotmore-than-the-liberty-of-a-translatorquot/)). The same letter admits he used *"more than the liberty of a translator."*
  - The Litany is built on fixed congregational responses ("Good Lord, deliver us"), see [Living Church](https://livingchurch.org/covenant/good-lord-deliver-us/) ✔.
- **Lesson:** The poetic layer need not be metrical to be chantable. Marking where the voice turns in each line is enough. Tibetan chant is also largely one note per syllable.

### A5. The Bay Psalm Book (Cambridge, Mass., 1640): the cautionary case ✔ [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_Psalm_Book)
- **Problem:** Congregations needed psalms they could sing to tunes they already knew.
- **Technique:** They forced the Hebrew into English hymn metres. The preface argues that every nation may use *"the graver sort of verses of their own country poetry."*
- **Quote:** they *"attended conscience rather than elegance, fidelity rather than poetry"* ✔ ([preface, CPRC](https://cprc.co.uk/quotes/prefacebaypsalmbook/)).
- **Result:** Wikipedia's verdict is that the translations "are not particularly polished, and none have remained in use" ✔. The widely repeated claim that "most psalms are in common metre (8.6.8.6)" I found only on a [course blog](https://blogs.butler.edu/pca263fall2021/2021/09/08/the-common-meter-and-bay-psalm-book/). Treat it as **UNVERIFIED** against the 1640 text.
- **Lesson:** Squeezing the 37 verses into a familiar English metre (ballad or hymn metre) buys singability at the cost of both sense and beauty. Avoid it.

### A6. Joseph Gelineau and the Grail Psalms (1963; Revised Grail approved 2010) ✔ [Grail Psalms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grail_Psalms), [Gelineau psalmody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelineau_psalmody)
- **Problem:** Keep the Hebrew accentual rhythm in a vernacular that people can sing.
- **Technique:**
  - Each line has *"a prescribed number of accented syllables, even though the total number of syllables varies"* (sprung rhythm).
  - Printed editions put acute accents on the stressed syllables ("O Gód, you are my Gód").
  - Each psalm is framed by a short, metrical **antiphon** (refrain) that the congregation sings.
- **Quote (Gelineau, as quoted on Wikipedia):** *"the words must never be allowed to fall into set musical rhythmical patterns which are opposed to the natural relative lengths of their syllables"* ✔.
- **Lesson:** This is the closest model to the 9-syllable Tibetan line: a fixed stress count per line (suggest 4–5 English stresses), a free syllable count, visible stress marks, and a sung refrain.

### A7. Psalm 136: one refrain in every verse, like *gyal-sé lak-len yin*
- **Facts:**
  - All 26 verses end with the Hebrew refrain *ki le'olam hasdo*.
  - The KJV renders it identically every time: "for his mercy endureth for ever" ✔ [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psalm_136).
  - Alter uses "for His kindness is forever" throughout ✔ ([text as reproduced here](https://disjectamembra2014.blogspot.com/2016/12/robert-alter-book-of-psalms.html)).
- **Call and response?**
  - Alter's note: *"This repeated refrain clearly must have been an antiphonal response in the liturgy, perhaps by the whole community of worshippers"* ✔ (same page).
  - D. Moster: *"the first clause of each line was recited by a leader, and the second clause was recited by the community"* ✔ ([929chapters](https://929chapters.com/2009/03/30/psalm-136-hymnic-chant/)).
- **Layout:** The UBS translators' handbook advises: *"it will be helpful for public reading to print the refrain (line b) in each verse differently from the way in which line a is printed"*. It adds that some Hebrew editions set the refrains as a column at the margin ✔ ([Bratcher & Reyburn via TIPs](https://tips.translation.bible/story/translation-commentary-on-psalm-1361-1363/)).
- **Lesson:** Fix one English refrain and never vary it. Set it apart on the page (its own column, colour or indent). Offer a leader/all mode in which the reader says the verse and everyone answers with the refrain.

## B. Theory

### B1. Walter Benjamin, "The Task of the Translator" (1923; tr. Zohn) ✔ [PDF](https://open.unive.it/hitrade/translations/BenjaminTaskZohn.pdf)
- **Technique/ideal:** Literalness of syntax, so that the original shows through. *"The interlinear version of the Scriptures is the prototype or ideal of all translation."*
- **Quote:** *"A real translation is transparent; it does not cover the original, does not block its light"* ✔.
- **Lesson:** An interlinear view (Tibetan script, phonetics, word gloss, English) is not a crib. It is the form Benjamin considered ideal for sacred texts.

### B2. Schleiermacher (1813) and Venuti's foreignisation/domestication (1995)
- **Schleiermacher:** *"Either the translator leaves the author in peace, as much as possible, and moves the reader towards him"* ✔ ([Laudator](https://laudatortemporisacti.blogspot.com/2023/04/two-methods-of-translating.html)). He also warns that mixing the two methods is unreliable.
- **Venuti:** foreignisation is *"an ethnodeviant pressure on [target-language cultural] values to register the linguistic and cultural difference of the foreign text, sending the reader abroad"* ✔ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication_and_foreignization)).
- **Lesson:** The poetic layer should lean toward foreignising: the refrain stays strange and fixed, the Tibetan stays audible. The commentary layer can do the domesticating.

### B3. Dryden, Preface to *Ovid's Epistles* (1680)
- **The triad (◐, [Oxford Handbook snippet](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/43514/chapter/364248588)):**
  - *metaphrase*: "word by word, and Line by Line";
  - *paraphrase*: "Translation with Latitude… words… not so strictly follow'd as his sense";
  - *imitation*: the translator may "forsake them both".
- **Quote (on imitation):** it is *"the greatest wrong which can be done to the memory and reputation of the dead"* ✔ ([Logos course, quoting Dryden](http://courses.logos.it/EN/4_14.html)).
- **Lesson:** Label the site's layers by Dryden's three categories. Never present an "imitation" as the translation.

### B4. Nabokov, *Eugene Onegin* (1964) versus rhymed versions
- **Facts:**
  - Arndt's rhymed version (1963) won the Bollingen Prize.
  - Nabokov's four-volume literal version *"completely eschew[ed] melody and rhyme"*, but it includes his own poem in two Onegin stanzas so readers can feel the form ✔ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Onegin)).
- **Quote:** *"The clumsiest literal translation is a thousand times more useful than the prettiest paraphrase."* ("Problems of Translation", 1955) ✔ [Wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Vladimir_Nabokov).
- **Lesson:** Separate the literal layer from the form. Then add one small demonstration of the Tibetan form (for example a 9-syllable English specimen) rather than forcing every verse into it.

## C. Sound-first and oral poetry

### C1. Jerome Rothenberg, "total translation" of *The 17 Horse Songs of Frank Mitchell* (translated 1965–70) ✔ [Jacket2, D. Furr 2017](https://jacket2.org/article/total-translation)
- **Problem:** Earlier translators deleted the Navajo vocables (sounds without dictionary meaning) and kept only the "content".
- **Technique:**
  - He sought English equivalents for the vocables and for the sound-distortions of the words.
  - He sang the results as "multi-track recordings… of Rothenberg singing his 'total translations'" ✔ ([New Wilderness Audiographics, released 1977](https://audiographics.bandcamp.com/album/7707a-horse-songs)), treating the song as the unit to translate.
- **Quote:** *"The English translation should match the character of the Indian original… take that as a goal & don't worry about how literal you're otherwise being."* (*Pre-Faces*, p. 78, as quoted by Furr) ✔.
- **Lesson:** Treat the sound of Tibetan recitation (its drive, its particles, the closing *yin*) as part of the text to be carried. An audio layer is translation, not decoration.

### C2. Arthur Waley, *A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems* (1918) ✔ [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/42290/42290-h/42290-h.htm)
- **Technique:**
  - One English stress for each Chinese syllable, with free unstressed syllables in between.
  - No rhyme, because rhyme would "injure either the vigour of one's language or the literalness".
  - No added or removed images.
- **Quote:** *"Each character in the Chinese is represented by a stress in the English; but between the stresses unstressed syllables are of course interposed."* ✔
- **Also:** *"considering imagery to be the soul of poetry, I have avoided either adding images of my own or suppressing those of the original"* ✔.
- **Later statement:** In "Notes on Translation" (*Atlantic Monthly*, Nov. 1958 according to [Unz](http://www.unz.org/Pub/AtlanticMonthly-1958nov-00107); [WIST](https://wist.info/waley-arthur/54511/) says 1948) he describes wanting to *"re-embody… the emphasis, the tone, the eloquence of the original"* ✔.
- **UNVERIFIED:** that Waley himself called the method "sprung rhythm". I saw that only on a fan wiki that I could not open.
- **Lesson:** One stress for every two Tibetan syllables gives about 4–5 stresses per line. That is a rhythm English can hold.

### C3. Pound's *Cathay* (1915), Snyder's *Cold Mountain Poems* (Han Shan), David Hinton
- **Pound:**
  - Worked from Fenollosa's notes, "despite a complete lack of knowledge of the Chinese language" ✔ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathay_(poetry_collection))).
  - Eliot called him *"the inventor of Chinese poetry for our time"*, and the word is *inventor*, not translator ✔ ([Ploughshares](https://pshares.org/blog/the-modernist-revision-of-a-foreign-culture-in-ezra-pounds-cathay/)).
- **Snyder's technique, lived imagery:** *"internalizing and visualizing the taste of the whole scene – cold, wet, rocky, lonely, or whatever was called for"* ✔ ([quoted here](https://phillipjohnston.net/blog/2019/03/07/gary-snyders-cold-mountain-poems/)).
- **Hinton (interview with *The Sun*):**
  - Classical Chinese has no tenses and often no "I" ("Hear temple bell").
  - So he re-creates the poem, because otherwise there would be *"no poem in English, just a stale documentation of the original"* ✔ ([davidhinton.net](https://www.davidhinton.net/interview)).
- **Lesson:** Tokme's refrain lines have no subject. They are nominal statements (ཕ་ཡུལ་སྤོང་བ… "leaving-homeland… is the heirs' practice"). Resist inserting "you should". Let each image (the burning house, the jewel, the dream) arrive with its full sensory weight. Wonder comes from the image, not from adjectives.

### C4. Seamus Heaney, *Beowulf* (1999)
- **Problem:** Finding a speaking voice for an old oral poem, starting with its famous first word *Hwæt*.
- **Technique:** He wrote in the voice of his "big-voiced Scullion" relatives, whose words came "with a weighty directness", and translated *Hwæt* as "So."
- **Quote:** "So" works *"as an expression that obliterates all previous discourse and narrative, and at the same time functions as an exclamation calling for immediate attention"* ✔ (Heaney, quoted in [Shippey, TLS 1999](https://drmarkwomack.com/engl-2322/handouts/shippey-review/)).
- **Lesson:** Choose and name the voice of Tokme Zangpo, a hermit-monk speaking plainly to students, and test every verse by reading it aloud in that voice.

### C5. A. J. Arberry, *The Koran Interpreted* (1955), and the "meaning, not the Book" convention
- **Technique:**
  - He tried to imitate the rhythmic patterns.
  - He learned them by *listening* to recitation in Egypt.
  - His title concedes that *"the Koran is untranslatable"* ✔ ([preface excerpts](https://inreflectionofthequran.wordpress.com/excerpts-from-the-prefaces-of-arthur-john-arberrys-the-koran-interpreted/)).
- **Quote:** no earlier version had made *"a serious attempt… to imitate, however imperfectly, those rhetorical and rhythmical patterns which are the glory and the sublimity of the Koran"* ✔.
- **Pickthall (1930):** *"The Koran cannot be translated… It is only an attempt to present the meaning"*. He calls the original *"that inimitable symphony"* ✔ ([Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Meaning_of_the_Glorious_Koran_(1930)/Translator's_Foreword)).
- **Lesson:** Be honest in the title. Call the layer something like "The 37 Practices, sounded in English" rather than "the translation", and pair it with Tibetan audio.

### C6. Coleman Barks's Rumi: the cautionary tale
- **Facts:**
  - Barks *"neither spoke nor read Persian"* and rewrote earlier English versions ✔ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coleman_Barks)).
  - His book credits "the work of John Moyne, Nevit Ergin, A. J. Arberry, and Reynold Nicholson" ✔ ([title page quoted here](https://laurietobyedison.com/body-impolitic-blog/2020/05/spiritual-colonialism-the-erasure-of-islam-from-the-poetry-of-rumi/)).
  - Rozina Ali's *New Yorker* essay "The Erasure of Islam from the Poetry of Rumi" appeared on 5 Jan 2017 ✔ ([date](https://westernsufism.blogspot.com/2017/01/rumi-in-new-yorker.html); [URL](https://www.newyorker.com/books/page-turner/the-erasure-of-islam-from-the-poetry-of-rumi), not fetchable).
- **Quote (Barks, via Ali):** *"Religion is such a point of contention for the world."* ✔ ([as quoted here](https://amptoons.com/blog/?p=22601)).
- **Lesson:** Wonder that is cut loose from the source is easy to produce and it sells. Everything in the poetic layer must stay traceable to Tokme's Tibetan and to the Buddhist frame: refuge, bodhicitta, emptiness. Nothing should be universalised away.

## D. Tibetan specifically

### D1. Classical Tibetan poetics and the culture this text lives in
- **"Beautiful sound":** Chögyam Trungpa (*Loka 2*, Naropa, 1976): *"The Tibetan word for poetry is nyen-ngag which means 'beautiful sound' or 'beautiful utterance' or 'beautiful voice.'"* ✔ ([Beezone transcript](https://beezone.com/current/tibetan-poetics-chogyam-trungpa-rinpoche.html))
- **Metre (Trungpa, same source):**
  - *"The traditional nyen-ngag is based on nine syllables… The nine-syllable line can be given different stress points"*.
  - Seven-syllable lines *"express emotions more"*.
  - Nine-syllable lines *"are more eloquent. You can use metaphor to stretch them out"* ✔.
  - He praises the colloquial songs' *"blunt and sincere"* quality ✔.
- **Metre and stanza (◐, [*Michigan Quarterly Review*, "Notes on Treatise on Passion"](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/m/mqr/act2080.0052.213/--notes-on-treatise-on-passion-a-tibetan-poem?g=mqrg%3Brgn%3Dmain%3Bview%3Dfulltext%3Bxc%3D1), page returns 403):**
  - Lines are "usually seven or nine" syllables.
  - "The standard meter was trochaic."
  - Since the 13th century the four-line stanza has been the basic unit, "the Tibetan version of the Sanskrit śloka".
  - Poets add repeated syllables, alliteration and assonance.
- **Tibetans themselves re-metred their sources:**
  - The translators of the *Subhāṣitaratna* *"decided to use only the most common type of Tibetan verse in which the line consists of seven syllables"*, turning Sanskrit stanzas into 4–12 Tibetan lines ✔ ([M. Hahn, *Pacific World*](http://www.shin-ibs.edu/documents/pwj3-9/08Hahn39.pdf)).
  - A review in *Tricycle* adds that English's "polysyllabic words… make this method impossible for us" ✔ ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/guide-bodhisattva-way-life/)).
- **Ornament theory:**
  - Sakya Paṇḍita (1182–1251) translated parts of Daṇḍin's *Kāvyādarśa* (*snyan ngag me long*) ✔ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakya_Pandita), "Kāvyādarśa of Dandin (partial)").
  - That he introduced it in his *Gateway to Learning*, concentrating on the ornaments of chapter 2, is ◐ (from a [van der Kuijp](https://texts.mandala.library.virginia.edu/book_pubreader/15882) snippet).
- **Recitation and memorisation:**
  - Nalandabodhi's Lama Rabten Tshering: *"If we are not only able to recite but also able to memorize… we will readily recall and carry the deeper meaning… in our hearts"* ✔ ([Nalandabodhi](https://nalandabodhi.org/2022/05/27/37-practices-of-a-bodhisattva-recitation/)).
  - Dreyfus, *The Sound of Two Hands Clapping* (2003), treats memorisation as the base of monastic training. I verified this only at the level of the book's description ([Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/california-scholarship-online/book/28596)), not a quote.
- **Lesson:** The text is built to be *sounded and memorised*: 9-syllable trochaic lines, four-line stanzas and a hammered refrain. The English layer should be judged by whether it can be memorised and chanted, not only by whether it can be read.

### D2. Nālandā Translation Committee (Chögyam Trungpa; founded 1975 according to NTC's own site, ◐) and *The Rain of Wisdom* (1980)
- **Problem:** Western students had to chant liturgy without knowing Tibetan.
- **Method (✔, [L. Mermelstein, "Chögyam the Translator"](https://www.chronicleproject.com/chogyam-the-translator/)):**
  - *"The practitioners were going to read and chant in English"*, with no interlinear Tibetan.
  - *"We strove to be as literal and accurate as possible."*
  - At Seminary they spent hours *"experimenting with different styles of chanting, drum patterns, gong ringing"*.
  - Liturgy books were designed to lie flat for practice.
- ***Rain of Wisdom* (1980):** Trungpa's foreword says the songs are *"based on the melody of circumstance… They are cosmic onomatopoeia"*. They were read annually at Milarepa's parinirvana ✔ ([Foreword](https://www.chronicleproject.com/forward-to-rain-of-wisdom/)).
- **Tsadra 2014:** A workshop, "Translating Sadhanas & Rituals" (Gethin, Mermelstein, Klein; 4 Oct 2014), is ✔ ([handout PDF](https://conference.tsadra.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/6-Larry-Mermelstein-Vajrayogini-Sadhana-invocation-Tibetan.pdf)). Its listed topics are ◐ ([session page](https://conference.tsadra.org/session/translating-sadhanas-and-rituals/)): Gethin on *"the demand for chantability"*, Klein on *"melody as transmission"*, and Mermelstein on choices that *"elicit somatic experience"*.
- **Lesson:** Use literal English, then test and refine it by actually chanting it together, as NTC did.

### D3. Khenpo Tsültrim Gyamtso Rinpoche (1934–2024) and his translators (Ari Goldfield, Jim Scott, Rose Taylor Goldfield, Michele Martin)
- **What is verified (✔):**
  - The dohas of Milarepa and Khenpo Rinpoche were rendered *"into colloquial English"*, and many *"have been set to Western-style music so they can be sung"* ([Lion's Roar](https://www.lionsroar.com/songs-of-milarepa/)).
  - The Marpa Foundation hosts a "Melodies for Songs of Realization" audio library ([KTGR digital library](http://ktgr.dscloud.me/moodle/course/view.php?id=109)).
  - Songs carry credits such as "translated by Ari Goldfield and arranged by Robyn Traill" ([Seven Yogas](https://ktgrinpoche.org/songs/seven-yogas)).
  - Their colloquial English refrains are exactly the problem the 37 face. Milarepa's "Ten Important Points" ends its instruction lines with "—that's so important", and the last one with "now that's important, too!" ([ktgrinpoche.org](https://www.ktgrinpoche.org/songs/ten-important-points)). Gotsangpa's "Seven Yogas" ends "just like this".
  - Khenpo Rinpoche's whole three-line song "Why We Sing" (tr. Goldfield, 2007) reads: *"Singing the key instructions / Isn't meaningless, / It's the lineage tradition."* ([ktgrinpoche.org](https://www.ktgrinpoche.org/songs/why-we-sing))
- **UNVERIFIED:** any published statement of method by Goldfield or Scott about matching English syllable counts to *Tibetan* melodies. I found none. Their stated practice is Western-style settings.
- **Lesson:** A short, colloquial refrain that is fully sung ("that's so important") works in English. It shows the refrain can be *light* and still be the emotional anchor.

### D4. Anne Carolyn Klein, *Heart Essence of the Vast Expanse* (Longchen Nyingthig liturgy)
- **Technique:** A *"chantable English translation [that] matches the meter of the Tibetan original and thus can be sung to the traditional Tibetan melodies"*. This is ◐, from a publisher blurb in a [search snippet](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/2473716.Heart_Essence_of_the_Vast_Expanse).
- **The 2020 edition (✔, [PRH](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/224421/heart-essence-of-the-vast-expanse-by-introduced-and-translated-by-anne-carolyn-klein/)):** it includes *"downloadable audio of the chants in English"* alongside the chants "written in English and Tibetan".
- **Lesson:** This is proof that meter-matched English sung to Tibetan melodies is possible and published. It is the most direct model for a sung version of the 37.

### D5. FPMT: a hybrid of Tibetan for the melody and English for the meaning ✔ [Lama Chöpa Audio Guide](https://shop.fpmt.org/assets/images/eproducts/lamachopaguide.pdf)
- **Quote:** *"Verses chanted in Tibetan are emphasized for receiving the blessing of the tunes… Verses or sections recited in English are recommended… due to the importance of understanding the meaning"* ✔.
- **Lesson:** Chant the refrain (or the whole verse) in Tibetan and read the English. This carries the Tibetan melody without distorting the English.

### D6. Alexander Berzin: change the meter, but keep dignity (seminar, Tallinn 2006) ✔ [StudyBuddhism](https://studybuddhism.com/en/advanced-studies/vajrayana/tantra-theory/overview-of-tantra/tantra-the-role-of-translation-and-concepts-in-practice)
- **Quote:** *"Tibetans changed the meter, and the Chinese changed the meter, so we can change the meter as well. The point is to make it beautiful and harmonious."*
- **His warnings and distinctions:**
  - Metre and melody are *"two separate things"*.
  - Rhythmic recitation *"shapes the breath"*.
  - He warns against ritual texts set to guitar that sound *"like some hippie singing a folk song"*, and against sing-song metre ("Mary had a little lamb").
- **Lesson:** Solve the metre problem first and the melody problem second. Register is the real risk: aim for dignity, not ditty.

## Techniques we could use

1. **The Psalm 136 refrain (call and response).** Set the refrain apart on the page (its own colour or right-aligned column, per the UBS handbook). Offer a "leader / all" recitation mode in which lines 1–3 plus the practice-name are the call and the refrain is the response. *(A7, A4 Litany, A6 antiphon)*
2. **One fixed refrain, never varied.** Choose a single English refrain and use it word for word 37 times, as the KJV did 26 times. Consider a short chant form next to the full reading form. *(A7, D3)*
3. **Keep the hinge line.** Preserve the Tibetan line 4 = [practice-name] + [refrain] rather than moving the refrain to the front (Pearcey). McLeod already does this. *(Section 0)*
4. **Stress-count lines.** About 4–5 English stresses per 9-syllable Tibetan line, with a free number of unstressed syllables, and one extra stress tolerated. *(Waley C2, Alter A2, Gelineau A6)*
5. **Visible stress marks.** Optional acute accents on stressed syllables (Grail style) so any reader can find the beat. *(A6)*
6. **Pointing for chant.** Mark the mid-line turn and the cadence so any English line can go on a single reciting tone, one syllable per note. *(A4 Coverdale/Cranmer)*
7. **Breath-line layout.** Four lines per verse, each broken at breath and meaning units, never run on as prose. *(Fox/Buber A3)*
8. **A leading-word concordance.** Render each recurring key term (*lag len*, *rgyal sras*, *gzhan*, *bdag*, *sems*) consistently across all 37 verses so the echoes can be heard, and publish the map. Loosen it where it strains. *(A3)*
9. **No explanation in the poem.** No glosses or added "you should". Explanation lives in the existing commentary layer. *(Alter A2; Hinton C3 on missing pronouns)*
10. **Images at full strength, none added.** Keep every simile and metaphor (Tibetan ornament, *rgyan*) and add none, because the wonder is in the images. *(Waley C2, Snyder C3, D1)*
11. **Parallelism kept intact.** Preserve Tokme's paired clauses and images inside each line; this is the part that reliably survives translation. *(Lowth/Lewis A1)*
12. **An interlinear "shine-through" view.** Toggle between Tibetan script, phonetics, word-by-word gloss and English. *(Benjamin B1, Nabokov B4)*
13. **An audio-first layer.** Tibetan recitation, an English reading in a chosen voice, and a hybrid (Tibetan refrain, English verse). *(Rothenberg C1, Arberry C5, FPMT D5)*
14. **Honest labels for each layer.** Name them in Dryden's terms: literal "metaphrase", "sounded" version, and at most one "imitation" specimen. Never let a version stand in for the source. *(B3, C5, Barks C6)*
15. **A chosen voice and register, tested aloud.** Plain, "blunt and sincere" monastic speech, dignified and not twee. Read every draft aloud and, eventually, try singing it to a Tibetan tune (Klein) before publishing. *(Heaney C4, Trungpa D1, Berzin D6, Klein D4)*
