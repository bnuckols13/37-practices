## 1. Sonnet 29: the text

### (a) The 1609 Quarto

- **Where it is.** Sonnet 29 runs from the foot of sig. C2v onto C3r of *Shake-speares Sonnets* (1609). I read lines 3–14 on the page image of C3r in Sidney Lee's 1905 facsimile of the Bodleian Malone copy ✔ ([archive.org, image n92](https://archive.org/details/cu31924013134295)). The text below follows the Internet Shakespeare Editions old-spelling transcription by Hardy M. Cook and Ian Lancashire ✔ ([ISE](https://internetshakespeare.uvic.ca/doc/Son_Q1/section/Sonnet%2028~Sonnet%2030/index.html)). I checked it against Alden's Variorum ✔ ([Alden 1916, p. 82](https://archive.org/stream/ocm00234756.2162.emory.edu/ocm00234756_2162_djvu.txt)) and the facsimile ✔. Long s is printed as s. The facsimile also closes up "him,like" and "art,and".

```
When in disgrace with Fortune and mens eyes,
I all alone beweepe my out-cast state,
And trouble deafe heauen with my bootlesse cries,
And looke vpon my selfe and curse my fate.
Wishing me like to one more rich in hope,
Featur'd like him, like him with friends possest,
Desiring this mans art, and that mans skope,
With what I most inioy contented least,
Yet in these thoughts my selfe almost despising,
Haplye I thinke on thee, and then my state,
(Like to the Larke at breake of daye arising)
From sullen earth sings himns at Heauens gate,
   For thy sweet loue remembred such welth brings,
   That then I skorne to change my state with Kings.
```

- **Three witnesses agree, one does not.** ISE, Alden and the page image all read "cries," at line 3 and "fate." at line 4. Wikisource's page transcription swaps the two stops ("cries." / "fate,") ✔ ([page 17](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Shakespeare%27s_Sonnets,_Never_before_Imprinted_(1609).djvu/17)). That is an error; do not build on it.
- **What the Quarto's marks do.**
  - The full stop after "fate" makes two sentences, 1–4 and 5–14. Read as grammar, lines 1–4 are a *When* clause with no main clause. Read as speech, the stop is a deep breath after the first quatrain.
  - There is no comma after "When". Lines 8, 12 and 13 end on commas where modern editors print a semicolon, a semicolon and nothing.
  - Four capitals: Fortune, Larke, Heauens, Kings. Alden thought editors wrong to lower Fortune, a personified goddess ✔ (p. 83).
  - The spellings keep the syllable count. "Featur'd" has two syllables, "remembred" three, and "possest" is spelled for its rhyme.
  - We do not know whether this pointing is Shakespeare's, a scribe's or the compositor's (UNVERIFIED either way). Percy Simpson's *Shakespearian Punctuation* (1911) argued that the brackets in the Sonnets are deliberate ✔ (below).
- **One conjecture to know.** Staunton proposed "Yea" for "Yet" at line 9 ✔ (Alden's collation). It is the one small change that would erase the turn.

### (b) A modern-spelling text

This is the text printed in the order of service from which Judi Dench read at Westminster Abbey on 26 April 2022 ✔ ([order of service, p. 6](https://www.westminster-abbey.org/media/z5xelvbv/order-of-service-sir-john-gielgud-memorial-dedication.pdf)):

```
When, in disgrace with fortune and men's eyes,
I all alone beweep my outcast state,
And trouble deaf heaven with my bootless cries,
And look upon myself and curse my fate,
Wishing me like to one more rich in hope,
Featured like him, like him with friends possessed,
Desiring this man's art and that man's scope,
With what I most enjoy contented least;
Yet in these thoughts myself almost despising,
Haply I think on thee, and then my state,
(Like to the lark at break of day arising
From sullen earth) sings hymns at heaven's gate;
   For thy sweet love remembered such wealth brings
   That then I scorn to change my state with kings.
```

The Folger Shakespeare's online text has the same words. It has no comma after "When" and no brackets at all: "Like to the lark at break of day arising / From sullen earth, sings hymns at heaven's gate;" ✔ ([Folger](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/shakespeares-sonnets/read/29/)).

### (c) The parenthesis: who rises from sullen earth?

| pointing | who prints it | what rises | what sings, from where | the syntax | in the voice |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Quarto:** "(Like … arising) / From sullen earth sings" | 1609; Wyndham kept it; Simpson defends it ✔ (Alden) | the lark, from no place named | "my state" sings *from sullen earth* up to heaven's gate | the bracket is one whole line, an aside between subject and verb; "From sullen earth" goes with *sings* | line 11 closes on itself as an aside, and line 12 starts low, on the ground, and climbs. The man stays on the earth; only his song goes up |
| **Malone's:** "(Like … arising / From sullen earth) sings" | Malone and eight other editions in Alden's collation ✔; the Westminster Abbey text ✔ | the lark, from sullen earth | "my state" sings at heaven's gate | the simile runs over the line end, and *sings* arrives as the verb the ear has waited for since "my state", two lines back | one flight across lines 11–12, with no breath at "arising"; a small landing at "earth)", then the peak on "heaven's gate" |
| **Commas:** "Like … arising / From sullen earth, sings" | most editions in Alden's list (Globe, Cambridge, Dowden and others) ✔; Folger today ✔ | the lark, from sullen earth | the state | as Malone's, with the aside less marked | as Malone's |

- **The case for the Quarto.** Simpson asked, "What right has an editor to alter this". Detaching the state from the earth, he said, "breaks a subtle link with the thought of the opening lines" ✔ ([Simpson 1911, p. 95](https://archive.org/download/cu31924013165133/cu31924013165133_djvu.txt)). The outcast who began on the ground sings from it.
- **The case against.** Alden replied that the lark does not sing from the earth but rises from it. "It seems safer to follow the maker of the simile than the printer of the parentheses" ✔ (p. 84).
- **Both at once.** The phrase "may be taken both with this and with the preceding line" ✔ ([shakespeares-sonnets.com](https://www.shakespeares-sonnets.com/sonnet/29)). The Quarto's bracket sends "From sullen earth" to the singing state. The line's place, straight after the lark, lets a reader give it to the bird. This is a syntax that points two ways, made by punctuation and not by symbol (§6e). Most target languages force a choice, and a translation should say which one it took.

## 2. Close reading on Morton's board

The order is Morton's: structure, texture, perception, narrator, narrative. The heats are my trained reading. Beside them are the engine's figures, from a scratch run of `lib/english.mjs` and `englishBoard` (recipe in §2i).

### (a) The board

| channel | heat | engine | why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **lineation** | 0.8 hot | 0.84 | Fourteen pentameters: three quatrains and a couplet. Every line is a phrase, and every line ends on a mark in the Quarto. The one run-on (11→12) exists only in the modern pointing; the Quarto's bracket closes line 11 on itself, so the Quarto runs hotter here. |
| **syntax** | 0.35 mild | 0.95 | One sentence (modern) whose main clause is held back to line 10: hypotaxis, a mind thinking. Inside it, the envy list (5–8) is four participle phrases side by side, which runs hot. The engine counts commas as joins, so it hears only that inner heat. |
| **rhythm** | 0.8 hot | 0.78 | A strong iambic groove. The deviations are few and placed: trochees at 5, 10 and 11, feminine endings at 9 and 11, spondees at 1, 3 and 13. |
| **rhyme** | 0.85 hot | 0.89 | Seven pairs, all full rhymes in 1600 (§2c). One sound, -ate, serves two quatrains, and one word, *state*, rhymes twice. A hidden chime, -sing, runs through 9–14. |

Sonnet 29 is a Baked Alaska: hot sound (lineation, rhythm, rhyme) wrapped round a cool, thinking sentence.

- **Six of seven perfect today, seven of seven then.** Crystal: "Ninety-six of Shakespeare's sonnets contain rhymes which do not work in Modern English" ✔ (abstract, p. 295).
- **Vendler's couplet tie.** Vendler hears *sing* hidden in *despising* and *arising* before it arrives in *sings*: the "disgruntled present participles" *wishing* and *desiring* give way, and the poem "fairly carols" ◐ (Vendler 1997, as quoted by Tom Paulin, [*LRB* 20:2, 1998](https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v20/n02/tom-paulin/in-the-workshop)). The engine's letter-string search recovers the same chime by itself (§6c).

### (d) Words struck again, and how each changes

| word | lines | the change |
|---|---|---|
| **state** | 2, 10, 14 | At 2, "my outcast state" is a condition to weep over, the object of *beweep*, rhymed with *fate*. At 10, "my state … sings": the same word is now the subject of a singing verb, rhymed with *gate*. At 14, "change my state with kings" means estate and rank, even a realm ✔ (shakespeares-sonnets.com), and the word has left the line end. Vendler calls it the key word, and notes it is missing from the second quatrain, where the speaker looks only at other men's states ◐ (via Paulin). |
| **like** | 5, 6, 6, 11 | At 5, "Wishing me like to one more rich in hope": comparison as envy. At 6, "like him, like him": envy's stammer, twice in four syllables. At 11, "Like to the lark": the same two words, *like to*, as in line 5, now a simile that lifts. The comparison that tormented becomes the one that frees (our reading). |
| **heaven** | 3, 12 | "deaf heav'n" is one squeezed syllable, a heaven that will not hear "bootless cries". "heaven's gate" has two open syllables, a heaven reached by "hymns". The word's body opens as its meaning does (our reading). |
| **then** | 10, 14 | At 10, "and then my state": *then* means next, at that moment, a word of time. At 14, "That then I scorn": at such times, therefore, a word of consequence. |
| **myself** | 4, 9 | "look upon myself and curse my fate", then "myself almost despising": the self first looked at, then despised, with *almost* as the hinge. |
| **-sing** | 9, 11, 12 → 13, 14 | despi*sing*, ari*sing*, *sing*s, then br*ings* and k*ings* (Vendler ◐). |

Some words are not repeated but turned into their answer: *cries* (3) become *hymns* (12); "curse my fate" (4) becomes "scorn to change my state" (14); *rich* (5) becomes *wealth* (13).

### (e) Syntax

- **One sentence (modern), and the main verb waits.** *When* (1) governs four verbs: *beweep*, *trouble*, *look*, *curse*. A chain of participles then hangs on "I": *Wishing*, *Featured*, *Desiring*, *contented*, and *despising* at line 9. The main clause, "Haply I think on thee", comes only at line 10. Then *and then my state … sings* (10–12), then *For … such wealth brings / That then I scorn* (13–14), a cause and a result.
- **Parataxis inside hypotaxis.** Lines 5–8 are side by side, the mind going round, hot. But the whole list hangs from a *When* that has not yet paid out: the frame is cool, the inside hot. Morton's two syntaxes (parataxis hot, hypotaxis cool) are here at once.
- **The Quarto differs.** Its full stop after "fate" leaves lines 1–4 without a main clause, and line 5 starts again on a participle. That is a breath in speech and a broken sentence in grammar.
- **For a translator.** The held-back main verb is the poem's suspense. A verb-final language (Bengali is one) can hold a clause naturally. English holds it with *When* and participles. Keep the delay whatever the grammar costs.

### (f) Perception

- **The order of things seen.** Fortune (a goddess, capitalised in the Quarto), men's eyes, a deaf heaven, the speaker looking at himself, and other men reduced to what they have (hope, features, friends, art, scope). There is nothing to *see* until line 11.
- **The only image is the lark:** a bird, daybreak, a rise from earth, a gate in the sky. The eye opens exactly when the mood turns. Morton's "sensory forcing" arrives late, and upward.
- **The simile is flagged.** "Like to" says it is comparing. So Morton's "no symbolism" holds without strain: the lark means by moving, not by code.
- **Its ancestry.** Malone pointed to Shakespeare's own *Cymbeline* 2.3, "Hark! hark! the lark at heaven's gate sings". Reed pointed to Lyly's *Campaspe* (1584): "None but the lark so shrill and clear; / Now at heaven's gate she claps her wings" ✔ (both quoted by Alden, p. 84). A stock image, made new by where it stands.

### (g) Narrator and addressee

- **"I" is there from line 2, "all alone".** For nine lines there is no one addressed: self-talk, overheard.
- **"Thee" arrives at line 10, by chance.** "Haply" means "by chance, by a happy stroke of luck" ✔ (shakespeares-sonnets.com). *Hap* is luck, and the same root is in *happy*, so a modern ear hears "happily" as well. (YouTube's caption engine wrote "happily" for it: §5b.) The beloved enters the poem the way luck enters a life. "Thy" follows once, at 13.
- **No name, no signature.** The "I" is unnamed. Compare Sonnets 18 and 136 (§3, §6e).

### (h) Narrative: the turn and the couplet

- **From A to not-A:** disgrace to joy, outcast to above kings.
- **The turn at "Yet" (line 9)** is where an Italian sonnet turns, from octave to sestet, inside an English rhyme scheme of three quatrains and a couplet. The sense runs 8 + 6 while the rhyme runs 4 + 4 + 4 + 2: an Italian thought in English clothes. `morton-in-depth.md` notes it turns twice, at 9 and at the couplet ✔ (repo).
- **The turn is slow.** Line 9 still looks down ("myself almost despising"). The lift comes with "Haply" (10) and peaks at "heaven's gate" (12). *Yet* is the hinge; *Haply* is the swing.
- **The couplet.** It opens on *For*: it explains rather than trips. Morton's "thinking, thinking, thinking, oh, whoops!" belongs to 130, whose couplet reverses the poem (class 7, 31 Jan 2012 ✔ [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/HowToReadAnyPoemAnywhereClass7); content ◐ via `morton-in-depth.md`). In 29 the trip comes early and goes upward. But the couplet does come down from heaven to *wealth* and *kings*: the poem that escaped Fortune ends by counting riches. That is a small second turn, back to earth (our reading).

### (i) Where the engine's ear agrees, and where it does not

- **Recipe (scratch, not in the repo).** Import `lib/ear.mjs`. Then call `en.scan(line)` per line and `en.rhymeLines(a, b)` per rhyme pair. For `englishBoard(unit, lines)`, give the unit hand-made groups by rhyme pair (1/3, 2/4, …, 13/14), because the board pairs lines as caryā couplets.
- **It agrees on** the trochees at 5, 10 and 11; the feminine endings at 9 and 11, and the feminine rhyme; six perfect rhymes and one off rhyme; *heaven's* as two syllables in 12; and the spondee "men's eyes".
- **It disagrees, and why:**
  1. **Two-syllable light words count as one.** `wordStress` returns `[0]` for every word in `LIGHT`, so *upon* (also *into*, *onto*, *until*, *unless*, *because*, *whether*) loses a syllable. Line 4 scans as nine.
  2. **English words missing from CMU fall to the IAST stress guesser.** It treats each vowel letter as a syllable, so *beweep* and *bootless* get three. It needs an English digraph rule (*ee, oo, ea, ai*) before the IAST rule.
  3. **Verse elision.** CMU gives modern counts: *heaven* 2, *desiring* 4, *fire* 2 (Sonnet 73), *temperate* 3 (18). The fix is an elision list, tried when a line runs one syllable over.
  4. **Stresses the rhetoric needs.** *This/that* (antithesis) and *then* (the turn word) are never promoted mid-line.
  5. **The syntax channel counts commas and *and* as parataxis.** It cannot see nine lines hanging from one *When*, so it reads 29 at 0.95.
  6. **Feminine endings are read from CMU phones.** So *fire / expire* (73) and *temperate / date* (18) are wrongly called feminine.
  7. **The engine hears as a modern ear does.** Its only off rhymes in Sonnets 18, 29 and 116 are *temperate/date*, *possessed/least*, *love/remove*, *come/doom* and *proved/loved*. Every one is in Crystal's index of rhymes that were full in Shakespeare's pronunciation ✔ (pp. 304–305). This qualifies `morton-in-depth.md` §4c, which says 116 "cools exactly on its love sounds". That holds for a modern reader, probably not for the first readers.

## 3. Contrast sonnets: 18, 73, 116, 130

| sonnet | "thou/thee" first | sentences* | images | turn | couplet | engine L / S / Rh / Ry |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **18** | line 1 | 2 | many: a summer's day, winds, the buds of May, the eye of heaven, a gold complexion, shade | "But" (9) | "So long lives this, and this gives life to thee": the poem names itself as what lasts | .91 / .96 / .76 / .89 |
| **73** | line 1 | 4, one per quatrain | three, one per quatrain, each shorter in time: autumn boughs ("Bare ruined choirs"), twilight, a dying fire | the couplet ("This thou perceiv'st") | turns to "thy love" | .71 / .68 / .63 / .92 |
| **116** | never | 6 | a star and a ship, rosy lips, a sickle | a weak "But" (12), then the couplet's wager | "If this be error …": the poem stakes its own writing | .70 / .72 / .61 / .76 |
| **130** | never ("my mistress") | 4 | the Petrarchan blazon, each image switched off | "And yet" (13) | Morton's trip | .79 / .86 / .74 / .94 |
| **29** | **line 10** | **1** | **one: the lark** | **"Yet" (9)**, and the couplet | "For … That then": it explains, and lands | .84 / .95 / .78 / .89 |

\*Counted on the modern texts used for the run (L = lineation, S = syntax, Rh = rhythm, Ry = rhyme).

- **18** is hot everywhere, and it signs itself: the plan's "sonnet that says it will outlive its subject". *Temperate / date* was a full rhyme in 1600 ✔ (Crystal).
- **73** makes each quatrain one image. Its syntax is cooler (*which*, *as*, *that*), and its monosyllables are heavy ("Bare ruined choirs, where late the sweet birds sang").
- **116** and **130** are Morton's class 7 pair, both built on negation (`morton-in-depth.md` §4c). 116 has three rhymes that cool to us and were full then: *love/remove*, *loved/proved*, *doom/come* ✔ (Crystal lists all three under 116). 130's couplet is the whoops.
- **What makes 29 different:** the addressee arrives late, and by chance; it is one sentence, with the main verb held to line 10; it has one image, placed at the turn; the turn falls in the Italian place, carried by the only feminine rhymes; and the key word rhymes twice. It is the one of the five that most needs a speaker to hold a single thought across fourteen lines.

## 4. How it was practised

### (a) The grammar school: copia, double translation, the commonplace book

- **Copia.** Erasmus's *De duplici copia verborum ac rerum* was first printed by Josse Bade in Paris in 1512 ✔ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copia:_Foundations_of_the_Abundant_Style)). In Book I, chapter 33, it varies one sentence, *Tuae litterae me magnopere delectarunt*, "Your letter pleased me greatly".
  - **The count is not settled.** King and Rix's translation (Marquette, 1963) gives 150, as reported in a [1964 review](https://archive.dartmouthalumnimagazine.com/article/1964/6/1/on-copia-of-words-and-ideas) ✔. Wikipedia and a Folger Institute blog post give 195 ✔ ([JHI Blog, 2015](https://www.jhiblog.org/2015/11/04/education-in-excess-the-folger-institutes-theatres-of-learning/)). A snippet says the 1512 edition numbered them i–cxlvi ◐.
  - I could not count them myself. Say "some 150 to 195, by edition and by what counts as one variant".
- **Double translation.** Roger Ascham, *The Scholemaster* (1570), sets this exercise ✔ ([Arber's reprint, pp. 26–27](https://archive.org/details/scholemasterwrit00aschuoft)). The child translates a Cicero letter into English, "Then shewing it to his master, let the master take from him his latin booke, and pausing an houre, at the least, than let the childe translate his owne Englishe into latin againe, in an other paper booke." The master then lays it beside Cicero's and praises what came back right. This is the most useful Tudor exercise for this edition (§6d).
- **The commonplace book.** Pupils gathered sentences under heads to reuse them. Meres's *Palladis Tamia: Wits Treasury* (1598) is itself such a book ◐ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palladis_Tamia)), and it is where the Sonnets are first mentioned.
- **Shakespeare's own schooling.** It is presumed, not recorded. I did not read Baldwin's *Small Latine and Lesse Greeke* (1944) (UNVERIFIED here).

### (b) Circulated among friends

- **Meres, 1598:** "the sweete wittie soule of Ovid lives in mellifluous and hony-tongued Shakespeare, witnes his Venus and Adonis, his Lucrece, his sugred Sonnets among his private friends" ✔ (as transcribed in Alden's appendix, u/v normalised). Two caveats ✔ (Alden): Mackail doubted Meres meant this series at all, and in 1599 Jaggard's *The Passionate Pilgrim* printed versions of Sonnets 138 and 144.
- **What it means for a maker.** These were poems passed hand to hand before they were a book, like songs before a manuscript.

### (c) The English sonnet was born as translation

- **Wyatt from Petrarch.** Petrarch's *Rerum vulgarium fragmenta* 190 ✔ ([it.wikisource](https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/Canzoniere_(Rerum_vulgarium_fragmenta)/Una_candida_cerva_sopra_l%27erba), between 189 and 191) opens: "Una candida cerva sopra l'erba / verde m'apparve, con duo corna d'oro … levando 'l sole" ("A white hind on the green grass appeared to me, with two horns of gold … as the sun rose"). A dawn vision, again. Wyatt made "Whoso list to hunt, I know where is an hind" from it ◐ (text from web transcriptions, not checked against Egerton MS 2711).
- **What Wyatt changed.**
  - Petrarch's collar reads "Nessun mi tocchi … libera farmi al mio Cesare parve" ("Let no one touch me … it pleased my Caesar to make me free") ✔.
  - Wyatt's reads "Noli me tangere, for Caesar's I am, / And wild for to hold, though I seem tame" ◐. Free becomes owned.
  - Petrarch's vision, where the poet falls into the water and she vanishes, becomes a hunt the speaker gives up.
  - Wyatt kept Petrarch's octave (abba abba) and most often closed with cddc ee, ending on a couplet ✔ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Wyatt_(poet))). His and Surrey's sonnets reached print in Tottel's miscellany (1557) ✔, titled *Songes and Sonettes* ◐.
- **Surrey's form.** Surrey made the form Sonnet 29 uses: three alternating quatrains and a couplet, easier in English, which is poorer in rhymes ◐ ([Ohio State](https://ohiostate.pressbooks.pub/engl2201/chapter/the-sonnet-then-and-now/)).
- **The lesson.** Sonnet 29's form is a translator's form. The couplet that trips is where an Italian shape was bent to fit English.
- **For ten-line songs.** Hopkins's "Curtal-Sonnets" are "constructed in proportions resembling those of the sonnet proper, namely 6 + 4 instead of 8 + 6, with however a halfline tailpiece" ✔ ([*Poems*, 1918, Author's Preface](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/22403/pg22403.txt)). That is the sonnet sized to a caryā's ten lines.

## 6. What survives, and what it gives the practice

### (a) The reverse question: what survives translation out of English

- **What survives** in most languages: the argument's shape (eight lines of misery, six of joy); a turn word at line 9; the addressee held back to line 10; one image, placed late; the order earth → daybreak → heaven's gate; a simile marker that also means "similar to" (*like*); *heaven* struck twice.
- **What survives only in part.**
  - **"State."** Few languages have one word for condition, soul, rank and realm, so the translator must choose, or find a word that holds two of them.
  - **The held-back main verb.** It survives, but by each language's own means.
- **What never survives:** "Haply" (*hap* and *happy*); the falling-and-rising feminine rhyme *despising / arising*; the iambic groove; *heav'n* against *heaven's*; the *-sing* chime; the 1600 rhyme *possest / least*; the Quarto's *himns*.
- **The lesson for this edition, which translates *into* English.** These are exactly what English can pay back when a caryā version loses rhyme or refrain: a stress groove, a feminine ending at the turn, one word struck three times with three meanings, and a main verb held back. They are "the trade you mean", in `how-to-read-a-song.md`'s terms.

### (b) A proposed lens (for `prompts/lenses.json`; not added there)

```json
{
  "id": "sonnet",
  "name": "Shakespearean sonnet",
  "after": "after Shakespeare's Sonnets (1609), above all Sonnet 29",
  "how": "Five-beat lines rhymed in alternating quatrains and a closing couplet; for a ten-line song, Hopkins's curtal proportion (six lines, then four and a half). One long sentence: hold the main verb back and lay out the trouble in phrases side by side, then turn hard on one word where the song turns. Strike the song's key word three times and let it mean something new each time. One image, late, rising. The refrain and the singer's name stay where the song has them.",
  "why": "The English sonnet was born as translation: Wyatt made his from Petrarch, keeping the images and changing the ending. It is English's form for a mind that turns, as song 10 turns from courting Ḍombī to killing her.",
  "touchstone": "Like to the lark at break of day arising / From sullen earth, sings hymns at heaven's gate",
  "board": { "lineation": 0.8, "syntax": 0.35, "rhythm": 0.85, "rhyme": 0.85 },
  "suits": "songs that turn, a key word struck again, one image that lifts the song"
}
```

Its signature on the board is the gap between cool syntax and hot sound. Every existing lens runs syntax at 0.45 or hotter.

### (c) Measurable features (one-line algorithms)

These add to `morton-in-depth.md` §5(i), which already proposes rhyme-scheme lettering (1), the turn (3) and pentameter regularity (10). Results marked "prototype" come from the scratch run on 18, 29, 73, 116 and 130.

1. **Substitutions by name.**
   - *Algorithm:* over `scan()` with the fixes in §2i, an opening trochee is a pattern starting `/x`; a feminine ending is 11 syllables ending `x`; spondees and pyrrhics are `//` and `xx` inside a foot.
   - *Prototype:* for 29, trochees at 5, 10 and 11 and feminine endings at 9 and 11 ✔.
2. **Turn finder, with a position label.**
   - *Algorithm:* flag lines 5–13 opening on *Yet / But / And yet / So / Thus / This*.
   - *Prototype:* 29 → Yet (9, Italian); 18 → But (9); 130 → And yet (13, English). 73 is missed until *This* is added.
3. **Addressee arrival.**
   - *Algorithm:* the first line with *thou / thee / thy / thine / you / your*, as a line number and as a fraction of the poem.
   - *Prototype:* 29 → line 10; 18 and 73 → line 1; 116 and 130 → never.
4. **Sentence count and main-clause delay.**
   - *Algorithm:* count sentence-final stops. After an opening subordinator (*When / If / As / Though*), find the first line with a first-person subject and a finite verb outside it.
   - *Prototype:* 29 has one sentence in the modern text and two in the Quarto; the main clause is at line 10 (by hand).
5. **Words struck again, with positions.**
   - *Algorithm:* lemmatise *'s* and *'st*; drop only articles, prepositions and pronouns (keep *like* and *then*); print word@lines.
   - *Prototype:* for 29, like@5,6,6,11; state@2,10,14; heaven@3,12; myself@4,9; then@10,14 ✔.
6. **Hidden chime (Vendler's couplet tie).**
   - *Algorithm:* find letter strings of 4–5 letters (better, phone strings) shared by three or more different words.
   - *Prototype:* 29 → *sing* (despising, arising, sings) and *ings* (sings, brings, kings); 116 → *love* and *ever* (ever, never, every).
7. **Rhyming in 1600.**
   - *Algorithm:* keep Crystal's index pairs in a table; when a pair reads off, eye or vowel and is listed, report "full in 1600" and give it the heat of a perfect rhyme.
   - *Prototype:* every off rhyme in 18, 29 and 116 is on the list ✔.
8. **Elision candidates.**
   - *Algorithm:* when a line runs one syllable over without a weak ending, re-count with *heaven, even, spirit, being, flower, power, hour, fire, desire, -ered, -ed* shortened.
   - *Result:* line 3 → 10 with *heav'n*; line 7 → 10 with *desiring* as three.
9. **Bracket span.**
   - *Algorithm:* record whether a parenthesis closes at its own line end (Quarto: lineation hotter) or crosses a line break (Malone). Where editions differ, report both readings.
10. **Antithesis promotion.**
    - *Algorithm:* in a line with *this … that*, *one … other* or a word said twice (*him … him*), promote the pair.
11. **Syntax heat that sees the frame.**
    - *Algorithm:* weight an opening subordinator by the number of lines it governs before the main clause.
    - *Result:* 29's *When* governs nine lines, so the frame reads cool while lines 5–8 stay hot inside.
12. **Engine fixes** (from §2i):
    - light disyllables keep two syllables;
    - an English digraph fallback before the IAST guesser;
    - feminine endings judged after elision.

### (d) Exercises for the Practice room

1. **Copia on one caryā line (Erasmus).**
   - Take one accurate English line, such as "I will kill Ḍombī; I will take her life", and write twenty versions.
   - Use Erasmus's moves: a synonym, the other voice, a denied opposite ("not unwilling"), a question, another person, a metaphor, a periphrasis, an expansion, a contraction.
   - Put each through the board and keep the one nearest the song's heat.
2. **Double translation (Ascham).**
   - Make your English from the gloss, then put the gloss away for an hour.
   - Rebuild a word-by-word gloss from your English alone and lay the two glosses side by side. Every word that does not come back is a drift.
   - (A reader of Old Bengali can go back into the song itself, as Ascham's pupils went back into Latin.)
3. **Wyatt's move.**
   - Make a sonnet, or a curtal sonnet, from one caryā. Keep every image and the refrain, and turn where the song turns.
   - Change one thing on purpose, as Wyatt turned "free" into "Caesar's I am", and say in a note what you changed and why.
4. **One sentence.** Write fourteen lines, or ten, as one sentence, with the main verb held back to the turn. Say it aloud without letting the voice fall before the last word.
5. **Strike it three times.** Take the song's key word and use it three times with three meanings, as *state* is used. Or strike a name, as song 10 strikes Ḍombī nine times.
6. **The feminine turn.** Rhyme the two lines round the turn with a two-syllable rhyme: one falling, one rising.
7. **Perform it.**
   - Print the poem and mark breaths (/), holds (_) and lifts (↑). Record it twice (Quarto and Malone brackets) and time each line.
   - Compare with §5b, and write one sentence on what your breath did to the thought.

### (e) Bridges to the Charyapada

- **One image, late, rising, and flagged; or many images, stated as identity.**
  - Sonnet 29 holds its single image back and marks it with "Like to". Song 1 opens by *identity*: "The body is a fine tree with five branches", leaving the reading to Munidatta ([`drafts/cp.01.json`](../texts/charyapada/drafts/cp.01.json)).
  - A sonnet-lens version must not add *like* to a caryā's identities: that would read the riddle inside the line. It may borrow 29's placement.
  - The directions differ too. The lark rises from earth to heaven's gate; song 14 crosses between two rivers towards "the city of the Jina", with "the moon and the sun, two wheels" ([`drafts/cp.14.json`](../texts/charyapada/drafts/cp.14.json)). Keep the song's direction.
- **The volta and song 10's turn.**
  - Song 10 swings from "Hey Ḍombī, I will keep company with you" to "I will kill Ḍombī; I will take her life" in its last line ([`drafts/cp.10.json`](../texts/charyapada/drafts/cp.10.json)). That is 130's place, the English couplet, not 29's Italian line 9.
  - The lens says "turn where the song turns", so the sonnet's volta never moves a caryā's turn.
- **Self-reference and the bhaṇitā.**
  - Lūyī names himself in couplets 2 and 5 ("Lūyī says: ask the guru, and know"; "Lūyī says: I have seen in meditation"), and Kāṇha in couplet 2 of song 10.
  - Shakespeare's nearest bhaṇitā is Sonnet 136: "Make but my name thy loue, and loue that still, / And then thou louest me for my name is Will" ✔ (Alden, p. 328). Next nearest is 18's "So long lives this".
  - Sonnet 29 does not sign. A version keeps the song's signature where the song puts it, and lets it do the sonnet's work of self-reference.
- **Two kinds of double language.**
  - The Quarto's bracket makes a line point two ways, lark or state: an ambiguity made by syntax, not by code. The caryā's *sandhyā-bhāṣā* makes its images point two ways: an ambiguity kept by the tradition's reading.
  - The edition treats both alike: the line keeps its strangeness, and the notes say what was chosen (for 29, which pointing; for a caryā, Munidatta's reading).
- **The answering poem.** Wyatt answering Petrarch is the model of the Workshop's "version": Dryden's third kind, held to the images, with the change owned.

**On the plan's table** ([`POETRY-PLAN.md`](../POETRY-PLAN.md), "The one idea"):
- **The line that comes back:** *state*, *like*, *heaven* and *then*, struck again and changed, plus the *-sing* chime.
- **The singer who signs:** 18's "So long lives this" and 136's "Will"; 29 itself does not sign.
- **The answering poem:** Wyatt from Petrarch, Ascham's double translation and Erasmus's copia.
- **The double language:** the conceit, and also the Quarto's two-way syntax.
