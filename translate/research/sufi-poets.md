## Part 1 — The practice: how the poems were made

### 1.1 Rumi: ghazals in the turning, the *Masnavī* read back to him

- **The *Dīvān-e Shams* in numbers.** Foruzanfar's critical edition has 3,229 ghazals and qaṣīdas, "a total of almost 35,000 lines", besides stanzaic poems and nearly two thousand quatrains. The *Masnavī* adds 25,688 lines (Estelami's edition) ✔ (Lewis, *Rumi: Past and Present, East and West*, [Oneworld](https://oneworld-publications.com/), 2000, p. 313). Ganjoor's ghazal section holds 3,230 poems ✔ (§2.2).
- **Composed while turning: the hagiographers' account.** "While he turned, he composed" is Lewis's summary of Sepahsālār. Aflākī's stories of the occasion of each poem are "perhaps more often than not purely imaginary" ✔ (p. 171). Lewis's own judgement: the "rhythmic turning of sama' no doubt induced many an impromptu poem". Rumi may have composed so much to give his followers "a suitable repertoire of librettos for the sama'", and many poems address the musicians ✔ (pp. 313–14).
- **Made for singing, filed by metre.** The Chester Beatty manuscript groups the poems "by meter and then alphabetically within each different meter", with first lines in red. Lewis, following Arberry, calls it "a kind of hymn book, for ease of locating poems to perform to sama'" ✔ (p. 296). The Konya manuscript is also arranged by metre, "establishing this arrangement as early Mevlevi practice" ✔ (same page). That is the Persian counterpart of the caryās' rāga headings: a book arranged for the singer.
- **The *Masnavī* was dictated, heard, and revised.**
  - Rumi began it about 1258 at Ḥusām al-Dīn Chalabī's request and composed until his death in 1273. "Rumi would deliver the verses and Chalabi would write them down and recite them back to him" ✔ ([Wikipedia, *Masnavi*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masnavi)). Lewis: Rumi "heard his poems read back to him and edited them, making corrections and improvements as any craftsman would" ✔ (p. 313).
  - The work is six books, about 25,000 couplets, each book with a prose introduction. It "has no framed plot"; its stories break off into "side stories … sometime taking hundreds of lines to make a point" ✔ (Wikipedia, same).
  - Book I's preface calls the work "the roots of the roots of the roots of the (Mohammedan) Religion", quotes the Light Verse (Q 24:35), and names Ḥusām al-Dīn as the one "at the request of" whom the "Poem in Rhymed Couplets" was lengthened ✔ ([Nicholson, *Mathnawí* vol. 2, 1926](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.70297), pp. 3–4). That the preface is in Arabic I take from the tradition, not from the fetched text: ◐.
- **Rumi on his own art, in both directions.** In the *Discourses* he says he composed only to entertain friends, "I am sick of poetry" (Lewis's translation, p. 172 ✔).
  - In ghazal 38 he complains of the metre in the metre. Its second hemistich is the metre's own feet, *mufṭaʿilun mufṭaʿilun mufṭaʿilun kusht ma-rā*, "…has killed me". The next couplet tells the flood to carry off *qāfiya*, rhyme: "husk, husk" ✔ ([Ganjoor, ghazal 38](https://ganjoor.net/moulavi/shams/ghazalsh/sh38), *rajaz muthamman maṭwī*). Lewis: "All this ta-dum-da-dum ta-dum-da-dum just kills me!" ✔ (p. 329). The same poem ends *man khamusham*, "I am silent", his pen name (§1.6).

### 1.2 *Samāʿ* and *dhikr*

- **Samāʿ** ("listening") "consisted in listening to music or even dancing … usually in the lodge". Not all Sufis and "certainly not all jurisconsults" approved, and Rumi's son Sulṭān Walad defended it against the charge that it broke the Sharia ✔ (Lewis, p. 27). Nicholson records the old view "that samā' is bad for novices, but good for adepts" ✔ ([*Studies in Islamic Mysticism*, 1921](https://archive.org/details/studiesinislamyst00nichuoft), p. 34).
- **The Mevlevi ceremony came later.** Lewis reports Gölpınarlı's view that making Rumi the head of an order is an anachronism; in Lewis's account the rite took its shape from "oral memories" of what Rumi and Shams did ✔ (p. 419). Its modern order: a *naʿt* for the Prophet; an improvisation (*taqsīm*) on the *nay*, the reed flute of the *Masnavī*'s first line; the walk of Sulṭān Walad; four *salām*s of turning; a last *nay taqsīm*; Qur'an recitation ✔ (p. 621). The reed of the poem opens the rite.
- **Dhikr** (Persian *zekr*) is "the meditative repetition or chanting of certain formulas or mantras", performed "congregationally, accompanied by rhythmic breathing and sometimes movement" ✔ (Lewis, p. 27). Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* ([UNC Press](https://uncpress.org/), 1975), gives its end point: since even "O God!" implies subject and object, "the last mystery of recollection is complete silence" ✔ (p. 172). That is the arc of Rumi's signature, *Khāmūsh*: "Silent!"
- **Dhikr in the rhyme.** Schimmel ties dhikr to form. The monorhymed forms are "ideal vehicles for mystical experience", and "the mystical poet can go on reiterating one of the divine names in the radif". The poem turns on one idea, "at times uprising, at other times becoming a monotonous hammering" ✔ (pp. 162–63). This is the plan's claim, that the radīf is the Sufi "line that comes back", made by a scholar.

### 1.3 The Arabic poets

- **Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya (8th c.): attributed verse only.** The "two loves" lines ("I have loved Thee with two loves, a selfish love and a love that is worthy (of Thee)") come to Margaret Smith from Abū Ṭālib al-Makkī's *Qūt al-qulūb* (10th c.). Abū Ṭālib admits "these verses were attributed to Rabi'a by the people of Basra, and by others" to four named men, but gives them as hers ✔ ([Smith, *Rābiʿa the Mystic*, 1928](https://archive.org/details/margart_smith_rabia)).
  - Rkia Elaroui Cornell's *Rabi'a from Narrative to Myth* (Oneworld, 2019) traces how the legend and its poems were built ◐ ([publisher](https://oneworld-publications.com/work/rabia-from-narrative-to-myth/)). Treat every "Rābiʿa poem" as attributed.
- **al-Ḥallāj (d. 922).**
  - Nicholson translates the chiasmus: "I am He whom I love, and He whom I love is I: / We are two spirits dwelling in one body" ✔ ([*The Mystics of Islam*, 1914](https://archive.org/details/cu31924074296231), p. 151).
  - The Arabic is *anā man ahwā wa-man ahwā anā / naḥnu rūḥāni ḥalalnā badanā* ✔ ([text](https://www.aldiwan.net/poem67610.html), via search). Its metre is given as *ramal* ◐. My scansion agrees if *anā* has a short final vowel inside the line (∪∪–– | –∪–– | –∪–): the *Masnavī*'s family.
  - Schimmel: Ḥallāj's *Kitāb al-ṭawāsīn* first tells of the moth that casts itself into the flame, "never to return and never to give any information about the Reality". ʿAṭṭār paraphrases it "almost word-for-word", and it reached Goethe ✔ (p. 70).
- **Ibn al-Fāriḍ (d. 1235).** The *Khamriyya* is read in §3.3. Nicholson praises his "terse, striking, and energetic expression", an "Arabian eloquence which … defies all attempts to transplant it" ✔ (1921, p. 183). The modern translation is Th. Emil Homerin, *ʿUmar Ibn al-Fāriḍ: Sufi Verse, Saintly Life* (Paulist, 2001, preface by Michael Sells) ◐ ([record](https://archive.org/details/umaribnalfaridsu0000ibna)).
- **Ibn ʿArabī (d. 1240): the poet who wrote his own commentary.** He finished the *Tarjumān al-ashwāq* at Mecca in 611/1215. He wrote its commentary at Aleppo months later, because a theologian said the preface's mystical claim was a cover for love poems ✔ ([Nicholson, *The Tarjumán al-Ashwáq*, 1911](https://archive.org/details/tarjumanalashwaq00ibnaiala), pp. 3–6). His words, in Nicholson's translation:
  - "Whenever I mention a name in this book I always allude to her, and whenever I mourn over an abode I mean her abode. In these poems I always signify Divine influences and spiritual revelations" ✔;
  - "I have used the erotic style and form of expression because men's souls are enamoured of it" ✔. The case is weighed in §5.5.

### 1.4 The Persian line: Sanāʾī, ʿAṭṭār, Rumi, Ḥāfiẓ

- **Sanāʾī (d. c. 1131)** "carved out a niche for a new kind of poetry": religious and didactic verse for gatherings of scholars and preachers ✔ (Lewis, p. 325). His *Ḥadīqat al-ḥaqīqa* is a masnavī in *khafīf* (x∪––|∪–∪–|∪∪–) ✔ ([Wikipedia, *Persian metres*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_metres)).
- **ʿAṭṭār (d. c. 1221) set the *Masnavī*'s metre.** His *Manṭiq al-ṭayr* (1177) is in 11-syllable *ramal*. "Because of its use in mystical poems by Attar, the 11-syllable *ramal* became associated particularly with poems on a mystical theme" ✔ (same, citing Elwell-Sutton and Thiesen). Rumi "modeled his narrative couplets on the genre" as Sanāʾī and ʿAṭṭār shaped it; Foruzanfar counts 35 *Masnavī* passages that borrow from ʿAṭṭār ✔ (Lewis, pp. 287, 303).
- **Ḥāfiẓ (d. 1390): the ghazal perfected, ambiguous by design.** His pen name means "one who has memorized the Koran" ✔ (Lewis, p. 328): the signature itself is Qur'anic.
  - His first ghazal is macaronic ✔ ([Ganjoor, Ḥāfiẓ 1](https://ganjoor.net/hafez/ghazal/sh1), *hazaj muthamman sālim*). It opens with the Arabic *alā yā ayyuhā s-sāqī adir kaʾsan wa-nāwilhā* ("O cupbearer, pass round a cup and hand it on") and ends in Arabic. Its radīf *-hā* is the Arabic pronoun "it" in *nāwil-hā*, *ahmil-hā* and the Persian plural in *mushkil-hā*, *dil-hā*, *manzil-hā*. That reading is my observation from the text.
  - Schimmel: Rumi's ghazals already have the ambiguous style, "refined by Hafiz, in whose poems it achieved a fascinating iridescence" ✔ (p. 163).

### 1.5 The answering poem: *naẓīra*, *javāb*, *istiqbāl*; Arabic *muʿāraḍa*

- **The Persian terms.** Paul Losensky's *Welcoming Fighānī* (Mazda, 1998) sorts the practice ◐ (via a [2025 article's summary](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1475262X.2025.2568505)): *tatabbuʿ*, imitating another poem's rhyme and metre; *naẓīra-gūʾī*, "speaking the parallel", the neutral term; *javāb-gūʾī*, answering the problem another poem set; *istiqbāl*, "welcoming" an old poet into a new setting (Losensky's title); *mushāʿara*, the open duel.
- **Arabic *muʿāraḍa*** answers a model in its own metre and rhyme ◐. The classic case is Aḥmad Shawqī's *Nahj al-Burda* (1910), answering al-Būṣīrī's *Burda*; both rhyme on *mīm*, "each verse ends with the Arabic letter *mīm*", and Tamīm al-Barghūthī's *Burdat Tamīm* (2010) extends the chain ✔ ([Wikipedia, *Al-Burda*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Burda)).
  - In performance the *Burda* is sung with a refrain couplet between its verses ✔ (same), a practice a caryā singer would know.
- **Rumi in the chain.** Rumi quotes and comments on lines of ʿAṭṭār and Sanāʾī inside the *Masnavī* ✔ (Lewis, p. 287), and his poem for Sanāʾī embeds half a line of Rudakī ✔ (p. 312). That is quotation (*taḍmīn*), the answering poem's cousin. A documented Rumi ghazal written as *javāb* to Sanāʾī in the same metre and rhyme: UNVERIFIED.
- **For this edition.** The Workshop's versions already answer the songs. A *naẓīra* adds one discipline: it names what it answers, keeps the model's metre and rhyme, and says something new.

### 1.6 The signature: *takhalluṣ*

- **What it is.** The ghazal poet adopts "a poetic persona" and names it "usually toward the end of each poem". The name is woven in "usually as an apostrophe to his persona, by way of consoling himself, summing up his situation, or urging action on his audience" ✔ (Lewis, p. 328). The Persian poet speaks *to* himself ("Ḥāfiẓ, hush!"); the caryā poet speaks *of* himself ("Lūyī says").
- **When it became the rule.** By Saʿdī (d. 1291) the pen name in the *maqṭaʿ* had become "a mandatory structural element" ◐ (Wikipedia, *Ghazal*, citing Thackston 1994).
- **Rumi's two signatures.** Rumi signed "either Khamush (Silence!) or Shams-e Tabrizi" ✔ (Lewis, pp. 328–29). *Khamūsh* "usually calls for an end to the complaint" and acts as a command to the reader: "Silence!"
  - *Shams-e Tabrīzī*, "the second and more common signature", puts the teacher-beloved's name in the signing slot, "a kind of imagined ghost writer". So the *Dīvān* bears Shams's name, not Rumi's.
  - Some ghazals carry no signature and let the radīf keep the silence instead (§3.2).

### 1.7 Correcting a common error: Rumi's languages

- **Mainly Persian.** Ganjoor draws its metre statistics from 65,747 Persian couplets out of 66,200 in its Rumi corpus ✔ ([Ganjoor, metre statistics](https://ganjoor.net/moulavi/vazn)). The remaining ~450 are, I infer, the Arabic, Turkish and Greek poems; mixed poems seem to count as Persian.
- **Arabic and other tongues.** Rumi "had an excellent command of Arabic … as a language in which he could compose poems", and read al-Mutanabbī at night ✔ (Lewis, p. 314). Some ghazals open in Turkish; D 1362–63 alternate Turkish and Persian lines; D 2542 has the Greek refrain "you are the Agapos" ✔ (pp. 314–15).
  - The *Masnavī* is Persian "riddled with Arabic excerpts from Muslim scripture" (Rozina Ali ✔, Part 4), and its prologue ends on the Arabic *wa-s-salām* (§3.1).
- **So "Rumi the Arabic poet" is wrong and "Rumi the Persian poet" is incomplete.** He wrote Persian, in a metre system taken from Arabic, with Arabic scripture folded into the line.

## Part 2 — The machinery

### 2.1 *ʿArūḍ*: quantitative metre

- **Origin.** al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad (d. 786) "described 15 types of meter", and al-Akhfash a 16th ✔ ([Wikipedia, *Arabic prosody*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_prosody)).
- **Length, not stress.** In Persian, an open syllable with a long vowel is long and with a short vowel is short. A closed short syllable is long. A long vowel plus a consonant, or a final cluster, counts long + short (*Shams* = – ∪) ✔ (Lewis, pp. 330–31).
- **The Arabic workhorse is *ṭawīl*:** *faʿūlun mafāʿīlun faʿūlun mafāʿilun* (∪–x | ∪––– | ∪–x | ∪–∪–). It is about 50% of early Bedouin poetry and 35% by the 8th century, with *kāmil*, *wāfir* and *basīṭ* next ✔ (Wikipedia, citing Vadet and Stoetzer).
- **Persian made the system stricter.** Its metres are "fixed", with few free positions. They avoid three shorts in a row and prefer four-syllable feet, so the Arabic *kāmil* "is almost never found" ✔ (Wikipedia, *Persian metres*). About 30 metres serve lyric verse, 70% of lyrics use one of seven ◐ (Elwell-Sutton 1976, p. 162, via Wikipedia).
- **For the English ear.** A Persian hemistich is about a whole English line long; the *Masnavī*'s 22-syllable couplet equals "two lines of Dante" ✔ (Lewis, p. 331). So one English line per half-line, the engine's rule for the caryās, is the right unit for Persian too.

### 2.2 Which metres Rumi favours: a count

- **The *Masnavī*:** *ramal musaddas maḥdhūf*, *fāʿilātun fāʿilātun fāʿilun*, – ∪ – – | – ∪ – – | – ∪ –. Ganjoor calls it "the Masnavī metre" ✔ ([Ganjoor, *Masnavī* I.1](https://ganjoor.net/moulavi/masnavi/daftar1/sh1)), and Lewis and Wikipedia agree ✔.
- **The *Dīvān*: no single favourite.** I counted the metre tags on all 3,230 ghazals in Ganjoor's *Dīvān-e Shams*: 3,210 tagged, 52 distinct metres ✔ ([API](https://api.ganjoor.net/api/ganjoor/cat/99?poems=true&mainSections=true)).
  - By family: *hazaj* 33.5%, *ramal* 23.4%, *rajaz* 10.8%, *mujtathth* 9.0%, *muḍāriʿ* 8.9%, *munsariḥ* 4.2%, *khafīf* 3.4%, *sarīʿ* 3.0%.
  - No single metre exceeds 9%. The leaders are *hazaj musaddas maḥdhūf* 9.0%, *mujtathth muthamman makhbūn maḥdhūf* 8.4%, *ramal muthamman maḥdhūf* 8.1%, *hazaj muthamman akhrab* 7.5%, *hazaj musaddas akhrab maqbūḍ maḥdhūf* 7.0% and *hazaj muthamman sālim* 6.4%.
  - Caveats. Ganjoor warns its tags are approximate: many were set from one hemistich, some by machine, and convertible metres can be confused. A Persian study of 75 ghazals also puts *ramal* and *hazaj* first ◐ (search summary).
- **The distinctive thing is the drum.** Lewis: Rumi "tends to choose the more fluid meters and employ them rhythmically, as one would expect for poetry extemporaneously composed to a turning dance". His internal rhymes make a rhyme word recur "two, three or even four times as often as prosody requires" ✔ (p. 333).
  - The doubled metres split the line and often rhyme at the middle: *ey ʿāšeqān ey ʿāšeqān * hengām-e kūč ast az jahān*, – – ∪ – | – – ∪ – || – – ∪ – | – – ∪ – ✔ (Wikipedia, *Persian metres*).
  - In *ramal makhbūn*, a sentence break at mid-line falls in 75% of Rumi's lines against 25% of Saʿdī's ◐ (Jeannine Heny, via Wikipedia).
  - Ganjoor tags four of his ghazals in the Arabic *kāmil* family, which is almost absent from Persian (my count).

### 2.3 *Qāfiya* and *radīf*; *matlaʿ* and *maqṭaʿ*

- **Qāfiya.** The rhyme is built on the *rawī*, the final consonant and its vowel, and one rhyme runs through the poem ✔ (Wikipedia, *Arabic prosody*). All medieval Persian verse rhymes: there is "no equivalent of blank verse" ✔ (Lewis, p. 331).
- **Radīf** is "a word or series of words that is repeated after every rhyme", e.g. *šekāyat mīkonad / hekāyat mīkonad* ✔ (Wikipedia, *Persian metres*). Schimmel: "outside the Arabic-speaking world" the rhyme "was often extended" by it, "an additional rhyme word or even a whole sentence" ✔ (p. 162). It is Persian and Persianate, not classical Arabic.
- **Matlaʿ and maqṭaʿ.** The *matlaʿ* (first couplet) has rhyme and radīf in both lines, then only second lines do (aa ba ca da); the rhymed opening comes from the Arabic *taṣrīʿ*. The *maqṭaʿ* (last couplet) carries the *takhalluṣ*. A ghazal has five couplets at least and rarely more than fifteen ✔ (Wikipedia, *Ghazal*, citing Arberry 1958 and others).
- **Couplets complete in themselves?** Nicholson 1898: in the ghazal, "couplets complete in themselves are strung together in the slightest fashion" ✔ ([*Selected Poems from the Divani Shamsi Tabriz*](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.52959), p. ix). Lewis calls that view "reductive": Rumi's ghazals often move as wholes ✔ (p. 591).

### 2.4 The forms

- **Qaṣīda.** A long monorhyme ode in three parts: the *nasīb*, the poet at the abandoned campsite remembering the beloved; the *raḥīl*, the journey; a boast or praise ✔ ([Wikipedia, *Qasida*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qasida)).
  - Ibn ʿArabī says his mourning "over an abode" always means hers. Ibn al-Fāriḍ keeps the nasīb's Arabian names (Najd, al-ʿUdhayb, Ḥājir), which Nicholson finds "quite different … from the pictorial treatment of desert life" in the old odes ✔ (1921, p. 182).
- **Ghazal.** Five to fifteen couplets on one rhyme, often with a radīf, opened by the matlaʿ and signed in the maqṭaʿ. It is a love lyric that may be human, divine or both (§2.6).
- **Masnavī.** Couplets, "each couplet with a different rhyme, thus with the scheme AA BB CC", in one of seven 11-syllable metres (one of 10) ✔ (Wikipedia, *Persian metres*). It is the form of epic and romance and, from Sanāʾī on, of teaching.
- **Rubāʿī.** A quatrain rhymed "AA BA", in a metre used only for rubāʿīs (– | – ∪ ∪ – | – ∪ ∪ – | – ∪ ∪ – and a variant) ✔ (same). Rumi's quatrains are the least secure part of his canon: scholarship may remove "many of the roba'is" ✔ (Lewis, p. 313).
  - Rubāʿī 157 (Ganjoor numbering) ✔ ([Ganjoor](https://ganjoor.net/moulavi/shams/robaeesh/sh157)):
    - *az kufr u zi islām birūn ṣaḥrāʾī-st / mā-rā ba miyān-i ān faḍā sawdāʾī-st / ʿārif chu badān rasīd sar rā binihad / na kufr u na islām u na ānjā jāʾī-st*;
    - gloss: "Beyond unbelief and Islam there is a plain; in the midst of that space we have a longing; the knower, reaching it, lays down his head: no unbelief, no Islam, not even a 'there' is there."
  - Its rhyme is *-āʾī* with the radīf *-st* on lines 1, 2 and 4. *Kufr* and *islām* frame it, in the first and last lines. It is the "field" poem of Part 4.

### 2.5 Root-play, *tajnīs*, *iqtibās*

- **Tajnīs: words that nearly or wholly sound alike.** One prologue (§3.1) has three kinds: near-homonyms that differ by one consonant (*ḥikāyat / shikāyat*; *nay / may*, "reed / wine"); full homonyms (*nīst bād*, "is not wind", against *nīst bād*, "may he be naught"); and one root in two senses (*sharḥa sharḥa*, "strip by strip", against *sharḥ*, "exposition").
- **Arabic's three-consonant roots.**
  - Ibn al-Fāriḍ sets *karm* "vine" (v. 1) against *yakrumu* "is generous" (v. 19), both k-r-m. His v. 26 turns *khamr* and *karm* about: "There is a wine without a vine … there is a vine without a wine" ✔ ([Wikisource](https://ar.wikisource.org/wiki/%D8%B4%D8%B1%D8%A8%D9%86%D8%A7_%D8%B9%D9%84%D9%89_%D8%B0%D9%83%D8%B1_%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AD%D8%A8%D9%8A%D8%A8_%D9%85%D8%AF%D8%A7%D9%85%D8%A9); Nicholson 1921, p. 186).
  - Ibn ʿArabī glosses *qalb*, "heart", from its sense of "changing" ✔ (Nicholson 1911, p. 69).
  - In ghazal 2219, *qumrī* "turtle-dove" (c. 6) echoes *qamar* "moon" (c. 1). The echo is my observation.
- **Iqtibās: scripture quoted or bent into the line.** The *Masnavī* preface quotes the Light Verse ✔.
  - Rozina Ali: "Rumi often amended texts from the Koran so that they would fit the lyrical rhyme and meter of the Persian verse" ✔ ([*The New Yorker*, 5 Jan 2017](https://www.newyorker.com/books/page-turner/the-erasure-of-islam-from-the-poetry-of-rumi)).
  - Ibn ʿArabī glosses "I follow the religion of Love" with Q 3:31, "Follow me, then God will love you" ✔ (Nicholson 1911, p. 69).
  - Persian *balī* "yes" is the *balā* of the covenant of Q 7:172 ("Am I not your Lord?" "Yes!"; Schimmel's "Day of Alast" ✔, p. 172). "Nod your head, *balī*" in 2219.5 may carry that echo; the reading is mine.

### 2.6 The conventional images, and how the double register works

- **The stock:** wine, tavern, cupbearer (*sāqī*), the tavern-keeper (*pīr-i mughān*), the beloved's curl and mole, the reed, the moth and candle, the nightingale and rose. Bell (1897) gives the received key: "The tavern … is the place of instruction or worship, of which the tavern-keeper is the teacher or priest, and the wine the spirit of divine knowledge".
  - She quotes Ḥāfiẓ: "The nightingale, and none beside, knows the full worth of the rose … many a one reads the leaf and understands not the meaning thereof" ✔ ([*Poems from the Divan of Hafiz*](https://archive.org/details/poemsfromdivanof00hafiiala), pp. 61–62).
- **Deliberate and double.** Schimmel: "It seems futile … to look for either a purely mystical or a purely profane interpretation" of Ḥāfiẓ, Jāmī or ʿIrāqī. "Their ambiguity is intended, the oscillation between the two levels of being is consciously maintained" ✔ (p. 288).
- **Nicholson on Rumi, differently.** Rumi "does not balance literal and spiritual meanings so equally as to leave the choice uncertain … He is no juggler with mysteries" ✔ (1898, p. xlii). The scholars differ on Rumi and agree on Ḥāfiẓ.
  - If the ambiguity is intended, an English must keep both registers alive in one word: *wine*, not "divine love".
- **The forbidden gives the image its charge.** Dick Davis: it "would never occur to a Western poet to express the forbidden intoxications of mysticism" through wine, since wine was never forbidden in the West ✔ ([*On Not Translating Hafez*, *New England Review* 25, 2004](https://cat.middlebury.edu/~nereview/25-1-2/Davis.html)). And in a note the charge dies: "to explain a resonance is like explaining a joke" ✔.
- **The Victorian split.** Bell's introduction quotes Renan: Sufism as "a revolt of the Aryan spirit" against "the terrifying simplicity of the Semitic spirit" (my translation of the French) ✔ (p. 61). That is the racial story that pulled Sufi poetry away from Islam. Omid Safi dates the erasure to this period, when poets were thought "mystical not because of Islam but in spite of it" ✔ (in Ali, *New Yorker*).

### 3.1 "Listen to the reed": *Masnavī* I, couplets 1–18

  - Every couplet is at least **perfect** (*firāq / ishtiyāq*, *burīd / darīd*), and most add an **absolute** radīf.

- **Narrator.** The poet commands "listen!", then from couplet 2 the reed speaks as "I". The Persian has no quotation marks, so where the reed stops is an editor's choice: Whinfield closes its speech after couplet 8 ✔ ([Whinfield 1898](https://archive.org/details/masnaviimanavisp00jala_0), p. 1).
- **The turns.**
  - At couplet 9 the lament becomes a verdict, "fire, not wind", and a curse.
  - At couplet 18 the raw cannot grasp the cooked, "so speech must be short", and the prologue ends in Arabic.

- **In English.**
  - Nicholson keeps the image order, the address, "the reed" struck again and the double question: "Who ever saw a poison and antidote like the reed? Who ever saw a sympathiser … like the reed?"

  - Whinfield keeps a line-shape ("Hearken to the reed-flute, how it discourses / When complaining of the pains of separation—") but flattens the pun to "fire, not mere air. / Let him who lacks this fire be accounted dead!" ✔
  - Both put the tradition's reading below the line. Whinfield's footnote: "Love signifies the strong attraction that draws all creatures back to reunion with their Creator" ✔ (p. 1). That is the edition's rule, practised in 1898.

### 3.2 A ghazal with a radīf: *Dīvān-e Shams* 2219, *hīch magū* ("say nothing")

**Why this poem.** Its radīf is two words, returns in every couplet, and names the poem's subject. Text and metre are verifiable ✔ ([Ganjoor, ghazal 2219](https://ganjoor.net/moulavi/shams/ghazalsh/sh2219), *ramal muthamman makhbūn maḥdhūf*, *faʿilātun faʿilātun faʿilātun faʿilun*).

| # | Persian | gloss |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *man ghulām-i qamar-am, ghayr-i qamar hīch magū / pīsh-i man juz sukhan-i shamʿ u shakar hīch magū* | I am the moon's slave: but the moon, say nothing / before me, but talk of candle and sugar, say nothing |
| 2 | *sukhan-i ranj magū, juz sukhan-i ganj magū / w-ar az īn bī-khabarī, ranj ma-bar, hīch magū* | talk of pain, don't; but talk of treasure, don't / and if you know nothing of this, take no pain: say nothing |
| 3 | *dūsh dīwāna shudam; ʿishq ma-rā dīd u bi-guft: / "āmadam; naʿra ma-zan, jāma ma-dar, hīch magū"* | last night I went mad; Love saw me and said: / "I have come. Don't shout, don't tear your clothes, say nothing" |
| 4 | *guftam: "ay ʿishq, man az chīz-i digar mī-tarsam" / guft: "ān chīz-i digar nīst digar; hīch magū"* | I said: "O Love, I fear something other" / he said: "that other thing is no more; say nothing" |

  | – ∪ – – | ∪ ∪ – – | ∪ ∪ – – | ∪ ∪ – |

  The izafe *-i* of *ghulām-i* counts long, as the metre permits. The overlong *hīch* splits long + short across the last foot, so every couplet lands its radīf on the same final positions.

  - *magū* "don't say" 14; *guftam / guft* "I said / he said" 10.

  - *dil* "heart" 5; *digar* "other / any more" 3, in one couplet, as tajnīs.
- **Narrator.** A lover talks with Love, then with his own heart, who answers like a father (*jān-i pidar*, "my dear", literally "father's soul"). There is no *takhalluṣ*. The radīf does the signature's work: the poem that would be signed "Silent!" says "say nothing" in every couplet. The reading is mine; Lewis's account of *Khamūsh* supports it (§1.6).
- **The turn** is the last couplet. The speaker at last names the moon: "isn't this the describing of God?" He is told "It is" and, in the same breath, to say nothing. A lover's hush becomes apophasis: the name confirmed and silenced at once.
- **In English.** I know no pre-1929 translation, so the gloss is the evidence.
  - Simply keeping "say nothing" at every couplet end keeps the sun of Morton's system and most of the syntax.
  - What goes: the *-ar* chime; the metre; *qamar / qumrī*; *balī* and the covenant (c. 5); *zīr u zabar*, "topsy-turvy", which also names the vowel-signs below and above a letter (c. 9; a possible pun, mine).
  - Davis in miniature: the radīf is "often transferable to English without, I think, its appearing to be intolerably outré" ✔ (2004).

### 3.3 The Wine Ode: Ibn al-Fāriḍ's *Khamriyya*, verses 1–3

**Text.** Arabic Wikisource, 41 verses ✔; Nicholson's transliteration of v. 1 agrees ✔. The metre is *ṭawīl* by my scansion, rhyming on *mīm* (*-mu*); I could open no page that names it: ◐.

| v. | Arabic | gloss |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *sharibnā ʿalā dhikri l-ḥabībi mudāmatan / sakirnā bihā min qabli an yukhlaqa l-karmu* | we drank, remembering the Beloved, a wine / we were drunk with it before the vine was made |
| 2 | *lahā l-badru kaʾsun wa-hya shamsun yudīruhā / hilālun, wa-kam yabdū idhā muzijat najmu* | its cup the full moon; it a sun passed round by / a crescent; and when it is mixed, how many a star appears |
| 3 | *wa-law-lā shadhāhā mā htadaytu li-ḥānihā / wa-law-lā sanāhā mā taṣawwarahā l-wahmu* | but for its scent I had not found its tavern / but for its gleam imagination had not pictured it |

- **Scansion** of v. 1 (mine; the rhyme vowel lengthens in pause):

  | *sha-rib-nā* | *ʿa-lā-dhik-ril* | *ḥa-bī-bi* | *mu-dā-ma-tan* ‖ | *sa-kir-nā* | *bi-hā-min-qab* | *li-an-yukh* | *la-qal-kar-mū* |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | ∪ – – | ∪ – – – | ∪ – ∪ | ∪ – ∪ – ‖ | ∪ – – | ∪ – – – | ∪ – – | ∪ – – – |

  That is *faʿūlun mafāʿīlun faʿūl(u) mafāʿilun*, with the full *mafāʿīlun* closing the line; vv. 2–3 scan the same way. The first line does not rhyme its halves (*mudāmatan / al-karmu*): there is no *taṣrīʿ* (my observation).

| channel | heat | where the heat lives | Nicholson 1921 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lineation | 0.85 | Forty-one closed verses of one weight, usually a sentence each, on one rhyme; no refrain. | 0.4: numbered prose verses |
| Syntax | 0.6 | Conditionals hang clause under clause (*law-lā … mā*, "but for … I had not"), but stacked as pattern: vv. 8–16 all open *wa-law*, "and had…", and v. 17 keeps *law*. Subordination becomes a drumbeat. | 0.6: keeps "And had…" |
| Rhythm | 0.85 | *Ṭawīl*, fixed, with sanctioned variants (*faʿūlu* for *faʿūlun*) that ripple without breaking the groove. | 0.15 |
| Rhyme | 0.85 | Monorhyme: all 41 verses end consonant + *-mu* (*karmu, najmu, wahmu, katmu, ithmu, ismu…*). The last syllable chimes absolutely; the one before, as vowel or off rhyme. | 0.1 |

- **Struck again** (Wikisource text, by token): *wa-lā* "and not" 13; *wa-law* "and had / if" 12; *bihā* "with it" 10; *khamr* 3, *karm* 2 (the chiasmus of v. 26).
- **The apophatic verse.** Verse 22 packs four sayings and four unsayings into one line: *ṣafāʾun wa-lā māʾun, wa-luṭfun wa-lā hawā, wa-nūrun wa-lā nārun, wa-rūḥun wa-lā jismu*. Nicholson: "Pure, but not as water; subtle, but not as air; luminous, but not as fire; spirit, but not (joined to) body" ✔ (1921, p. 186). This is what Michael Sells calls apophasis, "unsaying" (Part 4).
- **Narrator and turns.** A drinking "we" becomes "I" ("I had not found the way"). From v. 5 the praise becomes a catalogue of miracles ("had they sprinkled with it the earth of a dead man's grave…"). At v. 21 the speaker turns expert witness ("They say to me, 'Describe it…'") and defines by negation ✔ (Nicholson).
- **In English.** Nicholson 1921 keeps the anaphora ("(8) And had the boon-companions…", "(9) And had they sprinkled…"), every image in order and every paradox. He loses *ṭawīl* and the 41-fold *-mu* ✔.
  - Lowth's lesson ([`precedents-song-translation.md`](precedents-song-translation.md) A1) holds: repeated *structure* crosses, repeated *sound* does not.
  - Every doctrinal reading sits in his footnotes, such as "The soul was intoxicated with the wine of Divine Love … before the body was created", never in the line ✔.

### 3.4 What the three readings show

- **Heat lives in rhyme and rhythm.** In all three poems the hottest channels are rhyme and rhythm, the two prose English cannot carry. Lineation survives if the couplet stays the unit (Nicholson's numbers, Whinfield's pairs).
- **The radīf is the most portable heat.** A repeated end-phrase costs English nothing and keeps the sun of Morton's system.
- **Sufi poems end in silence.** The prologue closes "speech must be short"; the ghazal's radīf is "say nothing"; the Wine Ode defines by negation. An English that explains, in the line or in an added moral, runs against the poem's own last move.

## Part 4 — What survives into English, and why

- **FitzGerald, *Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám* (1859).** Seventy-five quatrains, 250 copies ✔ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubaiyat_of_Omar_Khayyam)).
  - He kept the aaba in pentameter: "Awake! for Morning in the Bowl of Night / Has flung the Stone that puts the Stars to Flight: / And Lo! the Hunter of the East has caught / The Sultán's Turret in a Noose of Light" ✔ ([Gutenberg #246](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/246/pg246.txt)).
  - He kept the allusions in the line too ("the WHITE HAND OF MOSES on the Bough … Jesus from the Ground suspires") and explained them in notes ("the Healing Power of Jesus resided in his Breath") ✔.
  - His letters say "Many quatrains are mashed together" and "Better a live Sparrow than a stuffed Eagle" ◐ (via Wikipedia).
  - Lesson: the rubāʿī's rhyme and turn cross whole. The poet's identity does not; readers took FitzGerald for Khayyam ([`western-lenses.md`](western-lenses.md)).
- **Nicholson.**
  - The 1898 *Selected Poems* is in prose on principle: "My translation seeks to reconcile the claims of accuracy and art: it is therefore in prose. Obviously English verse cannot convey the full verbal sense of oriental poetry without lapsing into grotesque doggerel" ✔ (p. ix). He calls FitzGerald's *Omar* the "classic example" of "the highest and rarest kind of translation" ✔.
  - His *Masnavī* (text 1925–33, translation 1926–34, commentary 1937–40) goes line by line with notes. Lewis sends readers who want "the in-depth meaning" there ✔ (p. 333).
- **Arberry.** *The Mystical Poems of Rumi* gives 400 ghazals from Foruzanfar, 200 from each half ✔ (Lewis, p. 579). Lewis: these "literal cribs … inspired and invited a multitude of English-to-English 'translations'" ✔, Barks's among them.
- **Coleman Barks, and the argument that Islam was erased.** The basic facts are in [`precedents-song-translation.md`](precedents-song-translation.md) C6. Rozina Ali's essay, now read at source ✔, adds the cases:
  - Arberry's "Whoever asks you about the Houris, show (your) face (and say) 'Like this'" becomes a line about "the perfect satisfaction of all our sexual wanting". "The religious context is gone", while Jesus and Joseph are kept.
  - "Out beyond ideas of rightdoing and wrongdoing" stands, in Ali's account, for "iman ('religion') and kufr ('infidelity')". Ganjoor's rubāʿī 157 reads *kufr* and *islām* ✔ (§2.4); the discrepancy is small but noted.
  - Voices Ali quotes: Jawid Mojaddedi, "The Rumi that people love is very beautiful in English, and the price you pay is to cut the culture and religion". Omid Safi: "spiritual colonialism", and Rumi without the Koran is Milton without the Bible. Barks: "Religion is such a point of contention for the world". Mojaddedi's own *Masnavī* italicises every switch into Arabic.
  - Franklin Lewis's judgement is the most useful here, because it is about form ✔ (pp. 590–91):
    - Barks's versions "follow the structure of the original poems", so they "remain translations and not adaptations";
    - but they frequently "leave out material that might seem repetitious to an American audience not experiencing the poem in the metrical rhymes of the Persian";
    - and they "teleport the poems of Rumi out of their cultural and Islamic context".

    Cutting repetition is cutting heat: the Barks case is a temperature failure as well as a religious one.
- **Agha Shahid Ali: the radīf and the signature carried in.**
  - *Ravishing DisUnities: Real Ghazals in English* (Wesleyan, 2000) gathers 107 poets, with Ali's "instructions on how to compose one in English" ✔ ([publisher](https://www.weslpress.org/9780819564375/ravishing-disunities/)).
  - His rules as widely quoted: autonomous couplets; the qāfiya "immediately preceding" the radīf; a scheme that makes the poet "its slave", so that the poem is "the alluring tension of a slave trying to master the master" ◐.
  - "Tonight" (final form in *Call Me Ishmael Tonight*, [Norton, 2003](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780393051957)) ✔ (checked in a scan):
    - thirteen couplets on the radīf "tonight", with the qāfiya on *-ell* (spell, expel, cell, fell, infidel, Hell, knell, Jezebel, gazelle, farewell, Ishmael);
    - the Call to Prayer and the idols kept in the line;
    - it closes on the signature: "And I, Shahid, only am escaped to tell thee— / God sobs in my arms. Call me Ishmael tonight."
  - Stephanie Burt annotates it for the Poetry Foundation ◐ ([title](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/69597/agha-shahid-ali-tonight)).
  - Lesson: in English the radīf, the qāfiya (as off rhyme) and the vocative signature all hold, together.
- **Michael Sells: unsaying.** *Mystical Languages of Unsaying* (Chicago, 1994) reads apophasis, "speaking away", in Plotinus, Eriugena, Ibn ʿArabī, Porete and Eckhart, including a chapter on Ibn ʿArabī's "Polished Mirror: Identity Shift and Meaning Event" ◐ ([record](https://archive.org/details/mysticallanguage0000sell)). His Ibn ʿArabī translations are *Stations of Desire* (2000) ◐. For the engine: the Wine Ode's negations and the "say nothing" of 2219 are the content, so an English must unsay as hard as it says.
- **Annemarie Schimmel.** She is the scholar of the images and the intended double register (§2.6): *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* (1975); *The Triumphal Sun* (Rumi, 1978) and *A Two-Colored Brocade* (imagery, 1992) ◐.
- **Dick Davis on what is hard.** The radīf and monorhyme can be managed. "The real problem" lies in "conventions that exist … just below the conscious level", about what counts as poetic at all ✔ (2004).

| carries into English | is lost, or lives only in notes |
|---|---|
| the address: "listen!", "O heart", "I said… he said…" | quantity: every metre above (English has stress, not length) |
| the beloved's ambiguity, if *wine* stays wine | the radīf's chime when prose drops it; monorhyme, mostly |
| the images, in order (Nicholson, Whinfield) | *tajnīs*: *nīst bād / nīst bād*, *nay / may*, *qamar / qumrī* |
| the refrain, when kept (Ali; a literal "say nothing") | Qur'anic resonance: *balī*, the Light Verse, "religion of Love" as Q 3:31 |
| anaphora and parallelism (the Wine Ode's "And had…") | the forbiddenness that charged the wine (Davis) |
| the signature as apostrophe (Ali's "Shahid") | the Arabic inside the Persian (*wa-s-salām*, Ḥāfiẓ's opening) |

- **Why the ghazal lens fits the caryās.** A caryā has five couplets, the ghazal's minimum ✔ (Wikipedia, *Ghazal*). It signs in the last couplet and strikes a name again and again.
- **Where it doesn't.** A caryā rhymes like a *masnavī*, each couplet within itself (aa bb cc), not on one rhyme through the poem. Its refrain is a whole couplet, not a word or two. A ghazal version keeps the song's own couplet rhymes where it can, and takes its radīf from what the song strikes most. In short: the caryā rhymes like a masnavī and signs like a ghazal.

### 5.2 Measurable features for the English ear (`lib/english.mjs`, `lib/reading.mjs`)

Each takes lines `[{id, en, group}]` in the unit's order, as `englishBoard` does.

1. **Radīf.** Normalise the closing line of every couplet, plus both lines of the first. Find the longest token suffix (k ≥ 1) shared by ≥ 75% of them, over at least 3 couplets. Report `{radif, share, matlaBoth}` and count it as absolute rhyme at that share.
2. **Qāfiya and monorhyme.** Take the token just before the radīf, or the last token if there is none. Score each against the first couplet's with `rhymeWords`. Monorhyme is the share that rhyme; identical rhyme words are listed apart, since they repeat rather than rhyme.
3. **Form.** Rhymes across couplet-closing lines give ghazal/qaṣīda (aa ba ca); couplets rhyming within themselves and not across give *masnavī* (aa bb cc); four lines with 1, 2 and 4 rhyming and 3 not give *rubāʿī* (aaba).
4. **Signature.** Search the last couplet for the poet's names from the text's metadata (Lūyī, Kāṇha…) or the maker's pen name. Match `<Name> says` (third person, bhaṇitā) and `<Name>,` / `O <Name>` (vocative, takhalluṣ). Return `{found, couplet, person}`; a signature before the last couplet scores lower.
5. **Couplet autonomy.** The share of couplets that are end-stopped (`endStopped`) and whose next couplet does not open with a continuing word (and, but, so, which, who, that, since). High is ghazal-hot; low means the lines run on.
6. **Anaphora run.** The longest run of consecutive lines or couplets opening with the same one or two words (the Wine Ode's "And had…"), with the word and the length of the run.
7. **Unsaying.** The share of lines that pair a saying with an unsaying (`X, (and|but) not Y`, `no X`, `neither … nor`, `without X`) or command silence (`say nothing`, `hush`, `be silent`).
8. **Dhikr density.** The most-repeated content word or phrase (n ≤ 3), its share of all tokens and its spread across the poem. Count lemma families, so *say / said / saying / unsaid* register together, as the 27 "say" forms of 2219 do.
9. **Hemistich balance.** For lines with a medial break (comma, dash or `|`), the syllable ratio of the halves. Persian lines break near the middle, so this measures an English "hemistich".

### 5.3 Exercises for the Practice room

1. **A *naẓīra* to a caryā.** Keep one song's rhyme sound and refrain couplet (Lūyī's, song 1). Write five new couplets in your own voice that answer its claim: agree, argue or extend. Head it "a reply to Lūyī" and print it after the song, never instead.
2. **A ghazal on a struck name.** Take the word the song strikes most (song 10's *Ḍombī*, nine times) as radīf. Write five to seven autonomous couplets ending "…, Ḍombī", a different rhyme word before it each time. The last couplet addresses you by a pen name. The radīf detector should read 1.0.
3. **Choose a takhalluṣ.** Pick a pen name that is a quality ("Silent", "Witness", "Ferryman"). Write only the last couplet: one line consoling yourself, one rebuking, both addressed by that name. Set it beside "Lūyī says" and feel the difference in distance.
4. **A dhikr line.** Choose one word from a caryā (*boat*, *ferry*). Write a line that strikes it three times and changes around it each time, as *nīst* does in the reed song. Say it aloud nine times (Morton: read aloud, with the body), and keep what survives the ninth saying.
5. **Listen to X.** In four *masnavī* couplets, let a caryā's object tell what it was cut from (the oar from the tree, the boat from the shore). Open with "listen", strike one plain word at each couplet end, and close by cutting speech short.
6. **The rubāʿī turn.** Make a couplet and its refrain into an aaba quatrain whose unrhymed third line holds back and whose fourth turns. Then ask what the turn added, and whether it was in the song.
7. **Unsaying.** After Wine Ode v. 22, describe one thing from a caryā in a single line of four pairs, "X, and no Y". Use only pairs the song's own images license, and add no doctrine.
8. **Hotter and cooler with a radīf.** Add a radīf to a finished sung draft, remove it, and read both aloud. This is Morton's first exercise, with the Persian tradition's hottest tool.

### 5.4 Bridges to the Charyapada

- **Takhalluṣ and bhaṇitā.**
  - Same slot: the last couplet, and in Lūyī's model the refrain couplet too (N. Sen, [`charyapada-sound.md`](charyapada-sound.md) A3).
  - Different grammar: the bhaṇitā is third person, the singer outside the song. The takhalluṣ is an apostrophe, the poet turning on his persona (Lewis ✔).
  - Versions keep the person, and keep the tag's recurring English ("says").
  - Rumi's "Shams", another's name in the signing slot, has a caryā parallel only where a song names the guru. That needs checking song by song.
- **Radīf and ধ্রু.** Both sit at Morton's sun, at different scales.
  - The radīf is a word or two *inside* each couplet's last line. The dhru is a whole couplet sung *again* after each couplet.
  - The Arabic world knows the dhru too: the *Burda* is sung with a refrain couplet between verses ✔. The early *Dīvān* manuscripts were hymn books filed by metre, as the caryās are headed by rāga.
  - A caryā version can hold both: the refrain as the song's return, and a struck word inside the lines where the song itself strikes one.
- **Double register and *sandhyā-bhāṣā*.**
  - Alike: both traditions keep a conventional key (Bell's "accepted Sufi code"; Munidatta's glosses), and both say the surface is not the whole meaning.
  - Different surfaces: the caryā's literal surface is often absurd if read straight (Dasgupta, `charyapada-sound.md` B8). The Persian surface is a coherent love or wine poem. Schimmel's oscillation runs between two coherent poems; Hess's "pointer hub" runs from one strange image to several readings.
  - So a Persian-style English may be fluent at the surface, and a caryā English must not make the strange fluent.

### 5.5 The notes rule, tested: does Sufi practice support "the tradition's readings go in notes, never in the line"?

- **Supported by the poet himself.** Ibn ʿArabī wrote in "the erotic style" and never put "Divine influences" into a line. He kept his reading in a preface, then in a separate book ✔. The best-placed reader of the *Tarjumān* practised the edition's rule.
- **Supported by the translators.** Nicholson kept every reading of the Wine Ode in footnotes. He warned that the commentator al-Nābulusī "not only explains too much but brings in philosophical theories that belong to Ibn ʿArabī rather than to Ibn al-Fāriḍ" ✔ (1921, p. 184). Whinfield's "Love signifies…" is a footnote ✔. Bell met a Western mystic's gloss on Ḥāfiẓ's Shirazi Turk with "This makes everything clear" ✔ (Notes, pp. 129–30).
  - Lesson: every note says whose reading it is; a commentator, Munidatta included, can import a system.
- **No reading is the answer, not even the poet's.** Nicholson on Ibn ʿArabī's own commentary ✔ (1911, pp. 7–8): "his explanations overshoot the mark". To satisfy critics he gave "a precise and definite interpretation of every line and of almost every word", with "far-fetched verbal analogies". He even "admits that in some passages … the mystical import was not clear to himself". Even the author's reading is one spoke of Hess's hub.
- **Challenged in two places.**
  1. **Some poems put the key in the line.** The wine drunk "before the vine was made"; the reed that is "fire, not wind"; 2219's "isn't this the describing of God?" "It is." The rule must mean *add* nothing to the line, not *remove* the poem's own self-glossing paradox. Keeping those is keeping the images.
  2. **The *Masnavī* is commentary in verse.** Its stories stop for explanation inside the couplets, so a notes-only rule would falsify that form. The caryās are songs, nearer the ghazal, and the rule holds for them. A *Masnavī* lens may carry the teaching the source states in its own lines, and only that.
- **The Barks case adds the mirror rule.** The opposite error is to remove what the line names: the Houris, *kufr* and *islām*, the Kaʿba. The edition's "every image stays" forbids it. The Sufi case shows why: the "religion of Love" line is, in its author's own note, Q 3:31 and "a peculiar prerogative of Moslems" ✔ (Nicholson 1911, p. 69). The line keeps its openness, the note keeps its frame, and neither may swallow the other.
