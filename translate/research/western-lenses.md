# Western lenses: how English poets have made songs, and why each suits a caryā

The Workshop's lenses (`prompts/lenses.json`) are ways the English tradition has
made sung, short, charged poems. Each one is a way to hear a caryā, not a
costume to put on it. This note says where each comes from, and where the
edition's own three Englishes sit in the Western argument about translation.

## Three kinds of translation, and three Englishes

John Dryden, in the preface to *Ovid's Epistles* (1680), divided translation
into three kinds:

- **metaphrase**: "word by word, and line by line";
- **paraphrase**: "translation with latitude", keeping the author in view but
  following the sense rather than the words;
- **imitation**: the translator takes "the liberty not only to vary from the
  words and sense, but to forsake them both as he sees occasion".

The edition has all three, and keeps them apart. The word-by-word **gloss** is
metaphrase. The **accurate English** is paraphrase held very close. The **sung
version** stays near paraphrase and carries the song's shape and sound. A
**version** made in the Workshop is the third kind, but a disciplined one. It keeps
the song's shape, its refrain and every image. It keeps the commentary's
readings out of its lines. It says what it changed. The most famous imitation
in English, FitzGerald's *Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám* (1859), and Robert Lowell's
*Imitations* (1961) show both the power and the danger. They made English poems
readers loved, and let readers take the translator for the poet. A version here
is always printed as a version, after the song, with its lens and its maker named.

The nearest precedent for the whole method is Ezra Pound's *Cathay* (1915). Pound
could not read Chinese. He made the poems from Ernest Fenollosa's notebooks,
which gave each character, its sense and a crib. The result changed English
poetry. This edition's gloss is that notebook, and the Workshop is where a maker
works from it.

## The lenses

**Blake** (*Songs of Innocence*, 1789; *Songs of Innocence and of Experience*,
1794). Blake called his method "illuminated printing". Text and design were
etched together in relief on one copper plate, printed, and coloured by hand.
He is the English tradition's one great illuminated poet, and the bridge from
this edition's name to Western poetry. His Songs are short, sung, visionary
poems against priests and systems, mostly in rhymed four-beat couplets, and
many end their lines on the stress ("Tyger Tyger, burning bright"). The caryā's
sixteen-mātrā half-line sits close to that four-beat line.

**The ballad** (the popular ballads of Scotland and the English border, collected
by Francis James Child, 1882–98). A refrain stitched between the lines, as in
"The Twa Sisters" (Child 10) with its "Binnorie, O Binnorie". A story told in leaps.
Incremental repetition. The ballad refrain is the English twin of the caryā's
ধ্রু: a line the singers come back to after every verse, whatever the verse says.

**Dickinson.** Hymn metre (the 8·6·8·6 of the Protestant hymnal), slant rhyme,
the dash, and the riddle stated as a definition. Dickinson made an inward,
ecstatic, riddling poetry out of the plainest church tune, as the siddhas made
theirs out of the plainest folk song. Her slant rhyme is the honest rhyme
English can usually afford. The lens gives no touchstone line, and a version
should not borrow her diction.

**The Psalm** (the King James Bible, 1611). Robert Lowth's lectures *De sacra
poesi Hebraeorum* (1753) showed that Hebrew verse rhymes in **thought**, not
sound (*parallelismus membrorum*): a line in two halves, the second echoing,
answering or sharpening the first. That is why the Psalms survive translation,
and why this project began by asking how Hebrew poetry was carried over. Psalm
136's refrain, "for his mercy endureth for ever", answered after every verse,
is the ধ্রু again.

**Hopkins** (*Poems*, 1918). Sprung rhythm: count only the stresses and let them
crowd. Alliteration and inner chime. Compounds made on the spot. The caryā's
objects (the sky-scoop, the five oars, the lotus root) ask to be struck that
hard.

**Cathay** (Pound, 1915). The cool pole: plain statement, one image to a line,
no rhyme, feeling held back. Set a *Cathay* version beside a hot one (the
Workshop's examples for song 14 do exactly that) and you hear what the
images do on their own, and what the music adds.

**The work song.** Sea shanties and hauling songs of call and response: a
caller's line, the crew's answer, a beat set by the oar or the rope. The ferry
songs of the Charyapada are rowing songs ("Row, Ḍombī, row"). Work songs are
anonymous and oral. The lens takes their structure, never any song's words.

**Owen** (*Poems*, 1920). Para rhyme: the consonant frame kept, the vowel changed
("Strange Meeting": escaped / scooped, groined / groaned). The ear expects a
chime and gets a shudder. It is Morton's coldest rhyme, at the edge of the
solar system, and it belongs to the caryās' cremation grounds, bone garlands
and threats, where a sweet rhyme would lie.

## What no lens may do

The warnings in [`precedents-song-translation.md`](precedents-song-translation.md)
apply with more force to imitations:

- Robert Bly's Kabir and Coleman Barks's Rumi became beloved by leaving the
  sources' religion and specificity behind.
- Arvind Krishna Mehrotra warns against making a medieval poet sound like a
  contemporary one.

So every version keeps the song's own images and its strangeness. It keeps the
teaching's words in the edition's English. It adds no doctrine. It never quotes
a published translation.
