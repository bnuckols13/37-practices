# Research brief: the Zen mountain poets, and what they give a translator of the caryās

*Prepared for section 3 of [`POETRY-PLAN.md`](../POETRY-PLAN.md) ("The Zen mountain poets"). Compiled 27 Sept 2026.*

**How I read.** I read classical Chinese and Japanese through dictionaries, published glosses, rhyme tables and scholarship, not natively. The texts below are checked against open editions (Wikisource transcriptions of the *Quan Tang shi* and other standard texts). Where I scan tones, gloss characters or translate a prose source myself, I say so. Treat my scans as a careful reader's work, to be checked against a rhyme dictionary before print.

**Verification marks.** ✔ = I read the passage at the source (for quotes, the words were checked against the fetched text). ◐ = seen only in a search snippet, a summary or a secondary quotation. UNVERIFIED = I could not confirm it.

**Copyright compliance.** Long quotation here is only of originals (Tang poems, Song and Qing prose, Bashō's and Ryōkan's texts) and of English translations published before 1929: Pound's *Cathay* (1915), Waley's *A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems* (1918), Fletcher's *Gems of Chinese Verse* (1919) and Fenollosa's essay (in Pound's *Instigations*, 1920). Snyder, Watson, Red Pine, Hinton, Weinberger, Hori, Blyth, Shirane and other modern scholars and translators are quoted only on method, never more than 25 words at a time. The working cribs in Part 3 are my own glosses, not versions.

**Access problems.** The Chinese Text Project (ctext.org) refuses automated access, so I did not use it; Wikisource carried the same texts. OAPEN's open-access copy of Rouzer's Hanshan edition served a bot challenge, and De Gruyter's page returned an error, so I could not read Rouzer's introduction. ## Part 1 — The practice: how these poets worked

### 1.1 Hanshan and Shide: poems on rocks, a preface in doubt

- **The legend.** The preface attributed to Lüqiu Yin 閭丘胤, prefect of Taizhou, says that after Hanshan and Shide vanished into a cave that closed behind them, he had the monk Daoqiao gather what they had left: "唯於竹木石壁書詩，並村墅人家廳壁上所書文句三百餘首，及拾得於土地堂壁上書言偈，並纂集成卷" ("only the poems written on bamboo, trees, stones and cliff walls, with the lines written on the walls of village houses, more than three hundred, and the gāthās Shide wrote on the wall of the earth-god shrine; all were gathered into a scroll"; my translation) ✔ [Wikisource, 寒山子詩集序 (全唐文 162)](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%AF%92%E5%B1%B1%E5%AD%90%E8%A9%A9%E9%9B%86%E5%BA%8F).
- **The preface also makes him a singer.** Hanshan "或長廊唱詠" (chanted in the temple's long corridors) and "於村墅與牧牛子而歌笑" (sang and laughed with the village cowherds); Fenggan "夜乃唱歌自樂" (sang at night for his own pleasure). Fenggan names the pair as bodhisattvas: "寒山文殊…拾得普賢" (Hanshan is Mañjuśrī, Shide Samantabhadra) ✔ (same page).
- **A second, Daoist legend.** The *Xianzhuan shiyi* (by Du Guangting ◐), preserved in *Taiping guangji* 55, puts Hanshan in the Dali era (766–779) and says: "每得一篇一句，輒題於樹間石上" (whenever he got a poem or a line he wrote it on a tree or a stone). Admirers copied "三百餘首", and the Daoist Xu Lingfu prefaced and collected them in three scrolls ✔ [Wikisource, 太平廣記 卷55](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E5%A4%AA%E5%B9%B3%E5%BB%A3%E8%A8%98/%E5%8D%B7%E7%AC%AC055). Robert Borgen's article on this "neglected source" is "The Legend of Hanshan: A Neglected Source", *JAOS* 111:3 (1991), 575–79 ◐ ([PhilPapers record](https://philpapers.org/rec/BORTLO-5), blocked).
- **The doubt.** Burton Watson judged that the whole Lüqiu Yin picture "may be nothing more than literary fiction" ✔ (quoted at [en.wikipedia, Hanshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanshan_(poet)), from *Cold Mountain*, 1970, p. 8). Edwin Pulleyblank's rhyme study ("Linguistic Evidence for the Date of Han-shan", 1978) is summarised as putting about two thirds of the poems in the seventh century or earlier and one third in the late Tang ◐ (search summary; Wikipedia says only that the collection "may span the entire Tang dynasty" ✔). Wu Chi-yu (1957) identified Hanshan with the monk Zhiyan; Jia Jinhua (2003) attributed about fifty Chan-flavoured poems to Caoshan Benji ✔ (Wikipedia, citing both).
- **What the poems say about themselves** (all ✔ [Wikisource, 全唐詩 卷806](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E5%85%A8%E5%94%90%E8%A9%A9/%E5%8D%B7806); translations mine):
  - "五言五百篇，七字七十九。三字二十一，都來六百首。一例書巖石，自誇雲好手。" (Five hundred in five-character lines, seventy-nine in seven, twenty-one in three: six hundred in all. All alike written on the cliff rocks; I boast, and call myself a good hand. The text prints 雲 "cloud" for 云 "say".) The poet himself claims only rocks, as Wikipedia notes; about 313 poems survive ✔.
  - "有箇王秀才，笑我詩多失。云不識蜂腰，仍不會鶴膝。平側不解壓，凡言取次出。" (There's a licentiate Wang who laughs that my poems are full of faults: I don't know "wasp waist", can't manage "crane knee", can't press the level and oblique tones, and let any words out as they come.) "Wasp waist" and "crane knee" are two of Shen Yue's "eight defects" of tonal prosody ✔ ([zh.wikipedia, 永明體](https://zh.wikipedia.org/zh-tw/%E5%9B%9B%E8%81%B2%E5%85%AB%E7%97%85)). Hanshan answers: "我笑你作詩，如盲徒詠日" (I laugh at your poems: a blind man chanting about the sun). The colloquial 你 "you" is itself the point.
  - "若遣趁宮商，余病莫能罷" (make me chase the notes *gong* and *shang*, and my faults would never end).
  - "寒山出此語，此語無人信" (Hanshan puts out these words; these words no one believes): the poet names himself in the third person, as a caryā's bhaṇitā does, and strikes "these words" twice.
  - "吟此一曲歌，歌終不是禪" (I chant this one song; the song ends, and it is not Chan; a variant reads 歌中, "in the song there is no Chan").
- **The language.** Victor Mair counts Hanshan's language as more colloquial Medieval Vernacular Sinitic than almost any other Tang poet's ◐ (Wikipedia's paraphrase of Mair, *JAOS* 112:2, 1992, pp. 269, 271). His forms run from three- to seven-character lines, four to thirty-four lines long ✔ (Wikipedia).
- **The whole corpus in English** is available open access in Paul Rouzer's parallel-text edition, *The Poetry of Hanshan (Cold Mountain), Shide, and Fenggan* (De Gruyter, 2017) ◐ ([OAPEN](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/27418/9781501501913.pdf), bot-walled for me).
- **For the practice.** Hanshan's poems are signed by a place, written (the story says) where they would be found, sung before they were collected, and defended in their own lines against the rule-keepers. All four are things a caryā also does.

### 1.2 Wang Wei and Pei Di: a collection of answering poems

- **The Chan layman.** Wang Wei (699–761; dates vary) took the courtesy name Mojie, from Vimalakīrti, the lay bodhisattva of the sutra; the sources call him a devout Chan Buddhist who studied for years with a Chan master ✔ ([en.wikipedia, Wang Wei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_Wei)). Su Shi's verdict, a painting within the poems and poetry within the paintings, is the standard one ✔ (same page).
- **The Wangchuan collection.** Wang Wei's preface lists the sites on his estate in the Wang River valley, Deer Enclosure among them ✔ ([須溪本《王右丞集》](https://www.shidianguji.com/zh/book/SBCK134/chapter/SBCK134_128)) and ends that he and Pei Di "閑暇各賦絕句云爾", each wrote a quatrain on every place in their leisure ◐ (the ending seen in a search snippet). Twenty by Wang Wei, twenty by Pei Di.
- **Pei Di's answer to "Deer Park."** "日夕見寒山，便為獨往客。不知深林事，但有麏麚跡。" (At day's end I see the cold mountain and become the one who goes alone. I don't know what happens in the deep woods: there are only the tracks of roe and stag.) ✔ [Wikisource, 全唐詩 卷129](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E5%85%A8%E5%94%90%E8%A9%A9/%E5%8D%B7129). My observation: the answer picks up the host's own words, 深林 "deep woods" and 但 "only", so the reply is stitched to the poem it answers. It also opens on 寒山, "cold mountain", as a plain landscape word.
- **For the practice.** The Wangchuan poems are a pair-practice: one site, two poets, one quatrain each. A version made in the Workshop beside another maker's version of the same caryā is this form.

### 1.3 Jia Dao: push or knock

- **The monk who left.** The *New Tang History* says Jia Dao (779–843) "初為浮屠，名無本" (was first a Buddhist monk, named Wuben); Han Yu "教其為文，遂去浮屠" (taught him to write, and he left the order). It adds that when he was lost in "苦吟" (bitter chanting, his labour over lines) he failed to notice even great officials, and once rode his donkey into the Metropolitan Governor's train ✔ [Wikisource, 新唐書 卷176](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E6%96%B0%E5%94%90%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7176). Su Shi called his poems "thin" ✔ ([en.wikipedia, Jia Dao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jia_Dao)).
- **Two lines in three years.** Jia Dao's own quatrain: "二句三年得，一吟雙淚流。知音如不賞，歸臥故山秋。" (Two lines, three years to get; I chant them once and both eyes stream. If the friend who knows the tune doesn't prize them, I'll go home and lie down in my old hills' autumn.) ✔ [Wikisource, 全唐詩 卷574](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E5%85%A8%E5%94%90%E8%A9%A9/%E5%8D%B7574).
- **The anecdote.** In Ji Yougong's *Tangshi jishi* (Song): on his donkey Jia Dao found "鳥宿池邊樹，僧敲月下門"; "初欲著推字，或欲著敲字，煉之未定" (first he wanted "push", then "knock", and smelted it without settling), making the gesture of pushing and then of knocking, until he ran into the train of Han Yu, acting Governor. He told Han Yu he had "吟安一字未定" (not yet settled one word). "韓立馬良久，思之，謂島曰：『作敲字佳矣。』" (Han Yu reined in his horse a long while, thought, and said: "Knock is better.") ✔ as transcribed at a [Hsinchu school page](https://www.hchs.hc.edu.tw/ischool/publish_page/14/?cid=387); the printed edition ◐. From this, 推敲 *tuīqiāo* became the Chinese word for revising.
- **Three verdicts on one word:**
  - **Han Yu:** knock.
  - **Zhu Guangqian** ("咬文嚼字", 1943): "push" means the monk returns alone to a gate he left unlatched himself; "knock" means someone inside will answer, and "推可以無聲，敲就不免剝啄有聲" (push can be silent; knock cannot help making a sound), which wakes the roosting birds. To revise words is at bottom to revise thought and feeling ◐ ([newton.com.tw summary of the essay](https://www.newton.com.tw/wiki/%E5%92%AC%E6%96%87%E5%9A%BC%E5%AD%97/3416781)).
  - **Wang Fuzhi** (1619–1692), dissenting: "「僧敲月下門」只是妄想揣摩，如說他人夢…若即景會心，則或「推」或「敲」，必居其一，因景因情，自然靈妙，何勞擬議哉？" (The line is only guesswork, like telling someone else's dream. Had the scene met the mind, it would have been push or knock, one or the other, following the scene and the feeling; why labour over it?) He calls that direct meeting "禪家所謂「現量」" (what the Chan school calls direct perception) ✔ [Wikisource, 薑齋詩話](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E8%96%91%E9%BD%8B%E8%A9%A9%E8%A9%B1). 現量 translates Sanskrit *pratyakṣa* ◐ (standard Buddhist-logic sense; not checked at a dictionary here).
- **A second one-word revision.** Hong Mai records Wang Anshi's draft of "春風又綠江南岸" (the spring wind greens the south bank again): "初云『又到江南岸』，圈去『到』字，注曰『不好』，改為『過』，復圈去而改為『入』，旋改為『滿』，凡如是十許字，始定為『綠』" (first "reaches", circled "no good"; then "passes", "enters", "fills", about ten words, before settling on "greens") ✔ [Wikisource, 容齋續筆 卷八](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E5%AE%B9%E9%BD%8B%E7%BA%8C%E7%AD%86/%E5%8D%B7%E5%85%AB). The draft with its circled rejections is the model for a revision record.

### 1.4 Stonehouse, Ryōkan, Ikkyū

- **Stonehouse** (Shiwu Qinggong 石屋清珙, 1272–1352), a Yuan Chan master who wrote his mountain poems late in life in a hut; the publisher's description of Red Pine's translation quotes Stonehouse's instruction "Do not try singing these poems. Only if you sit on them will they do you any good." ◐ ([Goodreads listing of *The Mountain Poems of Stonehouse*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/651556.The_Mountain_Poems_of_Stonehouse)). I did not find the Chinese. It is the exact opposite of the caryā, which is headed by a rāga and meant to be sung.
- **Ryōkan** (Taigu Ryōkan, 1758–1831), a Sōtō monk who lived much of his life as a hermit (at the hut Gogō-an in his native Echigo ◐), wrote waka and Chinese poems (kanshi), refused any post as priest or "poet", and forgot his begging round when playing with the village children ✔ ([en.wikipedia, Ryōkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ry%C5%8Dkan)). His kanshi ✔ in the Japanese reading at [良寛ワールド](https://ryoukan-w.info/?page_id=74):
  - "孰か我が詩を詩なりと謂ふ／我が詩は是れ詩に非ず／我が詩の詩に非ざるを知りて／始めて与に詩を謂ふ可し" (Who calls my poems poems? My poems are not poems. Know that my poems are not poems, and then we can talk about poetry.) Hanshan's defence against the tone-keepers, turned into a koan.
  - "今日食を乞ふて驟雨に逢ひ／暫時廻避す古祠の中" (Begging today I met a sudden shower and sheltered a while in an old shrine): the begging round (*takuhatsu*) as the day's event.
  - "袖裏の毬子直千金／謂ふ言は好手にして等匹無しと" (the ball in my sleeve is worth a thousand gold; I tell you I'm a good hand without equal). My observation: this echoes Hanshan's "自誇雲好手" (I boast, and call myself a good hand). That Ryōkan read Hanshan is widely reported ◐ ([Hermitary](https://www.hermitary.com/articles/ryokan.html)).
  - His death verse to the nun Teishin, "ura wo mise / omote wo misete / chiru momiji" (showing its back, showing its front, the maple leaf falls) ✔ (Wikipedia, after Hoffmann, *Japanese Death Poems*, 1986).
- **Ikkyū** Sōjun (1394–1481), Rinzai monk and poet of the *Kyōunshū* ("Crazy Cloud Anthology"). His teacher named him Ikkyū, "one pause", after he broke through his koan while listening to a band of blind singers. Late in life he loved a blind singer, Mori ✔ ([en.wikipedia, Ikkyū](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikky%C5%AB)). Standard study: Sonja Arntzen, *Ikkyū and the Crazy Cloud Anthology* (1986; rev. 2022) ✔ (cited there).

### 1.5 Bashō: a group art, a road, and prose with poems

- **Haikai as a group practice.** Renga is linked verse made in sessions by a group of poets (*renju*, usually seven or eight), a scribe and a master. Its stanzas of 5-7-5 and 7-7 morae link only to their neighbours. The first stanza (*hokku*) must stand alone and reflect the season and the place of the gathering. In the Edo period the favoured form was the 36-verse *kasen*, and Bashō (1644–1694) was its greatest poet ✔ ([en.wikipedia, Renga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renga)).
- **Haibun and the road.** Bashō first used the word *haibun* in a letter of 1690: prose in the haikai spirit, joined with hokku. *Oku no hosomichi* is the famous extended example ✔ ([en.wikipedia, Haibun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haibun), citing Shirane, *Traces of Dreams*, p. 212 ◐).
- **Bashō on Hanshan.** In his afterword to Kikaku's anthology *Minashiguri* (1683): "李・杜が心酒を嘗めて、寒山が法粥を啜る。これに仍つて、其句、見るに遥かにして、聞くに遠し" (it tastes the heart-wine of Li Bai and Du Fu and sips Hanshan's dharma-gruel; so its verses are far to see and distant to hear). The same page speaks of smelting verses in a treasure cauldron, the Chinese metaphor for choosing words ✔ ([Yamanashi transcription](https://www2.yamanashi-ken.ac.jp/~itoyo/basho/others/minasiguri_preface.htm)).
- **The old pond was made back to front.** Shikō's *Kuzu no matsubara* (1692) says that on a spring day in 1686 Bashō first had "kawazu tobikomu mizu no oto"; Kikaku proposed "yamabuki ya" (golden kerria, the frog's classical partner), and Bashō chose "furuike ya" ✔ (as told by Geoffrey Wilkinson, ["Bashō's frog, the great survivor", *Frogpond* 41:2 (2018)](https://www.hsa-haiku.org/frogpond/2018-issue41-2/Wilkinson-BashosFrog-Fp41-2.pdf), pp. 100–101).

### 1.6 Chan and Zen practices that are poems

- **Gāthās and death verses.** The Hanshan preface already calls Shide's wall-writing 偈 (gāthā) ✔. The death poem is a practice with its origin in Zen, in kanshi or waka form. Bashō's last poem, "tabi ni yande / yume wa kareno o / kakemeguru" (sick on a journey, my dreams run over withered fields), is counted as his farewell ✔ ([en.wikipedia, Death poem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_poem)).
- **Capping phrases (jakugo 著語).** Victor Sōgen Hori describes the practice from inside Daitoku-ji. Once a monk passes a koan, the teacher sends him to find a capping phrase that expresses its point. The standard handbook, the *Zenrin kushū*, has more than 4,200 verses and phrases. It grew from the *Kuzōshi* (about 1,200 phrases, compiled around 1490 by Tōyō Eichō) and was revised by Ijūshi in 1683 (or 1687), so the exercise predates Hakuin. Some phrase books were indexed by rhyme class and grouped as 4-, 5- and 7-character couplets. Technical Buddhist terms are said to "stink of Zen"; the monk answers in poetry or everyday speech instead. Hori calls the practice "literary training in the use of words and letters that are not themselves founded on words and letters" ✔ [Hori, "Translating the Zen Phrase Book", *Nanzan Bulletin* 23 (1999), 44–58](https://nirc.nanzan-u.ac.jp/journal/3/article/61/pdf/download). The full phrase book is Hori, *Zen Sand: The Book of Capping Phrases for Kōan Practice* (University of Hawai'i Press, 2003) ✔ ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/8068)).
- **Reply poems.** The Qing critic Wu Qiao sorts them ✔ ([zh.wikipedia, 次韻](https://zh.wikipedia.org/zh-tw/%E6%AC%A1%E9%9F%BB)):
  - **和詩** *hè shī*: an answer that does not share the rhyme;
  - **和韻** *hè yùn*: the same rhyme class, other words;
  - **用韻** *yòng yùn*: the same rhyme words in another order;
  - **步韻** or **次韻** *cì yùn*: the same rhyme words in the same order.
  
  Wu Qiao: "步韻最困人，如相敺而自縶手足也" (stepping on the rhymes is the most confining: like fighting with your own hands and feet tied). Rhyme-matching began in the poems Yuan Zhen and Bai Juyi exchanged by post, and became a set form with Su Shi and Huang Tingjian ✔ (same page). Later Chan monks answered the whole Hanshan corpus: Chushi Fanqi (1296–1370) wrote *He Tiantai sansheng shi* ◐ ([Merit Times](https://www.merit-times.com.tw/NewsPage.aspx?unid=380754)).
- **Chan in the criticism itself.** The monk Huihong (1071–1128) was the first to use "poetic eye" (詩眼) in a prose discussion of poetry. Sarah Jane Babcock reads the term, whose variant in most editions is 法眼, "dharma-eye", as "a readerly approach to poetry shaped by a Huayan non-discriminatory perception of the world". The explicit Chan–poetry analogy was later made most famously by Yan Yu ✔ [Babcock, dissertation (UC Santa Barbara, 2020), pp. 121–22](https://escholarship.org/content/qt1w18f0fv/qt1w18f0fv.pdf).

## Part 2 — The machinery

### 2.1 Chinese verse

- **Lines and the pause.** Lines are of five or seven characters, one syllable each. The major caesura falls before the last three syllables, with a minor pause after the first pair in a seven-character line ✔ ([en.wikipedia, Regulated verse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulated_verse)): 2+3 (空山｜不見人) and 2+2+3, heard as 4+3.
- **Level and oblique tones (平仄).** Of Middle Chinese's four tones, the level (平) stands against the other three, grouped as oblique (仄). Regulated verse alternates them in pairs; the two lines of a couplet are opposed (相對), and the second line of one couplet "sticks" to the first line of the next (相黏) ✔ ([zh.wikipedia, 近體詩](https://zh.wikipedia.org/zh-tw/%E8%BF%91%E9%AB%94%E8%A9%A9)). Waley put it for English readers in 1918: in old style "the tones were disregarded"; in the new style the tones tend "to go in pairs" ✔ ([Waley, *A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems*, pp. 24–26](https://archive.org/details/hundredseventych00wale)).
- **Regulated verse.** The *lǜshī* has eight lines, the *juéjù* four; both rhyme on the even lines, on a level tone, as a rule one rhyme throughout ✔ (zh.wikipedia, 近體詩). The rules grew from Shen Yue's "four tones and eight defects" (the wasp waist and crane knee among them) in the late fifth century and were fixed by Shen Quanqi and Song Zhiwen in the early Tang ✔ (Wikipedia, both pages).
- **Ancient-style verse (古體詩).** Hanshan mostly writes it: tones free, oblique rhymes allowed, line counts free ✔ (Waley's description, above; Hanshan's forms, 1.1). His laugh at the licentiate Wang is a poem about this very choice.
- **Parallel couplets (對仗).** In the *lǜshī* the middle couplets (lines 3–4 and 5–6) must be parallel ✔ (zh.wikipedia). The poet marks the parallel "by using the same parts of speech in each position" ✔ ([en.wikipedia, Classical Chinese poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Chinese_poetry)). The Japanese monk Kūkai's *Bunkyō hifuron* already listed twenty-nine kinds of pairing ✔ (zh.wikipedia, 近體詩). Waley thought the device "used with some discretion in T'ang" became "an irritating trick in the hands of the Sung poets" ✔ (p. 26).
- **Parallelism as equivalence.** Kao Yu-kung and Mei Tsu-lin bring Jakobson's principle of equivalence (where Morton's "hot" comes from) to Tang poetry: "rhyme and alliteration are phonetic equivalences, parallelism and antithesis are in part grammatical equivalences" ✔ ["Meaning, Metaphor, and Allusion in T'ang Poetry", *HJAS* 38:2 (1978), p. 285](https://msellisliteraturelab.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/meaning-metaphor-and-allusion-in-tang-poetry-jstor.pdf). So a parallel couplet is a rhyme of grammar, and the board can count it as heat.
- **The poem eye (詩眼).** Huang Tingjian's "eye within the lines" once meant a whole line or couplet. By the Southern Song it meant the most vivid single word. One lost miscellany fixed it by position: "五字詩以第三字為句眼，七字詩以第五字為句眼，古人鍊字，只於句眼上鍊" (the third character of a five-character line, the fifth of a seven, is the eye; the ancients smelted their words only at the eye) ✔ (Babcock, pp. 167–68). My observation: the famous revisions don't sit there. 敲 is the second character, 綠 the fourth. The eye is wherever the verb carries the line.
- **Grammar without subject or tense.** Kao and Mei describe Tang "imagistic language" as "discontinuous and objective", built from simple noun-images set side by side ✔ (1978, pp. 282–86, summarising their "Syntax, Diction, and Imagery in T'ang Poetry", *HJAS* 31, 1971). Fenollosa's version, for all its errors about the script: "A true noun, an isolated thing, does not exist in nature. Things are only the terminal points, or rather the meeting points of actions" ✔ (below, Part 4).
- **Empty and full words (虛字 / 實字).** Full words name things and actions; empty words (negatives, adverbs, particles) carry relation. My observation: in "Deer Park" the full words are all landscape, and every hinge is empty: 不 "not", 但 "only", 復 "again". The turn is made of grammar.
- **Scene and feeling (景 / 情).** Wang Fuzhi: "情、景名為二，而實不可離…巧者則有情中景，景中情" (feeling and scene are two in name but cannot be separated; the skilful make scene within feeling, feeling within scene). He calls "one couplet feeling, one scene" in the middle of regulated verse only "one method" ✔ (薑齋詩話, above).

### 2.2 Japanese verse

- **Morae, not syllables.** Hokku and haiku have 17 *on* (morae) in phrases of 5, 7 and 5 ✔ ([en.wikipedia, Kireji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kireji)).
- **The season word (kigo).** The hokku was expected to reflect the season and place of the gathering, and the season word carried over into haiku ✔ (Wikipedia, Renga). "Frog" is a season word for spring ✔ (Wilkinson, p. 101).
- **The cutting word (kireji).** Renga had eighteen; the commonest are *ya*, *kana* and *keri* ✔ (Wikipedia, Kireji, citing Shirane, *Traces of Dreams*, p. 100 ◐). At the end of a verse, *kana* and *keri* close it and send the reader back to the start. In the middle, a kireji "both cutting and joining": it makes two centres and an implied comparison or equation between them ✔ (Wikipedia, citing Shirane pp. 101–102 ◐). English marks it with a dash or an ellipsis, or not at all ✔.
- **The cut as juxtaposition.** Yamamoto Kenkichi distinguishes *toriawase*, the "combination" of two images (which *yamabuki ya* would have been), from Bashō's *furuike*, which is "an essence… distilled from the scene" of the next twelve syllables and works on the "auditory imagination" ✔ (Wilkinson's translation, pp. 104–105).

## Part 3 — Close readings on Morton's board

The board is the one in [`how-to-read-a-song.md`](how-to-read-a-song.md): four channels, each from 0 (cool: variation, surprise) to 1 (hot: repetition, pattern). Heats are my estimates, source side, for the ear to check. Tone scans are mine, from Middle Chinese tone categories. Rhyme groups are checked against the Pingshui rhyme table ✔ ([Wikisource, 平水韻](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E5%B9%B3%E6%B0%B4%E9%9F%BB)).

### 3.1 Hanshan, "人問寒山道" (People ask the way to Cold Mountain)

✔ [全唐詩 卷806](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E5%85%A8%E5%94%90%E8%A9%A9/%E5%8D%B7806). Pinyin in modern Mandarin; glosses mine.

| | characters | pinyin | character by character |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 人問寒山道 | rén wèn Hánshān dào | person · ask · cold · mountain · road (also: the Way) |
| 2 | 寒山路不通 | Hánshān lù bù tōng | cold · mountain · path · not · go-through |
| 3 | 夏天冰未釋 | xià tiān bīng wèi shì | summer · sky/season · ice · not-yet · melt |
| 4 | 日出霧朦朧 | rì chū wù ménglóng | sun · come-out · fog · dim-hazy (a rhyming pair) |
| 5 | 似我何由屆 | sì wǒ hé yóu jiè | resemble · me · what · means · arrive |
| 6 | 與君心不同 | yǔ jūn xīn bù tóng | with · you · heart-mind · not · same |
| 7 | 君心若似我 | jūn xīn ruò sì wǒ | you · heart-mind · if · resemble · me |
| 8 | 還得到其中 | huán (hái) dé dào qí zhōng | then · get-to · arrive · its · middle |

- **Working crib** (mine): People ask the way to Cold Mountain. / Cold Mountain: the road doesn't go through. / Summer, and the ice not yet melted; / the sun up, and the fog a blur. / Like me: how would you get here? / Your mind and mine are not the same. / If your mind were like mine, / you'd be in the middle of it.

| channel | heat | why |
|---|---|---|
| lineation | 0.9 | Eight lines of exactly five characters, each closed, in four couplets; nothing runs on. |
| syntax | 0.65 | Lines 1–4 lay images side by side; lines 5–8 argue, with a question (何由, "by what means") and a condition (若, "if"), the poem's only subordination. |
| rhythm | 0.8 | Every line pauses 2+3. By my scan the tones keep the regulated alternation at positions 2 and 4 in all eight lines: strict for a poet whose critic, in another poem, says he cannot press the tones. |
| rhyme | 0.85 | Four level-tone rhymes, all in the Pingshui group 一東 (通, 朧, 同, 中), each perfect; the rhyming binome 朦朧 inside line 4; and the words struck again. |

- **Rhyme:** perfect on every even line (4 of 4), Morton's Mercury and Venus.
- **Words struck again:** 寒山 twice, chained across the line break (寒山道，寒山路); 似我 twice (5, 7); 君心 across the break (與君心 / 君心); 不 twice. My observation: in modern Mandarin the poem opens on 道 *dào* (road) and closes on 到 *dào* (arrive). In Middle Chinese the two share a final but not an initial or tone ◐ (standard reconstructions; unchecked here).
- **Narrator:** an "I" (我) who is the mountain's namesake, and a "you" (君), the one asking. The signature is inside the first line: the name is the place.
- **The turn:** line 5, 似我 ("like me"). The mountain becomes the man; the road that "doesn't go through" (line 2) becomes a mind that "is not the same" (line 6). Morton's A to not-A, made twice on the word 不.
- **The double language.** 道 is both "road" and "the Way"; 寒山 is the place, the poet and the state; 釋 "melt" is also the first syllable of 釋迦, Śākya, the Buddha's clan, and the surname Chinese monks take. Following the edition's rule, the line keeps "road" and "melt", and the readings go in a note.

### 3.2 Wang Wei, "鹿柴" (Deer Park; literally Deer Enclosure)

✔ [全唐詩 卷128](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E5%85%A8%E5%94%90%E8%A9%A9/%E5%8D%B7128). 柴 is read *zhài* ("fence, enclosure"); 景 is read *jǐng* (sunlight) in the school edition and *yǐng* (as 影, reflected light) in older glosses ◐ ([程少峰, 語文建設 2022](https://m.fx361.com/news/2022/0606/10377941.html)).

| | characters | pinyin | character by character |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 空山不見人 | kōng shān bú jiàn rén | empty · mountain · not · see · person |
| 2 | 但聞人語響 | dàn wén rén yǔ xiǎng | only · hear · person · talk · sound/echo |
| 3 | 返景入深林 | fǎn jǐng (yǐng) rù shēn lín | return · light · enter · deep · wood |
| 4 | 復照青苔上 | fù zhào qīng tái shàng | again · shine · blue-green · moss · on |

| channel | heat | why |
|---|---|---|
| lineation | 0.9 | Four five-character lines, each closed; two couplets. |
| syntax | 0.9 | Four clauses side by side, with no subject and nothing hung under; the only hinges are the empty words 不, 但, 復. |
| rhythm | 0.65 | The 2+3 pause holds in every line, but by my scan the tones break the regulated opposition between lines 1–2 and 3–4. With its oblique rhyme, this makes it an ancient-style quatrain. |
| rhyme | 0.55 | Lines 2 and 4 rhyme on an oblique tone (響, 上: both 養 in the Pingshui table), a perfect rhyme; 人 struck across lines 1–2. |

- **Rhyme:** perfect, on the even lines only (響 / 上), and oblique: the one chime the quatrain allows itself.
- **Words struck again:** 人 "person" (not seen; then heard). 返 "return" and 復 "again" are a doubling of sense across lines 3–4.
- **Narrator:** none. No 我. Who sees and hears is never said.
- **The turn:** line 3, on 返 "return": from sound back to light, from 不見 "not see" to 照 "shine".
- **The hot pole in English: W. J. B. Fletcher (1919), public domain:**

  > So lone seem the hills; there is no one in sight there.
  > But whence is the echo of voices I hear?
  > The rays of the sunset pierce slanting the forest,
  > And in their reflection green mosses appear.

  ✔ [Fletcher, *Gems of Chinese Verse* (Shanghai, 1919), p. 123](https://archive.org/details/gemsofchinesever00fletiala). It runs hot in rhythm (anapests) and rhyme (hear / appear), cooler in syntax (a question, "in their reflection"). It adds a narrator ("I hear") and a question the Chinese never asks.
- **What *Nineteen Ways* shows** (Eliot Weinberger, with Octavio Paz, *Nineteen Ways of Looking at Wang Wei*, Moyer Bell, 1987; expanded New Directions, 2016; all ◐ through [en.wikipedia's page-cited summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineteen_Ways_of_Looking_at_Wang_Wei)):
  - It opens: "Poetry is that which is worth translating."
  - The received text counts as the first "way", the pinyin as the second and a character-by-character gloss as the third. Then come sixteen translations in English, French and Spanish, in order of publication, from Fletcher (1919) to Snyder (1978).
  - Weinberger marks the "I" and "we" that translators add, where the Chinese has no narrator.
  - Weinberger attacks the urge to "explain" and "make improvements". Of one ornate version: Wang could have written it that way had he wanted to; "He didn't."
  - Weinberger weighs poet-translators (Rexroth) against scholar-translators (Watson) without taking accuracy as the only end.
  - The 2016 edition adds ten more ways, so the study stays open.
- **A model for the Workshop's Study room.** Show the source as way one, the transliteration and the gloss as ways two and three: this is the edition's own ladder, and it matches Weinberger's order. Then show each English in date order, each with its board beside the source's board and a one-line comment in Weinberger's manner ("adds an I", "explains the moss", "keeps 'only'"). Let the maker's own version be the last way, and let the list grow.

### 3.3 Jia Dao, "題李凝幽居": one word in a parallel couplet

✔ [全唐詩 卷572](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E5%85%A8%E5%94%90%E8%A9%A9/%E5%8D%B7572): 閒居少鄰並，草徑入荒園。**鳥宿池邊樹，僧敲月下門。** 過橋分野色，移石動雲根。暫去還來此，幽期不負言。 (Written at Li Ning's secluded house. The retreat has few neighbours; a grass path enters the wild garden. / Birds roost in the trees by the pool; a monk knocks at the gate under the moon. / Crossing the bridge divides the wild colours; moving a stone stirs the cloud-root [a stock word for rock ◐]. / I go for a while and will come back here; our quiet appointment won't break its word.) Rhymes 園, 門, 根, 言, all Pingshui 十三元 ✔. To a Mandarin ear they now chime unevenly (-uan, -en, -en, -an): rhyme is heard, not spelled, and the hearing changes over time.

| position | line 3 | pinyin | line 4 | pinyin | pairing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 鳥 bird | niǎo | 僧 monk | sēng | creature / creature |
| 2 | 宿 roost | sù | 敲 knock | qiāo | verb / verb: rest / act |
| 3–4 | 池邊 pool-side | chí biān | 月下 moon-under | yuè xià | noun + place-word |
| 5 | 樹 tree | shù | 門 gate | mén | thing / thing |

| channel | heat | why |
|---|---|---|
| lineation | 0.95 | Two closed five-character lines: the required parallel couplet (頷聯) of a regulated poem. |
| syntax | 0.95 | Two clauses of identical frame (subject, verb, place, object) side by side; nothing subordinate. |
| rhythm | 0.9 | Exact tonal opposition, 仄仄平平仄 / 平平仄仄平 (my scan). |
| rhyme | 0.5 | Only 門 rhymes within the couplet (with the poem's other even lines); the heat here is grammatical equivalence, not sound. |

- **The lesson in one word.** 推 *tuī* and 敲 *qiāo* are both level tone, so the tone rules could not choose between them. Nor could the grammar: both are verbs taking 門. What decided was:
  - **sound:** a knock is heard, a push is not;
  - **scene:** whose gate it is, and whether anyone is inside (Zhu Guangqian);
  - **the turn:** "knock" puts a sound into the still line about roosting birds.
  
  Wang Fuzhi's objection is the translator's rule. The choice should come from the scene, not from weighing words. For a translator the scene is the source: go back to the gloss, not to the thesaurus.
- **Rhyme:** 門 closes the couplet on the poem's one rhyme (十三元); 樹, ending an odd line, is oblique and unrhymed, as the rules want.
- **Words struck again:** none. The heat is all pairing.
- **Narrator:** none; the monk may be the poet, who was one.
- **The turn:** inside the couplet, from rest (宿) to act (敲), stillness to sound; across the poem, from arriving (lines 1–4) to going and promising to return (lines 7–8).

### 3.4 Bashō, "古池や" (the old pond)

古池や蛙飛こむ水の音 ✔ (as printed by Wilkinson, p. 99). *furu ike ya / kawazu tobikomu / mizu no oto* — old · pond · [cut] / frog · jump-in / water · 's · sound. 5 + 7 + 5 = 17 morae; *kawazu* (frog) is the spring season word; *ya* is the cut.

| channel | heat | why |
|---|---|---|
| lineation | 0.7 | Three metrical phrases, 5-7-5, written as one line in Japanese, with one cut. |
| syntax | 0.9 | A noun phrase, a cut, then a clause and a noun phrase: juxtaposed, with no connective and no subject. |
| rhythm | 0.75 | Exactly the count the form demands, 5-7-5 morae. |
| rhyme | 0.25 | No end rhyme; the vowels *u* and *o* run through (*furu*, *tobikomu*, *mizu no oto*). |

- **Rhyme:** none at the ends; the chime is vowel colour inside the phrases (Morton's Earth and Mars, faintly).
- **Words struck again:** none; water is named twice in different words (*ike*, *mizu*).
- **Narrator:** none. A hearer is implied only by *oto*, "sound".
- **The turn:** at *ya*, and again at *mizu no oto*: from a thing to an act to a sound.
- **The cut and its revision.** Kikaku's *yamabuki ya* would have made a *toriawase*, the flower beside the frog as the classical poets paired them. Shirane: it "would have left [his] hokku within the circle of classical associations". Bashō's frog jumps where the tradition had it sing ✔ (Shirane, *Traces of Dreams*, 1998, p. 15, quoted by Wilkinson, p. 101). So *furuike ya* is itself a one-phrase revision. It changes what the cut sets side by side, as 敲 changed what the couplet sets side by side.
- **What Morton does with it: UNVERIFIED.** The brief reports that Morton reads the old pond in class 11 of the course, and treats negative imagery, emptiness and apophasis in classes 1, 9 and 10. The maker's copy of the course notes is not in the repo, and I found no published reading by Morton of this poem. What follows is the method as [`how-to-read-a-song.md`](how-to-read-a-song.md) and the plan record it, not Morton's words:
  - **Structure:** a pause made audible. The cut is lineation inside the line.
  - **Perception:** sensory forcing moves from sight or time ("old pond") to motion to hearing.
  - **Ambient poetics:** the poem ends on what the plan calls the Aeolian, sound made by water, not by a voice.
  - **Narrative:** the smallest story, stillness to sound, whether or not one adds Takeuchi Yoshinori's return to stillness (as Wilkinson reports it, p. 105).
- **What all four touchstones share** (my synthesis). Each turns on a sound in a stillness or on a "not":
  - Hanshan: 不通, 未釋, 不同 (doesn't go through, not yet melted, not the same);
  - Wang Wei: 不見 (not seen) and then voices heard;
  - Jia Dao: a knock among roosting birds;
  - Bashō: a splash in an old pond.
  
  That is Morton's negative imagery done by concrete means. The rule for an English is: keep the "not", keep the sound, and add no one to hear it.

## Part 4 — What survives into English, and why

- **Pound and Fenollosa, *Cathay* (1915).** The title page reads "for the most part from the Chinese of Rihaku, from the notes of the late Ernest Fenollosa, and the decipherings of the professors Mori and Ariga" ✔ ([Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/50155/50155-h/50155-h.htm)).
  - **What carries: parallelism.** "Blue mountains to the north of the walls, / White river winding about them;" keeps Li Bai's parallel couplet 青山橫北郭，白水繞東城 ✔ ([全唐詩 卷177](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E5%85%A8%E5%94%90%E8%A9%A9/%E5%8D%B7177)).
  - **What is lost: the cut.** In the same poem, 浮雲遊子意，落日故人情 sets "floating cloud: the traveller's mind; setting sun: the old friend's feeling" side by side, and Pound supplies "like": "Mind like a floating wide cloud. / Sunset like the parting of old acquaintances". The juxtaposition becomes a simile.
  - **What carries: the word struck again.** Pound's closing note gives a Li Bai couplet (抽刀斷水水更流，舉杯銷愁愁更愁 ✔ 卷177) "in a literality which will be to many most unacceptable": "Drawing sword, cut into water, water again flow: / Raise cup, quench sorrow, sorrow again sorry." ✔
  - **What carries: doubling.** The first of the "Four Poems of Departure" is Wang Wei's 渭城曲 (渭城朝雨浥輕塵，客舍青青柳色新… ✔ 卷128). Pound: "Light rain is on the light dust. / The willows of the inn-yard / Will be going greener and greener". The doubled 青青 survives as "greener and greener", and Pound adds a doubling of his own ("light… light"). Pound's attribution wavered between Li Bai and Wang Wei ◐ (secondary).
  - **What is lost: allusion.** Pound's note to "The Jewel Stairs' Grievance" has to do in prose what the poem does by allusion: "Jewel stairs, therefore a palace… The poem is especially prized because she utters no direct reproach." ✔ This is the edition's own solution: the reading goes in a note, and the line stays bare.
- **Fenollosa's essay** (in *Instigations*, 1920; ✔ [Gutenberg, *Instigations*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/40852/pg40852.txt)): "we must hold as closely as possible to the concrete force of the original, eschewing adjectives, nouns and intransitive forms wherever we can, and seeking instead strong and individual verbs." That is the poem eye as a translation rule. His theory that characters are "shorthand picture[s]" is wrong for most of the script (a Yale sinologist called the essay "a small mass of confusion" ◐, publisher's summary), but the verb rule stands on its own.
- **Arthur Waley, *A Hundred and Seventy Chinese Poems* (1918)** ✔ ([archive.org](https://archive.org/details/hundredseventych00wale), pp. 33–34):
  - "I have aimed at literal translation, not paraphrase."
  - "Above all, considering imagery to be the soul of poetry, I have avoided either adding images of my own or suppressing those of the original." This is the edition's rule for versions, stated a century early.
  - "Each character in the Chinese is represented by a stress in the English": a rhythm rule that keeps the line's weight.
  - He did not use rhyme because English cannot reproduce Chinese rhyme "where the same rhyme sometimes runs through a whole poem".
  - "Blank verse" is "the worst medium" because it "varies the position of its pauses, whereas in Chinese the stop always comes at the end of the couplet."
  - The hot alternative was H. A. Giles's rhymed *Chinese Poetry in English Verse* (1898), which Waley's bibliography praises for combining "rhyme and literalness with wonderful dexterity" ✔ (Waley's list dates it 1896). Fletcher (3.2) is the same school.
- **Gary Snyder, "Cold Mountain Poems"** (*Evergreen Review* 2:6, 1958; 24 poems). Snyder's introduction says Hanshan and Shide turn up "in the skidrows, orchards, hobo jungles, and logging camps of America" ✔ (quoted at [en.wikipedia, Hanshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanshan_(poet))). Kerouac's *The Dharma Bums* (1958) ends with a vision of Hanshan, and at Snyder's suggestion Kerouac dedicated the book to Hanshan ✔ (Wikipedia, citing Suiter). An account of Snyder's method, internalising and visualising "the taste of the whole scene" before writing, is ◐ ([terebess compilation](https://terebess.hu/zen/chang/hanshan.html)). The colloquial Hanshan went into American vernacular easily. That is Mehrotra's warning from [`charyapada-sound.md`](charyapada-sound.md) (B4) in reverse: it worked because Hanshan's own register was low.
- **Burton Watson** (1925–2017), *Cold Mountain: 100 Poems* (Grove, 1962): plain scholar's English, with the biography deduced "from the poems themselves" ✔ (Wikipedia). Watson also translated Ryōkan (1977) ✔ ([en.wikipedia, Burton Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burton_Watson)).
- **Red Pine** (Bill Porter), *The Collected Songs of Cold Mountain* (1983; rev. 2000): the complete corpus, bilingual, and titled "songs" ✔ (Wikipedia). Red Pine now calls the work "closer to dance" ◐ ([Kyoto Journal interview, 2011](https://kyotojournal.org/fiction-poetry/dancing-with-words/)).
- **David Hinton** (*Mountain Home*, 2002, and later books) reads the rivers-and-mountains poets as a wilderness cosmology: 自然 rendered "occurrence appearing of itself", and Absence and Presence (無 / 有) as its two faces ◐ ([Hermitary review](https://www.hermitary.com/bookreviews/hinton.html)). In an interview Hinton gives a line like "hear temple bell", with no "you" in it, as the grammar that makes the experience ◐ ([davidhinton.net interview](https://www.davidhinton.net/interview), summary only).
- **Kenneth Rexroth,** *One Hundred Poems from the Japanese* (1955) and *from the Chinese* (1956) ✔ ([en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Rexroth)). His "Deer Park" (1970) is one of Weinberger's nineteen ◐.
- **R. H. Blyth** for haiku (*Haiku*, 4 vols., Hokuseidō, vol. I cited by Wilkinson as 1950; *A History of Haiku*, vol. I, 1963): "Haiku is a kind of satori, or enlightenment, in which we see into the life of things" ✔ (quoted by Wilkinson, p. 104, from *Haiku* I, p. vii). "See into the life of things" is Wordsworth's line. Wilkinson knows no authoritative Japanese commentator who went as far as Blyth in making haiku a form of Zen. Japan does have Zen readings of the frog: a 1795 commentary invokes Hakuin's one hand, and D. T. Suzuki adds a disputed "true history" of the poem's making ✔ (Wilkinson, pp. 104–107). *One Hundred Frogs* (Hiroaki Sato, 1983), a hundred versions of the old pond, is the Japanese twin of *Nineteen Ways* ✔ (cited at [en.wikipedia, Renga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renga)). Wilkinson also warns that forcing 5-7-5 syllables in English yields "repetitive gibberish" ✔ (p. 103).

| what carries | why | what is lost | why |
|---|---|---|---|
| the image, in order | nouns and verbs translate; Waley's rule | tone (平仄) | English has no pitch-contrast metre; Weinberger calls it untranslatable (p. 5 ◐) |
| parallelism | a grammatical rhyme (Kao and Mei); Lowth's point about the Psalms | the rhyme that runs one sound through a whole poem | English has too few rhymes on one vowel (Waley) |
| the plain statement | Tang lines are clauses side by side | the shape of the pause (2+3, the couplet stop) | English pauses float (Waley on blank verse) |
| the absent "I" | as a fragment ("Empty mountains:"), an imperative, or a bare verb ("hear temple bell") | allusion, puns (道 road / Way) | they need a note, as Pound's Jewel Stairs note shows |
| a word struck again | English can repeat ("sorrow again sorry", "greener and greener") | the cut | English reaches for "like" (Pound's cloud) |

- **Deepening Cathay, not duplicating it.** The existing lens is the cool pole. These notes suggest three changes, for the maker to accept or refuse.
  - Give it a public-domain touchstone: "Blue mountains to the north of the walls, / White river winding about them;".
  - Add to its "how": no "I" unless the source has one; keep a doubled word doubled ("greener and greener"); never turn a juxtaposition into a "like".

  The three new lenses differ from Cathay by channel. Cold Mountain keeps the "I", the "you" and some rhyme. Parallel couplet is hotter in syntax and allows a word struck twice. Haiku cut is Cathay reduced to two centres and one gap.

### 5.2 Measurable features for the English ear (one-line algorithms)

Each would sit beside `syntaxOf`, `rhymeWords` and `repeats` in [`lib/english.mjs`](../lib/english.mjs) and [`lib/reading.mjs`](../lib/reading.mjs). They give advice, never scores.

- **Parallelism (two lines or two half-lines):** tag each word's part of speech (closed-class lists plus suffix rules), drop articles, align the two tag sequences by edit distance. Score = matched positions ÷ longer length, plus a bonus for each pair from a small list of pairs (sun/moon, mountain/river, push/knock) and for each word struck in both lines.
- **The cut:** a break (a dash, colon or ellipsis, or a line end) where no connective (like, as, and, so, because, when, which, while) bridges the two sides and the phrase before it has no finite verb. Count the cuts; a haiku cut is exactly one.
- **The absent I:** the share of lines with no first-person pronoun. Flag any line where the English adds I/me/we that the source (the gloss, or 我/吾/余 in Chinese) lacks: Weinberger's test.
- **Fragment and imperative:** lines with no finite verb (fragment), or opening on a bare verb with no subject (imperative): the English ways of keeping a subjectless line.
- **Negation and the sound in silence:** count *not / no / never / nothing / without / empty* per line, and words for sound (hear, knock, echo, splash). Report the line where the first sound follows a "not".
- **Chained repetition:** a word at or near the end of one line or half-line that returns at the start of the next (此語，此語; 寒山道，寒山路), added to the words already found struck again.
- **Poem-eye candidates:** in each line, the main verb and the rarest content word by a reference frequency list. For a revision, compare the candidates on stress, onset consonant, vowel, and whether the word makes a sound.
- **Reply-rhyme check:** given the source's end words, classify an answering poem as *cì yùn* (the same words in the same order), *yòng yùn* (the same words, any order), *hè yùn* (a perfect rhyme with each, by `rhymeWords`) or *hè shī* (none).
- **Scene and feeling:** per line, the ratio of concrete nouns to words of feeling or thought and to pronouns. The line where the ratio flips is the turn; compare it with the source's turn.
- **The pause:** whether a caesura (a comma, or a phrase boundary) falls before the last three stresses of a line, the English shadow of 2+3. Report 5-7-5 syllable counts as shape only, following Wilkinson's warning.

### 5.3 Exercises for the Practice room

1. **Tuīqiāo (one-word revision).** Take one line of a version and find its eye, usually the verb. Write five candidates in the margin, as Wang Anshi did ("reaches, passes, enters, fills… greens"), and say each aloud. Then decide Wang Fuzhi's way: go back to the gloss and ask which word the source's scene makes. Keep the rejected words, circled, in the record.
2. **Capping phrase.** Treat a caryā couplet as a koan. Answer it with one line of your own: an image or a plain sentence, never a doctrine word (the edition's "never in a line" list is the Zen rule that technical terms "stink"). The answer is judged, as Hori says the teacher judges a jakugo, by what it shows: grasp, nerve, pedantry or imagination.
3. **Reply on a caryā's rhyme words.** Take the end words of the caryā's couplets in the accurate English, or the Old Bengali rhyme words, and write a new poem on them: the same words in order (*cì yùn*), in any order (*yòng yùn*), or only rhyming with them (*hè yùn*). Wu Qiao's warning about tied hands comes with it.
4. **Nineteen ways.** Translate one couplet many ways: the gloss, a rhymed Fletcher, a Cathay, a broken-line version, one with an "I" and one without, a fragment, an imperative. Set them side by side with their boards, and write one Weinberger line under each.
5. **Write on a rock.** Write the poem where the place it names is, or name the place it is written in: a ferry song at a river crossing, a mountain poem on the way up. Leave it there (chalk, a note, a photograph) and sign it with the place, as Hanshan signed with his mountain.
6. **Link and shift (renga, for two).** With another maker, alternate lines after a caryā couplet. Each line links only to the one before it; the first must stand alone and name the season and the place.

### 5.4 Bridges to the Charyapada

- **Siddha songs beside Chan poems.** Both are Buddhist poems in a low register on purpose: Hanshan's vernacular (Mair ◐), and the caryās' "highest truth in the lowest terms" (Kværne, in [`charyapada-sound.md`](charyapada-sound.md), B8). Both are signed inside the song: "寒山出此語" beside "Lūyī says". Both mock the learned: Hanshan has his licentiate and "沙門不持戒" (monks who don't keep precepts) ✔ 卷806, and `lenses.json` already hears the siddhas' songs as "short sung poems of vision against the temple". Both were sung before they were books: Hanshan with the cowherds, the caryās under their rāga headings. Stonehouse's "do not sing these" marks the other pole ◐.
- **Haibun beside Munidatta.** The caryās survive inside prose: the Nepal manuscript is Munidatta's Sanskrit commentary, which gives each song and reads it phrase by phrase (*X iti Y*), and three songs survive only inside its Tibetan translation ([`prompts/texts/charyapada.md`](../prompts/texts/charyapada.md)). Haibun is also prose with poems, but its prose is the poet's own and does not explain the poem. They link and shift, like renga stanzas. The lesson for the woven commentary: sit beside the song like a haibun's prose, and never make the song an illustration of its gloss.
- **The koan's riddle beside twilight language.** Sandhyā-bhāṣā hides a teaching in an image; the jakugo answers a riddle with another image and forbids the doctrine word. Hori found that, once the point was seen, "every verse expressed 'Mu'" ✔ (p. 52): one riddle, many images. That is close to Hess's "charged pointer hub" (B8). The edition's rule, that readings go in notes and never in the line, is the capping phrase's own rule. Morton's "no symbolism" holds here too, with one difference: Munidatta supplies readings (several for one image, as Kværne shows), and the Zen master withholds them.
- **Two tests, not claims.** Ikkyū's poems to the blind singer Mori beside the songs to Ḍombī: Wikipedia's sources call his attitude "tantric", which needs checking before any comparison is printed ◐. And Wang Fuzhi's 現量, Buddhist direct perception, as a standard for revising a line: an Indian Buddhist term deciding a Chinese word choice ◐.
