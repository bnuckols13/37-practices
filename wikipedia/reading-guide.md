# Reading guide: Gyalse Tokme Zangpo (1295–1369)

This is a map of the sources, not text for the article. Each entry says where a fact is, gives the source's own words where helpful, and flags where sources disagree.

**How to use it (this matters):** Wikipedia's guideline [WP:NEWLLM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Writing_articles_with_large_language_models) bans putting AI-written text into articles. Its supplement [WP:LLMRESEARCH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Responsibly_using_large_language_models#Research) allows AI help with *finding* sources, but you must read them yourself and write your own sentences.

So:
1. Open the page cited.
2. Read the passage in full.
3. Close it.
4. Write what it says in your own words.
5. Never paste sentences from this file into Wikipedia.

The quotes below are the sources' words. They are there so you can find the passage quickly and check nothing has been garbled.

Every page reference below was checked against the source text on 26 September 2026.

## Section-by-section pointers

### Names

- **Birth name Könchok Zangpo.** Chien p. 55: "Tokmé Zangpo was named by his parents as Könchok Zangpo (Dkon mchog bzang po)." Also p. 66 n. 120.
- **Novice name Zangpo Pal, given in 1308.** Chien p. 60.
- **How he got the name "Tokmé" (the Tibetan name of Asaṅga).**
  - Chien pp. 65–66 retells it from the earliest biography. At Bodong É, at about age 20, during a memorial service, a scholar posed a question on the *Treasury of Abhidharma*; he answered it and won the debate.
  - Quote: "Even though [Master Tokmé] said that 'I am not a new Tokmé,' he was renowned and called as Tokmé from then on."
  - *Heart of Compassion* (bio section) gives a different version: "His teachers declared him to be a second Asanga … He was only nineteen."
- **"Gyalsé" (son of the Victorious One, meaning a bodhisattva).**
  - Chien pp. 72–73: after the lice episode he was "renowned everywhere with the honorific term 'Great Bodhisattva'", which "reads 'gyel sé chen po' (rgyal sras chen po) … This explains why Tokmé Zangpo is alternatively called Gyelsé Tokmé."
- **"Ngulchu Tokmé."** Chien p. 70 n. 136: "In most of his writings, Tokmé Zangpo specified that he wrote at Ngülchü Chödzong. Thus people alternatively call him Ngülchu Tokmé even nowadays."
- **Self-description in the colophon.** Pearcey translation: "the monk Tokme, a teacher of scripture and reasoning".
- **"Second Avalokiteśvara."** Chien p. 195: Palden Yeshe says the teaching passed "to Gyelsé Tokmé, who became well-known as the second Avalokiteśvara."
- **Name variants actually printed in reliable sources (useful for a footnote):**
  - "Gyalse Ngulchu Thogme" (*Heart of Compassion*)
  - "Ngulchu Gyalsas Togmed Zangpo" (Garfield p. 166)
  - "Tokme Zongpo" (McLeod's book title)
  - "Togme Sangpo" (Tegchok 1999 subtitle)
  - "Thokmé Sangpo" (Wisdom Publications)
  - "rGyal-sras Thogs-med-pa" (Roerich)
  - "Tokmé Zangpo" / "Gyelsé Tokmé" (Chien)

### Where the biography comes from (a short "Sources" subsection is good practice)

- **The three earliest biographies** are listed on Chien p. 12. They are *Guide to Your Devotion* by Zhönnu Gyeltsen Pelzangpo, *Ray of Altruism* by Pelgyi Rinchen, and *Drops of Ambrosia* by Palden (Penden) Yeshe.
  - Chien argues *Guide* "could be the earliest extant version".
  - P. 18 says its colophon year "could be as early as the Year of the Wood Tiger, 1374."
- **Later lives.** Chien p. 49: Künga Gyeltsen (1432–1506) and Yéshé Gyeltsen (1713–1793). The notes give the titles: a Kadam history, and the Lamrim lineage biographies.
- **Genre caution.**
  - Chien p. 3: namtar "contains both the biography and hagiography of a subject."
  - P. 55: birth miracles are "common motifs in a Tibetan saint's biography."
  - P. 57: asked about the childhood stories, he says "I, by myself, cannot remember those kinds of well-known things at that time."
  - **Use these to frame the miracle and compassion stories as what the biographies say (NPOV).**
- **English translation of Palden Yeshe.** The *Heart of Compassion* translators' introduction says "substantial excerpts of an inspiring biography of Ngulchu Thogme, by his disciple Palden Yeshe, have also been included". The translator was Matthieu Ricard.

### Early life

- **Born 1295 at Drakya, near Sakya.**
  - Chien p. 55 says "born in the Year of the Wood Female Sheep".
  - Chien p. 54 gives the birthplace as "Drakya (brag skya) near the Sakya monastery".
  - *Heart of Compassion*: "born in Phuljung, a few miles to the southwest of Sakya Monastery, in Tsang".
- **Parents and family.**
  - Chien p. 54: father Könchok Pel, mother Bumdrön.
  - Also p. 54: "ancestors were well-off local officials", and his grandfather's uncle was "a close attendant of Chögyel Pakpa".
- **Orphaned.** Chien p. 58: his mother died when he was three and his father when he was five. His grandmother cared for him until he was nine, then his uncle Rinchen Tashi supported his living and education until he became a novice at 14. *Heart of Compassion* agrees and adds that the uncle taught him to read and write.

### Education and ordination

- **Novice vows in 1308, at 14,** from Rinchen Pembarba (*rin chen dpal 'bar ba*) of Samling monastery (Chien p. 60).
- **Where he studied.**
  - Chien p. 60: mainly Samling, Bodong É and Sakya.
  - Chien p. 65: he studied the *Compendium of Abhidharma* (Abhidharmasamuccaya) "with five masters and finished his studies within five years", plus Prajñāpāramitā and "Kadam" teachings, and went on summer debating trips to Shalu and other monasteries.
- **Teachers and subjects** (Chien p. 67).
  - "around thirty names of masters". Subjects included Abhidharma, epistemology (tshad ma), Vasubandhu and Asaṅga, rituals for generating the awakening mind, and tantric empowerments.
  - Mahāmudrā and the Six Yogas of Nāropa, learned at Ngulchu, "even though he was not much affiliated with the Kagyü school".
  - Guru yoga from Jamyang Dönyö Gyaltsen, Sakya throne holder 1342–44.
  - The Seven-Point Mind Training from Sönam Drakpa (1273–1345).
- **More teachings** (Chien p. 68).
  - The Eight Verses from Lozangpa.
  - The *Bodhicaryāvatāra* "on two separate occasions".
  - "completed all his studies by age thirty-two".
  - "recognized as a spiritual guide at the Sakya monastery at age twenty-seven".
- **Full ordination.** Chien p. 86: "received full ordination at Bodong É Monastery … in 1325". Sources differ on his age:
  - Chien p. 68: 30.
  - *Heart of Compassion*: 29, "at the Monastery of É".
  - Tibetan age counting explains the difference; a footnote is a good place for this.

### Tara monastery and the Bodong É abbacy (handle with care: sources disagree)

- **Chien pp. 68–69.** At 32 he was an instructor (zur chen) at Bodong É. "The same year, he became the manager (den sa pa) at the Tara (Rta ra) monastery by the requests of monks there". He accepted only after they promised little administrative work.
- **BDRC P1830:** "1326–1333. seven year stint as abbot of rta ra."
- ***Heart of Compassion*:** "When Gyalse Thogme was thirty-two, he accepted the position of abbot at the Monastery of Tara, and remained there until he was forty-one."
- **Location unknown.** Chien p. 69 n. 132: "For the location of the Tara monastery, I did not find it during my trip in Tibet."
- **The Bodong É offer** (Chien p. 69).
  - "At age thirty-nine in 1333, when Tokmé Zangpo was teaching in Ngülchu in the fall, Jampéyang Dönyö Gyeltsen asked him to be the abbot of Bodong É Monastery. Instead of accepting this invitation, Tokmé Zangpo requested that his teacher Pang Lotsāwa Lodrö Tenpa (… 1276-1342), take this position."
  - The *Heart of Compassion* version: he "declined … He recommended the famous Khenpo Wanglo". Don't equate Wanglo with Pang Lotsāwa unless a source does.
  - Chien pp. 1 and 69–70: monks at Bodong É still told this story in 2013.
- **The conflicting claim.** Search-engine snippets of the Treasury of Lives entry say he was **abbot of Bodong É for about nine years, 1326–1335**.
  - The site blocks automated access, so open it in your browser (https://treasuryoflives.org/biographies/view/Gyelse-Tokme-Zangpo/3153) and read the wording yourself.
  - **Correction (26 Sep):** an earlier version of this guide said the Rubin Museum's catalogue repeats the claim. It doesn't. Its page (https://rubinmuseum.org/collection/c2002-4-1/) gives only the painting's title, date, medium, dimensions and object number. The Bodong É wording came from a search-engine summary. Don't cite the Rubin page for anything but the painting.
  - Neutral handling: follow the earliest biographies in the text, and add a footnote saying some reference works state the Bodong É abbacy, with a citation.
  - Do not call the other sources "wrong" in Wikipedia's voice.

### Reputation for compassion (hagiography: attribute it)

- **Examples of his generosity.** Chien p. 58: *Guide* "narrates Tokmé Zangpo's generosity to beggars, a crippled woman, dogs, bandits, etc."
- **The lice story** (Chien pp. 71–73).
  - He took a beggar home, gave him food and "a new fur cloak", took the beggar's lice-ridden clothes, and fell ill.
  - His words: "From beginningless time, many bodies again and again [in the cyclic existence] were meaningless. Now even though [I] die, it is meaningful."
  - "Around seventeen days" later the lice died.
  - Chien compares the story with Butön's account of Asaṅga and the worm-infested dog (pp. 72–73).
- **The robbers.** *Heart of Compassion*: robbers attacked him on his way back from receiving teachings from "the Dharma Lord Sönam Gyaltsen" at Sakya, and he dedicated prayers to them.

### Retreat at Ngulchu, travels, and patrons

- **Location.** Chien p. 67 n. 124: Ngulchu Chödzong is "40 miles northeast of Bodong É Monastery".
- **The retreat** (Chien p. 70).
  - He taught mainly at Bodong É and Ngulchu Chödzong until 1337, then retreated "after 1337 and rarely met with other people".
  - "This retreat seemed to last around twenty years."
  - He visited Pang Lotsāwa before the latter's death in 1342.
- **Travels** (Chien p. 70). "at age sixty-seven in 1361" he travelled to Samye, the Jokhang and elsewhere, and returned to Ngulchu "in the Year of the Rabbit that occurred in 1363".
- **Figures who invited him to teach** (Chien p. 71; not tied to particular years):
  - Butön (abbot of Shalu)
  - Tai Situ Changchub Gyaltsen ("who ruled Central Tibet after 1354")
  - Lama Dampa Sönam Gyaltsen ("fourteenth throne holder of the Sakya school")
  - Tai Situ Gelek Zangpo ("twelfth secular throne holder … of Tselpa Kagyü order")
- **Blue Annals** (Roerich pp. 584–585). Two Phagmodru hierarchs "heard" doctrines from him. The p. 585 passage reads: "He heard many doctrines from rGyal-sras Thogs-med-pa".

### Death

- **Blue Annals p. 310:** "In the next Earth-Female-Hen year (sa-mo-bya — 1369 A.D.) rGyal-sras Thogs-med-pa died." The OCR on archive.org garbles "1369"; the index confirms p. 310.
- **Chien p. 202:** "passed away in 1369 at remote Ngülchü Chödzong".
- ***Heart of Compassion*:**
  - "From that morning until the next evening he remained seated in the lotus posture … he departed into bliss."
  - Cremation took place nine days later, and his disciples placed his relics "in precious reliquaries and statues".
- **Chien p. 203:** "Tokmé Zangpo does not have a reincarnation".

### Works

- **Collected works, four editions** (Chien pp. 80–81): Thimphu 1975, the Drepung manuscript, the 18th-century Degé xylograph, and Labrang (Lanzhou, 2011).
  - All contain the "113 Texts".
  - Degé and Labrang add a 114th, *Great Hearing Transmission of Mind Training*. P. 194: its first section is by Palden Yeshe and its second by Tokmé Zangpo.
- **Commentaries.**
  - On the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*: *Ocean of Good Explanation* (p. 189 n. 372).
  - On the *Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra*: *Precious Garland* (p. 189 n. 371).
  - On the *Ratnagotravibhāga* / Uttaratantra (p. 192 n. 379).
  - A lost commentary on the *Abhidharmasamuccaya*: "so far I have not found it" (p. 192 n. 377).
- **English translation.** His *Bodhicaryāvatāra* commentary appeared in English in 2026 in *The Bodhisattva Ideal: Selected Key Texts* (Library of Tibetan Classics vol. 11, Wisdom). **Check the table of contents and the translator credit in the book itself before citing.**
- **The editors' role.** Chien p. 203: "Through editing or arranging Tokmé Zangpo's writings, Penden Yéshé and the Fifth Dalai Lama associated themselves with Tokmé Zangpo's Lojong transmission."

### Mind-training (lojong) writings

- **Two commentaries on the Seven-Point Mind Training** (Chien p. 191).
- **Text 9, "Lineage of Mahāyāna Lojong Masters"** (Chien pp. 186–188).
  - The list runs from the Buddha, Maitreya, Asaṅga and Vasubandhu, through Atiśa and the Kadampas, to Sönam Drakpa.
  - Note: Chien says "twenty-three masters" on p. 186 but numbers Sönam Drakpa as the 24th on p. 187. Avoid giving a count.
- **Chien's argument** (abstract, p. ii; pp. 186, 191–192).
  - The lineage "was shaped by his involvement with Kadam masters and texts".
  - Its "unique element … is the inclusion of the five masters who are associated with Asaṅga".
  - P. 186: "While Tokmé Zangpo was affiliated with the Sakya school, as stated in his biographies, his text on Lojong transmission does not include any Sakya master."

### *The Thirty-Seven Practices of Bodhisattvas*

- **Where it sits and what it was called.**
  - Chien p. 154: it is text 29 in the Degé edition.
  - N. 301: "The word 'Thirty-Seven' is not given in the title by Tokmé Zangpo. In Guide to Your Devotion, the name of the text is 'rgyal sras lag len.'"
- **The composition story** (Chien p. 154, from *Guide* pp. 11–12). People suggested he give tantric empowerments to earn a living; he answered "If I would engage in practice, I would prefer to do something like this", and composed the text. The wind blew the pages away while he was editing in a ravine, and he rewrote it.
- **Where it was written.** Colophon (Pearcey): "composed in Jewel Cave (Rinchen Puk) in Ngulchu". *Heart of Compassion* also links the composition to a time of material hardship.
- **Structure** (count it yourself in Pearcey's translation). It has:
  - an opening homage and a statement of purpose;
  - 37 four-line verses, each containing "The practice of all the bodhisattvas";
  - four closing verses, the last a dedication;
  - a colophon.
- **What scholars say about it.**
  - Garfield p. 166 says the text "covers the basic Bodhisattva practices, with a special emphasis on the practices associated with morality and altruism". He adds that it is "remarkable in its emphasis on practices … that are appropriate to lay practictioners [sic] as well", and valuable "in virtue of the unusual degree to which it explains and justifies each of the practices".
  - *Heart of Compassion* translators: the text "is considered to be a distillation of … The Way of the Bodhisattva (Bodhicharyavatara), and has been the subject of many teachings and commentaries by the great masters of all traditions of Tibetan Buddhism".
- **Reception** (Chien pp. 1–2).
  - It "is preached to the public in Asia and America and has been translated into various languages".
  - Teachers who taught it include Dilgo Khyentse, Geshe Jampa Tegchok, the 14th Dalai Lama and the 17th Karmapa.
  - P. 154: it "has attracted the most discussion from scholars and lay people alike".
- **History of commentary** (Chien p. 155 and n. 305).
  - "no recorded commentary was composed between the fifteenth and the eighteenth century".
  - TBRC (now BDRC) lists eight commentaries from the 19th to the early 21st centuries, including Ngawang Tendzin Norbu (1867–1940).
- **Garfield p. 166:** the Dalai Lama gave "three days of oral teachings" on it at Bodh Gaya in 1974.
- **Minyak Kunzang Sönam (Chökyi Drakpa), "a learned disciple of Patrul Rinpoche":** his commentary is named in the *Heart of Compassion* translators' introduction, and translated as *Uniting Wisdom and Compassion* (Wisdom 2004).

### Legacy

- **Claimed by several traditions.**
  - Sakya: Chien p. 186.
  - Kadam history by Künga Gyaltsen: Chien p. 49 n. 91.
  - Lamrim lineage biographies by the Gelug scholar Yeshe Gyaltsen: Chien p. 49 n. 92.
  - The Fifth Dalai Lama's lojong lineage lists: Chien p. 203.
- **How he is remembered.** Chien p. 203: "He is remembered because he is included in the Lojong transmission by later scholars, such as the Fifth Dalai Lama."
- **Students** (BDRC P1830 "teacher of"). They include Rendawa Zhönnu Lodrö (1349–1412), Palden Yeshe, Buddhaśrī (1339–1420), Nyungne Lama Sönam Zangpo (1341–1433) and Nugo Gyalwa Rinchen (1328–1386).
  - Ignore BDRC's link to a man born in 1888. That is a database error.
- **Blue Annals p. 1024:** he appears in an Amoghapāśa (Avalokiteśvara) transmission lineage in Book XIV, "The Cycle of the Mahākaruṇika".
- **The site today.** Chien p. 1: Ngulchu Chödzong is "located in the Xietongmen County of Shigatse". There, "a shrine of Tokmé Zangpo sits across from various meditation caves", and local people "prostrated in front of Tokmé Zangpo's statue" (August 2013).
- **The painting.** The Rubin Museum holds *Gyelse Tokme Zangpo (1295–1369) and scenes from his life*: Tibet, 18th century, pigments on cloth, object C2002.4.1.

## Common errors to avoid (you will meet these online)

| Claim you'll see | What's actually supported |
|---|---|
| Dates **1297–1371** (Lotsawa House, some Sakya sites) or **1285** (a 1989 article) | **1295–1369**: Chien, the Blue Annals, BDRC, the Library of Congress |
| "Abbot of Bodong É for nine years" | Earliest biographies: head of **Tara**; he **declined** Bodong É (see above) |
| "Ngulchu means Silver River" | No reliable source for this. As a common noun *dngul chu* means mercury. Just call it a place name. |
| "A Nyingma master" | No scholarly support. Nyingma masters *teach his text*; that is different. |
| "Emanation of Avalokiteśvara" as fact | Only in devotional sources. Palden Yeshe's epithet "second Avalokiteśvara" can be quoted, with attribution. |
| Butön was his teacher / his student | Sources disagree. Say only that Butön invited him (Chien p. 71). |
| "Studied Dzogchen with …", "student of Dolpopa", "met Longchenpa" | No reliable source found. Leave them out. |
| "The 37 Practices is 12th century" (en.wiki *Bodhicitta* article) | 14th century. This makes a good first practice edit; see README. |
| Linking "Tsering Wangchuk" | The en.wiki article of that name is a different person (a politician), not the scholar of the Uttaratantra. |
